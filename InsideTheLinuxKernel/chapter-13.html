The previous chapter showed how the Virtual Filesystem turns a read() or write() into a call on a filesystem's method table. But underneath the filesystem lies real hardware—disks, serial ports, network adapters, GPUs, sensors—and something has to drive it. That something is the device driver, and the machinery the kernel provides to organize thousands of drivers across wildly different buses and processors is the subject of this chapter.

The problem device drivers solve is variety. A modern system has devices on PCIe, USB, I2C, SPI, and on-chip buses; it may be an x86_64 server that discovers its hardware through ACPI, or an ARM64 board that is handed a device tree describing peripherals that announce themselves in no other way. The kernel hides all of this behind a single device model, described in detail later in this chapter, which gives every piece of hardware one representation the rest of the kernel can work with regardless of how it was found. On top of that model sit the mechanisms a driver actually uses—memory-mapped registers, interrupts, and DMA, plus, on a system-on-chip, the clocks, supply rails, pins, and firmware images a device needs before it will respond at all—and the device file abstraction that lets a user program reach a driver through an ordinary path in /dev. That last step is the common case rather than the universal one: network interfaces have no device file and are reached through sockets and netlink instead, but they sit on the same device model as everything else.

This chapter follows Linux 7.0.12 rather than the Linux 2.6/i386 implementation. The shift is large: the 2.6-era picture of port I/O and a nascent kobject model has been replaced by a mature unified device model, firmware-driven enumeration (device tree and ACPI), the generic irq_domain and threaded-interrupt framework, and a portable DMA-mapping API that hides IOMMUs and bounce buffers. We use x86_64 and ARM64 as the two reference architectures throughout, because the contrast between a discoverable, ACPI-described PC and a device-tree-described ARM64 SoC is exactly what the modern driver model was built to span.

One further change is more recent than any of those: the language. Linux 7.0.12 ships safe Rust abstractions over this same driver model under rust/kernel/—device.rs, driver.rs, devres.rs, platform.rs, pci.rs, irq/, dma.rs—together with a small set of in-tree Rust drivers that use them. Those abstractions wrap the very C structures this chapter describes, so the model presented here is the model a Rust driver joins rather than an alternative to it; Chapter 26 covers the language side. Everything below is C, because that is what the driver core itself is written in.

The Role of I/O and Device Drivers

A CPU by itself computes; it becomes a computer only when it can move data to and from the outside world. That movement happens over buses—the paths that connect the processor and RAM to disks, network adapters, displays, keyboards, and the hundreds of smaller controllers on a modern board. The word bus is inherited from an era when it was literal: ISA and conventional PCI really were shared electrical paths that every device on them listened to. Almost nothing works that way now—PCIe and USB are point-to-point serial links, and an SoC's peripherals hang off an on-chip fabric—but the kernel keeps the vocabulary because what matters to software is unchanged: a bus is a set of addressable devices, a way of discovering them, and a route their transactions travel. The kernel's job is to let a single body of code, the device driver, operate a specific piece of hardware while presenting it to the rest of the system through a uniform interface. Everything in this chapter is in service of that one idea: turning the endless variety of real hardware into a small number of abstractions the rest of the kernel can rely on.

It helps to picture the whole path a request travels. When a program calls read() on /dev/ttyS0, the call descends through the VFS and the relevant kernel subsystem into a specific device driver; the driver in turn reaches the hardware through just three low-level mechanisms—it reads and writes the controller's registers, it receives interrupts when the device has news, and it moves bulk data with DMA. Underneath those mechanisms sit the physical realities: a bus that carries the accesses, a controller that implements the device's protocol, and the device itself. The proportions of those three mechanisms have shifted since Linux 2.6. Driving a UART still means writing a register per byte, but a modern NVMe SSD or network adapter is driven almost entirely out of host memory: the driver builds descriptors in a DMA-mapped ring, writes one doorbell register to tell the device the ring has moved, and the device DMAs the data and raises an interrupt when it is done. The mechanisms are the same three; the traffic has moved from registers to DMA. Buses, Controllers, and Interfaces

Between the CPU and a physical device sit two layers. A bus carries data between the processor complex and a set of attached devices; examples are PCI Express (PCIe), USB, I2C, SPI, and the on-chip buses that connect the blocks of a system-on-chip. A controller (or interface) is the hardware block that actually speaks the device's protocol and exposes a set of registers—small addressable words that the driver reads and writes to command the device and observe its state. The driver never touches the wires; it reads and writes these registers, and the controller translates those accesses into electrical activity on the bus.

Buses are rarely flat. A real system is a tree of buses joined by bridges: on a PC, a root complex fans PCIe out to slots and to bridges that lead to USB and SATA controllers, while an SoC has a central fabric with bridges down to slower I2C and SPI buses. Each bridge forwards a sub-range of addresses to the devices behind it, which is why the resource tree in the next section is hierarchical: its shape mirrors the bus topology. A device's position in that tree also determines how it is reached—an I2C sensor three bridges deep is addressed very differently from a PCIe NIC on the root complex, though the driver model hides most of that difference.

Not every bus has wires. In the driver model a bus is a piece of software—a namespace of devices plus a rule for matching them to drivers—and several of the buses that carry the most code in a modern kernel exist nowhere on the board. The platform bus (drivers/base/platform.c) is the home of devices that firmware described rather than a bus enumerated, which on an SoC is most of them. virtio (drivers/virtio/virtio.c) is a bus of paravirtualised devices whose "hardware" is a hypervisor. The auxiliary bus (drivers/base/auxiliary.c) exists so that one physical device can be split into several driver-model devices: a multi-function NIC registers auxiliary devices for its Ethernet, RDMA and subfunction personalities (drivers/net/ethernet/mellanox/mlx5/core/sf/dev/), each bound by its own driver, rather than linking every feature into one enormous module. And the faux bus (drivers/base/faux.c, added late in the 6.x series) is deliberately almost empty—its match function returns 1 unconditionally—as a home for pseudo-devices that want a struct device only in order to own a sysfs directory, a job previously done by abusing platform devices. A bus, in other words, is whatever the kernel needs to treat as one.

This two-layer split—bus plus controller—is why the kernel can reuse so much code, and it is worth seeing as a narrow waist. Above the waist, the rest of the kernel deals only in generic objects (a device, a driver, a file); below it, each controller is idiosyncratic. A driver for a specific UART chip cares only about that chip's registers, while the code that finds the UART, assigns it an address, and routes its interrupt is shared machinery belonging to the bus and the driver core. The driver announces which devices it can handle; the core matches it to hardware and hands it the resources—register windows, interrupt numbers, DMA channels—that the device was assigned. The bulk of this chapter is about those shared mechanisms and the three things a driver ultimately does with a device, the three channels of Figure 13-1: read and write its registers, receive its interrupts, and move bulk data with DMA.

Port I/O versus Memory-Mapped I/O

There are two historical ways for a CPU to reach a device register. The first is a separate I/O address space accessed with dedicated instructions—on x86 these are the in and out instructions, and the addresses they use are called I/O ports. This space is small (64 KB on x86), cannot be used for large register windows, and exists on essentially no architecture but x86. The second is memory-mapped I/O (MMIO), in which device registers appear at addresses in the ordinary physical-memory space and are reached with the same load and store instructions used for RAM. MMIO is the dominant model on all modern hardware: it is uniform across architectures, supports arbitrarily large register windows (a GPU exposes megabytes of them), and needs no special instructions. On PCIe, a device advertises its MMIO windows through base address registers (BARs) in configuration space, and the kernel programs those windows into the address map at boot.

The crucial subtlety is that MMIO registers are not memory, even though they are reached with load and store instructions. A read can have side effects—reading a status register may clear it, or pop a byte from a receive FIFO—and writes must reach the device in program order rather than being reordered, cached, or write-combined the way the CPU and compiler are free to treat plain RAM. Two writes that a programmer intends as "set the address, then trigger the transfer" must not be reordered, or the device sees a transfer to the wrong address. For that reason Linux never lets a driver dereference an MMIO pointer directly. It provides accessor functions (readl(), writel(), and relatives) that mark the access as volatile and insert the ordering barriers and byte-swapping the architecture requires—a point developed in the next section.

Two things about port I/O have changed since Linux 2.6, when it was a fact of life. The first is that it is now optional at build time. The accessors exist only if CONFIG_HAS_IOPORT is set (lib/Kconfig); x86 and ARM64 both select it, but many architectures and configurations do not, and there the generic header supplies stubs that refuse to compile— __compiletime_error("inb()) requires CONFIG_HAS_IOPORT") (include/asm-generic/io.h). A driver that reaches for a port must say depends on HAS_IOPORT in its Kconfig entry. The second change is that the cost has become conspicuous. A port access is a small, non-posted, strictly serialising transaction: the processor waits for the device to answer, and unlike an MMIO store it can be neither posted, nor write-combined, nor batched. Under confidential computing it is worse—inside an Intel TDX guest every in and out raises a virtualisation exception that is bounced to the hypervisor by handle_io() (arch/x86/coco/tdx/tdx.c), and even the string forms are unrolled into one trap per word, because the host cannot be allowed to walk guest memory (CC_ATTR_GUEST_UNROLL_STRING_IO, arch/x86/include/asm/io.h). Ports survive because old hardware does; nothing new is designed around them. Even PCI configuration space, historically reached through the 0xCF8/0xCFC port pair (arch/x86/pci/direct.c), is normally reached today through the memory-mapped ECAM region that ACPI describes (arch/x86/pci/mmconfig-shared.c), with the port pair kept for early boot and for machines whose firmware does not describe ECAM.

Because the same device may appear behind a port on one machine and an MMIO window on another, the kernel also provides one set of accessors that works with either. pci_iomap() (drivers/pci/iomap.c) maps a BAR and returns a cookie without the driver caring whether it was a memory BAR or an I/O BAR, and ioread32() / iowrite32() (lib/iomap.c) dispatch on that cookie: port addresses are encoded by biasing them into a reserved low range (PIO_OFFSET, PIO_MASK, PIO_RESERVED), so each accessor compares the cookie against that range and calls either inl() or readl()—and warns loudly through bad_io_access() if the value fits neither. The price is a branch per access; the reward is a single body of driver code for two address spaces.

x86_64 and ARM64 Contrasts

The two reference architectures of this book differ in ways that shape driver code. On x86_64, the separate I/O-port space is a real CPU feature: inb()/outb() compile to genuine in/out instructions (the inline assembly is in arch/x86/include/asm/shared/io.h, wrapped by arch/x86/include/asm/io.h), the 64 KB limit mentioned above is the constant IO_SPACE_LIMIT, and legacy PC peripherals—the interrupt controller, timers, the old serial and keyboard controllers—still live at fixed port addresses. Hardware is overwhelmingly self-describing: firmware hands the kernel ACPI tables and PCIe configuration space, from which the kernel discovers what exists and where.

On ARM64, there is no separate I/O-port instruction at all. Everything is memory-mapped; what portable code calls a "port" is emulated as a window into PCI I/O space at PCI_IOBASE (arch/arm64/include/asm/io.h), which is a fixed kernel virtual range carved out of the memory map—PCI_IO_START is VMEMMAP_END + 8 MB and the window is 16 MB long (arch/arm64/include/asm/memory.h), so IO_SPACE_LIMIT here is larger than x86's genuine port space rather than smaller. ARM64 defines arch_has_dev_port() as unconditionally 1, which is worth reading correctly: it does not claim the CPU has ports, only that the emulation is always available, because inb() is an ordinary load from that window. Just as important, most on-SoC peripherals cannot be discovered by probing at all: they sit at fixed physical addresses with no enumeration protocol. ARM64 therefore relies on a device tree—a firmware-provided data structure that names each peripheral and lists its register range, interrupt, clocks, and DMA channels. The unified device model in the sections that follow is precisely what lets one driver framework serve both the discoverable, ACPI-described PC and the statically-described ARM64 SoC.

One deeper difference shapes how carefully a driver must be written: memory ordering. x86_64 has a strongly-ordered memory model in which stores mostly stay in program order, so a sloppy driver may accidentally work there. ARM64 is weakly ordered—the CPU may freely reorder independent loads and stores—so the ordering barriers baked into readl()/writel() are not a nicety but a correctness requirement. This is the practical reason the accessor discipline from the previous subsection matters: code that dereferences an MMIO pointer directly, or uses the __raw accessors, can pass every test on an x86_64 workstation and then corrupt data the first time it runs on an ARM64 board. Writing to the accessor contract, and letting the device model hand the driver its resources, is what makes a single driver correct on both.

The mechanism behind that difference is worth seeing, because it explains the doorbell pattern described at the start of this chapter. On ARM64, ioremap() maps registers with the Device-nGnRE attribute (PROT_DEVICE_nGnRE, arch/arm64/include/asm/io.h): non-gathering, non-reordering, early-write-acknowledge. That keeps device accesses ordered with respect to one another, but it says nothing about their order relative to ordinary memory—and a DMA descriptor the driver has just filled in is ordinary memory. This is why writel() on ARM64 places a dma_wmb() before the store (the architecture's __io_bw() hook) and readl() places a dma_rmb() after the load, followed by a synthetic control dependency that stops a subsequent udelay() from being hoisted above the read. A doorbell write is correct only because the barrier inside writel() guarantees the descriptors reached memory first. On x86_64 the same accessors reduce to a plain load or store with a compiler barrier, because the TSO memory model and the uncached mapping already supply what those barriers ask for—which is precisely why omitting them is invisible on a PC.

A third architectural difference never appears in the register accessors at all, yet shapes every driver that moves bulk data: cache coherence with DMA. On x86_64, devices snoop the CPU caches, and the architecture does not select CONFIG_ARCH_HAS_SYNC_DMA_FOR_DEVICE; with that option off, dev_is_dma_coherent() is a compile-time true and the dma_sync_* calls compile away to nothing (include/linux/dma-map-ops.h). ARM64 does select it, so coherence becomes a per-device property: dev->dma_coherent is set during enumeration by arch_setup_dma_ops() (arch/arm64/mm/dma-mapping.c) from the device tree's dma-coherent property (drivers/of/device.c) or from ACPI's _CCA attribute (drivers/acpi/scan.c), and for a device that lacks it every map and unmap becomes real cache maintenance—clean the lines before the device reads, invalidate them after it writes. It is also why ARM64 sets ARCH_DMA_MINALIGN to 128 bytes (arch/arm64/include/asm/cache.h): a streaming buffer must not share a cache line with unrelated data, or invalidating that line after a transfer would destroy a neighbour's. The identical dma_map_single() call is therefore free on one architecture and a cache operation on the other, which is the real reason the DMA-mapping API of the later section insists on being told about every ownership transfer even when the calls appear to cost nothing. Interrupt delivery differs in yet another way—x86_64's local and I/O APICs versus ARM64's GIC and its ITS—but there the generic irq_domain layer hides almost all of the difference, as the section on interrupt handling shows.

I/O Ports and I/O Memory

Reaching a device's registers is the most basic thing a driver does, and it follows a fixed four-step lifecycle: claim the address range so no one else uses it, map it into kernel virtual space (for memory-mapped devices), access it through the kernel's accessor functions, and release it when the driver is unbound. Linux tracks every I/O port range and every physical-memory range in one hierarchical structure, the resource tree, and layers the port and MMIO accessors on top of it. This section walks through the tree—which turns out to serve as an access-control list as well as a registry—and both accessor families, together with the managed helpers and higher-level abstractions that most drivers reach for instead.

The Resource Tree

Every assignable address range on the system—each block of I/O ports and each region of physical memory—is described by a struct resource in include/linux/ioport.h. Resources form a tree: a root describes an entire address space, and children carve out sub-ranges, so a PCIe bridge's memory window can contain the windows of the devices behind it. The two roots are ioport_resource (the I/O-port space) and iomem_resource (physical memory), and they are exactly what a developer sees when reading /proc/ioports and /proc/iomem—a flattened dump of the tree, with each range labeled by the driver that claimed it. Reading those files is often the first debugging step when a device is not working: if the expected range is missing or owned by the wrong driver, the problem is enumeration or a conflict, not the driver's register logic.

With one caveat about reading them. The addresses in those files are useful to an attacker trying to defeat KASLR, so r_show() prints 00000000-00000000 for every range unless the reader holds CAP_SYS_ADMIN in the initial user namespace (kernel/resource.c). An ordinary user greps /proc/iomem and sees the tree's shape and the owners' names with every address zeroed—which looks far more like a broken kernel than like a redaction, and has confused more than one bug report. 

struct resource {
        resource_size_t start;
        resource_size_t end;
        const char *name;
        unsigned long flags;            /* IORESOURCE_* */
        unsigned long desc;
        struct resource *parent, *sibling, *child;   /* tree links */
};

A driver claims a range before using it, which both prevents two drivers from touching the same registers and records the owner's name in /proc/iomem. For ports the call is request_region(); for memory it is request_mem_region(). Both are thin wrappers over __request_region(), which inserts a new child under the appropriate root and fails if it would overlap an existing busy range (kernel/resource.c). That failure is how a driver discovers a conflict: the call returns NULL, and the driver aborts its probe with -EBUSY rather than scribbling on hardware another driver owns. The matching release_region()/release_mem_region() free the range; the flags field classifies the resource, as Table 13-1 lists. 

#define request_region(start,n,name)      __request_region(&ioport_resource, (start), (n), (name), 0)
#define request_mem_region(start,n,name)  __request_region(&iomem_resource, (start), (n), (name), 0)

Two points matter for how a developer uses the tree. First, claiming a range is separate from mapping it: request_mem_region() only reserves the range in the tree, and the driver must still call ioremap() to obtain a usable pointer (the next subsections combine the two). Second, a modern driver almost never hard-codes an address. The range comes from firmware—a device-tree reg property or an ACPI resource—and is handed to the driver as a struct resource via platform_get_resource(). So the typical probe reads its resource from the platform device, claims it, and maps it, and the same driver works on any board whose firmware describes a compatible device at whatever address that board happens to use.

The tree does a second job that is easy to overlook: it is also the access list that keeps userspace out of registers a driver owns. When a process maps /dev/mem, the kernel asks iomem_is_exclusive() whether the target page falls inside a range that may not be shared (kernel/resource.c). A range qualifies if its owner set IORESOURCE_EXCLUSIVE, or—when CONFIG_IO_STRICT_DEVMEM is enabled—if it is merely busy; ranges holding system RAM are exclusive unconditionally, because the driver that owns them adds and removes memory underneath. Above that sits CONFIG_STRICT_DEVMEM, on by default on x86, ARM64, PowerPC and s390, which keeps /dev/mem away from kernel RAM entirely, and the iomem=relaxed boot parameter, which switches the stricter check off for people deliberately poking at hardware. The consequence for a driver author is a pleasant one: claiming a range is not merely etiquette between drivers, it is what stops even a root process from reaching the same registers behind the driver's back.

Port I/O Accessors

On architectures with a separate I/O space, a driver reads and writes ports with the in/out family: inb(), inw(), inl() read one, two, or four bytes, and outb(), outw(), outl() write them. Each takes a port number, so the usage pattern is to claim the port range with request_region(), then read and write status and data registers by port number, then release_region() on cleanup. On x86_64 these expand to the actual in/out machine instructions (arch/x86/include/asm/io.h); on ARM64 there is no such instruction, and the same names resolve to accesses of a memory window representing PCI I/O space, so portable code compiles unchanged while doing MMIO underneath. 

if (!request_region(base, 8, "mydev"))
        return -EBUSY;                   /* someone else owns these ports */

outb(CMD_RESET, base + CMD_REG);         /* write a command byte    */
while (!(inb(base + STATUS_REG) & READY)) /* poll a status byte      */
        cpu_relax();
val = inl(base + DATA_REG);              /* read a 32-bit data word */
...
release_region(base, 8);

For moving a block of data through a single FIFO port there are string variants—insb()/outsb() and their word and long forms—which repeat the access a given number of times without advancing the port address. The ioread8()/iowrite8() family (and ioread32() and so on) is a newer, address-space-agnostic alternative that works on a cookie returned by a mapping function, letting one code path serve both ports and MMIO. Even so, port I/O is a shrinking legacy—confined mostly to old PC peripherals—so new devices on both architectures are almost always memory-mapped instead, which is the subject of the next subsection.

Two details of that example deserve correcting before it is copied. The first is the poll loop: if the device never sets READY—because it is wedged, absent, or unpowered—the loop spins forever inside a probe, holding whatever locks the caller held. The kernel's answer is include/linux/iopoll.h, which wraps exactly this pattern with a deadline. read_poll_timeout() takes any accessor and its arguments, so it works with inb as readily as with a function call; readl_poll_timeout() specialises it for MMIO; and the _atomic variants busy-wait with udelay() for contexts that must not sleep. Each returns -ETIMEDOUT when the condition never comes true, which a probe can report to the driver core instead of hanging boot. The second detail is where the cookie for ioread32() comes from in the port case: ioport_map() (lib/iomap.c, available when CONFIG_HAS_IOPORT_MAP is set) biases a port number into the reserved low range that the accessors recognise, and pci_iomap() calls either it or ioremap() depending on the kind of BAR. Finally, the inb_p()/outb_p() spellings occasionally seen in old drivers are the same accesses followed by slow_down_io(), a deliberate pause for ISA peripherals too slow to keep up with back-to-back accesses—a good marker for code that predates every device a new driver will meet.

ioremap and MMIO Accessors

A memory-mapped device exposes its registers at a physical address, but the kernel runs in virtual memory, so the driver must first map that physical range into kernel virtual space with ioremap(). The returned void __iomem * is deliberately opaque: it is not a normal pointer and must never be dereferenced directly. The mapping is created uncached (writes are not held in the CPU write-back cache) so that register accesses reach the device, and it is released with iounmap().

ioremap() is no longer per-architecture boilerplate. Most architectures now select CONFIG_GENERIC_IOREMAP and inherit generic_ioremap_prot(), supplying only the page protection they want for device memory; ARM64 keeps a thin version of its own, in which ioremap() is __ioremap_prot() with PROT_DEVICE_nGnRE (arch/arm64/include/asm/io.h). What matters to a driver is that the mapping's memory attribute is a choice, and each choice has its own entry point. ioremap_wc() asks for write-combining—normal non-cacheable memory on ARM64—so that consecutive stores into a framebuffer may be merged into bursts. ioremap_np() asks for the strictest attribute, non-posted device memory (Device-nGnRnE), in which a write is not complete until the device has taken it; some SoCs require it for correctness rather than as a tuning knob. ioremap_uc() and ioremap_cache() cover rarer cases. The fallback behaviour differs in a way worth knowing: where an architecture does not implement ioremap_wc() or ioremap_wt() the generic header aliases them to plain ioremap(), so using them is never a portability error—but unimplemented ioremap_np() and ioremap_uc() return NULL (include/asm-generic/io.h), so a driver that wants either must carry its own fallback. 

void __iomem *regs = ioremap(phys_addr, size);
u32 status = readl(regs + STATUS_REG);   /* ordered, little-endian load  */
writel(cmd, regs + CTRL_REG);            /* ordered, little-endian store */
iounmap(regs);

Access always goes through the typed accessors: readb()/readw()/ readl()/readq() and writeb()/writew()/writel()/writeq() (include/asm-generic/io.h). These do two things a raw pointer dereference cannot. First, they insert ordering barriers so that a sequence of register writes actually reaches the device in the order written—essential on weakly-ordered ARM64, where the CPU may otherwise reorder stores. Second, they perform fixed little-endian conversion, so the same driver works on a big-endian CPU. Table 13-2 summarizes the accessor families a driver chooses among.

memcpy_toio, memcpy_fromio, memset_io Bulk copies to/from an MMIO region (e.g. loading a framebuffer or a firmware image)
__raw_readl, __raw_writel Native-endian, no barriers — almost never what a portable driver wants

A subtlety developers hit early is the _relaxed variants. The default readl()/writel() place a barrier around every access, which is correct but costly in a tight loop that, say, drains a receive FIFO. The readl_relaxed()/writel_relaxed() forms omit the barrier; they are safe only when the driver adds its own dma_rmb()/dma_wmb() or a trailing plain accessor to establish the one ordering point that actually matters. Getting this wrong is a classic ARM64-only bug—code that works on strongly-ordered x86_64 and fails on ARM64—so the relaxed forms should be used deliberately, not by default. A common, safe idiom is a small read-modify-write helper built on the ordered accessors: 

/* set bits in a control register, ordered */
static void reg_setbits(void __iomem *regs, u32 off, u32 bits)
{
        u32 v = readl(regs + off);
        writel(v | bits, regs + off);
}

In practice a modern driver rarely calls ioremap() and request_mem_region() by hand, because the two steps—claim and map—are almost always done together and undone together. The driver core offers a managed helper that folds both, plus automatic cleanup on unbind: devm_platform_ioremap_resource() takes a platform device and a resource index, looks up the device's IORESOURCE_MEM range (from device tree or ACPI), claims it, maps it, and returns a ready-to-use void __iomem * (drivers/base/platform.c). Because the mapping is released automatically when the device unbinds, the driver needs no iounmap() in its error paths or remove function—eliminating a large class of leak-on-error bugs. This managed pattern, revisited with the device model, is why the worked example at the end of the chapter maps its registers in a single line.

That helper hides two decisions worth surfacing. It calls devm_ioremap_resource() (lib/devres.c), which inspects the resource it was handed and quietly upgrades the mapping to the non-posted variant when the range carries IORESOURCE_MEM_NONPOSTED—a flag the device-tree address parser sets for any node whose parent declares nonposted-mmio (drivers/of/address.c). A driver written against the managed API therefore gets the attribute its board needs without knowing that such a thing exists. The other decision is how failure is reported, and here the managed and unmanaged forms disagree: ioremap() returns NULL, whereas the devm_ functions return an encoded error built with IOMEM_ERR_PTR() (include/linux/err.h), so a probe must test them with IS_ERR() and can return the value unchanged. Checking a devm_ result against NULL yields a pointer that is not null, not a mapping, and faulted on the first readl(). PCI drivers have the same convenience under different names: after pcim_enable_device(), pcim_iomap_region() claims and maps a single BAR with automatic release (drivers/pci/devres.c), and the older mask-based pcim_iomap_regions() is explicitly marked deprecated in 7.0.12.

Two tools make the accessor discipline enforceable rather than merely advisable. The __iomem annotation is not decoration: under sparse it expands to __attribute__((noderef, address_space(__iomem))) (include/linux/compiler_types.h), so make C=1 reports any dereference of an I/O pointer, and any confusion between I/O and kernel pointers, as an error; the only way to silence it is an explicit __force cast, which a reviewer can grep for. And when hardware misbehaves, CONFIG_TRACE_MMIO_ACCESS turns every generic readl()/writel() into a tracepoint—rwmmio_read, rwmmio_write, and a post-read event carrying the value returned—so ftrace can record a driver's entire register-level conversation with its device. It depends on ARCH_HAVE_TRACE_MMIO_ACCESS, which ARM64 and s390 select and x86 does not, because x86 defines its own readl() in inline assembly rather than using the instrumented generic one.

Finally, many drivers no longer call these accessors directly at all. regmap (drivers/base/regmap/) is a register-access layer that sits above MMIO, I2C, SPI and several other transports: a driver describes its register layout once—width, stride, which registers are volatile, which may be cached, which are readable—and then works through regmap_read(), regmap_write() and regmap_update_bits() without naming a bus. In exchange it gets a register cache (flat, rbtree, or maple-tree backed), the locking, a debugfs dump of every register, and through regmap-irq a ready-made implementation of the "interrupt status and mask registers sitting behind a slow bus" pattern that would otherwise be rewritten in every I2C driver. For a memory-mapped device the entry point is devm_regmap_init_mmio(), and around 250 drivers in this tree use one of the MMIO forms. Everything regmap does is built on the accessors above, but a new driver for a chip with more than a handful of registers should decide deliberately whether it needs them directly.

The Unified Device Model

The device model was a young addition to the 2.6 kernel. In Linux 7.0.12 it is the backbone of the entire driver subsystem. Every device, every driver, and every bus is a kernel object linked into one global tree, exported to userspace through /sys, and joined by a single matching-and-probing algorithm. The payoff is uniformity: hotplug, power management, /dev node creation, and the devm_* managed-resource lifetime all work the same way for a PCIe NVMe controller and for an I2C temperature sensor on an ARM64 board. Figure 13-2 shows the relationship between the four central objects, and Figure 13-3 expands it into a full reference — the same structures mapped onto the sysfs tree, the match-and-bind flow, and the core APIs — that the rest of this section works through.

For a developer, the practical shape of this is that writing a driver means filling in structures and callbacks, not calling into the core imperatively. You declare which hardware you support, provide a probe() to bring a device up and a remove() to tear it down, keep your per-device state behind dev_set_drvdata(), log through the device with dev_err()/dev_info(), and expose tunables as sysfs attributes. The core does the rest: it finds your hardware, calls your probe(), creates the sysfs and /dev entries, and—if you used devm_* helpers—frees everything automatically on unbind. The subsections below introduce each object and the developer-facing calls that go with it.

kobjects, ksets, and sysfs

At the base of the model is the struct kobject (include/linux/kobject.h). A kobject gives an object three things: a name, a reference count (kref) that controls its lifetime, and a directory in /sys. It is almost never used on its own; instead it is embedded inside a larger structure—a struct device, a struct device_driver, and so on—so that the generic sysfs and lifetime machinery can operate on any object through its embedded kobject, recovering the container with container_of(). 

struct kobject {
        const char              *name;
        struct list_head        entry;
        struct kobject          *parent;   /* -> sysfs parent directory */
        struct kset             *kset;
        const struct kobj_type  *ktype;    /* release + sysfs_ops + attrs */
        struct kernfs_node      *sd;       /* the sysfs directory entry   */
        struct kref             kref;      /* reference count             */
        ...
};

It is worth being precise about what "embedded" means, because Figure 13-2 draws a single kobject feeding three objects and that can mislead. Each object embeds its own, separate kobject—they are not one shared instance. A struct device contains its kobject directly, as the dev->kobj member; a struct device_driver carries its own kobject inside a private sub-structure (driver_private), and a struct bus_type carries its own inside subsys_private. So a device, its driver, and their bus each hold a distinct kobject, and the single box in the figure depicts the shared pattern, not a shared object.

The reason they cannot share one kobject is exactly what a kobject provides. Each of these objects needs its own directory in sysfs (/sys/devices/… for the device, /sys/bus/<bus>/drivers/… for the driver, /sys/bus/<bus> for the bus), its own name, and its own reference count so its lifetime can end independently of the others—a driver module may unload while the device it drove remains. One kobject can back exactly one directory, one name, and one refcount, so three independently-named, independently-reclaimed objects necessarily need three kobjects. The embedding pattern gives each its identity while letting one body of generic code manipulate them all through the common struct kobject type.

The kobj_type attached to each kobject supplies its release() callback (run when the last reference drops), its sysfs_ops, and its default_groups—the set of attribute files to create. A struct kset is a collection of kobjects that also is a kobject, which is how sysfs gets its directory hierarchy: a kset is a directory, and the kobjects it contains are entries beneath it. When kobject_add() runs (lib/kobject.c), it calls into create_dir(), which invokes sysfs_create_dir_ns() and then materializes the attribute files from default_groups. In short, the kobject graph is the sysfs tree; the familiar layout under /sys/devices, /sys/bus, and /sys/class is a direct projection of the kobject parent links. Drivers seldom manipulate these objects by hand, but understanding the core API makes the model concrete—and subsystem authors use it directly. Setup follows a fixed shape: kobject_init() attaches a kobj_type, kobject_add() places the kobject in the tree and creates its sysfs directory, and kobject_init_and_add() combines the two. Teardown mirrors it: kobject_del() removes the directory and kobject_put() drops a reference (freeing the object, via its release(), when the count hits zero), balanced by kobject_get(). Two lifetime rules go with that API and are the source of most bugs at this layer. The first is that a kobject is freed only by its release() callback, never by the code that allocated it: once kobject_init() has run, the containing structure belongs to the reference count, so calling kfree() on it directly—even in an error path—corrupts the model. The second follows from it: kobject_init_and_add() initializes before it can fail, so a failing call must still be cleaned up with kobject_put() rather than by freeing the container (lib/kobject.c says so explicitly in the function's documentation). A kobj_type without a release() method is likewise a bug: kobject_cleanup() notices the missing method and reports the object as broken rather than leaking it silently.

Most driver authors never touch a kobject directly—two higher-level facilities cover what they actually need. The first is sysfs attributes: to expose a value under a device's directory, a driver declares an attribute with the DEVICE_ATTR_RO/DEVICE_ATTR_RW macros, which pair a name with show() and store() callbacks, groups them with ATTRIBUTE_GROUPS(), and points the driver's dev_groups at the result so the files are created and removed automatically with the device. Reading the file calls show(); writing it calls store(). This is how a driver publishes status and accepts tuning without inventing an ioctl. 

static ssize_t temperature_show(struct device *dev,
                                struct device_attribute *attr, char *buf)
{
        struct my_dev *d = dev_get_drvdata(dev);
        return sysfs_emit(buf, "%d\n", read_temp(d));   /* cat /sys/.../temperature */
}
static DEVICE_ATTR_RO(temperature);

static struct attribute *my_attrs[] = { &dev_attr_temperature.attr, NULL };
ATTRIBUTE_GROUPS(my);            /* -> my_groups, hooked via .dev_groups */

Three details of that example carry conventions worth stating. The show() method writes into a one-page buffer, so it must use sysfs_emit() (or sysfs_emit_at() when appending), which knows the buffer's origin and bounds it—plain sprintf() is a long-standing source of overflow bugs and is now flagged in review. The attributes are reached through default_groups in the kobj_type, not through a default_attrs array; the older field was removed during the 5.x series, so pre-2022 code and documentation show a form that no longer compiles. And sysfs enforces a one value per file rule: a file holds a single number or string, not a parsed record, because userspace tools read these with cat and expect no format to parse. Data that does not fit that rule belongs in debugfs, a binary attribute, or a chardev interface instead.

Sysfs itself is not a filesystem in its own right in modern kernels: it is a thin client of kernfs (fs/kernfs/), the in-memory pseudo-filesystem library that also backs cgroupfs and a few others. That is what the struct kernfs_node *sd pointer in struct kobject refers to—the kernfs node standing for the object's directory. Kernfs owns the directory tree, the attribute-file seq_file plumbing, and the notification support behind sysfs_notify() and poll() on an attribute, which is how a driver can wake a userspace poller when a value changes rather than making it spin on cat.

The second facility is reference counting. Because a kobject carries a kref, a developer who stashes a pointer to a device that might be unplugged pins it with get_device() and releases it with put_device(); the device's release() callback runs only when the last reference drops. In ordinary probe/remove code the core holds the references for you, so this matters mainly for drivers that keep a device alive past remove()—but it is the reason a device object never disappears out from under code that legitimately holds it.

One structural change since Linux 2.6 is visible in the listing and easy to miss: buses and classes are now const. A device points at a const struct bus_type and a const struct class, bus_register() takes a const struct bus_type *, and match() receives a const struct device_driver *. In the 2.6 model these objects were writable and subsystems sometimes stored mutable state inside them; in 7.0.12 all such state has moved into the private subsys_private and driver_private structures the driver core allocates, so the definitions a subsystem writes can live in read-only memory. Code ported from older trees that tries to assign to a bus_type field at runtime will simply fail to compile.

Binding is also under administrative control from userspace, which is where the fields at the end of the bus_type listing come in. Every driver directory in sysfs normally carries write-only bind and unbind files (drivers/base/bus.c): writing a device name to unbind detaches the device from its driver, and writing it to bind attaches it again. A driver that cannot survive this sets suppress_bind_attrs and the files are not created. Complementing them, a bus that sets driver_override gives each of its devices a driver_override sysfs file; writing a driver name there forces that device to be matched only against that driver, bypassing the bus's normal match(). Together these are the mechanism behind device assignment to userspace or to a virtual machine: unbind the native driver, write vfio-pci into driver_override, and bind again.

Probing, Matching, and Deferred Probe

Devices and drivers are registered independently and in any order: device_register() (which is device_initialize() followed by device_add()) adds a device, and driver_register() adds a driver (drivers/base/core.c, drivers/base/driver.c). Each side triggers a search of the other. Registering a device runs bus_probe_device(), and registering a driver runs bus_add_driver(); both converge on the same matching loop, so a driver that loads after its hardware, or before it, ends up bound either way.

Matching is delegated to the bus. For each candidate pairing the core calls bus->match(dev, drv); only if that returns true does it proceed to really_probe() (drivers/base/dd.c). really_probe() performs supplier checks, optionally sets up the device's DMA, adds sysfs links, and then calls call_driver_probe(), which prefers bus->probe(dev) if the bus supplies one and otherwise calls drv->probe(dev). A successful probe records the binding (dev->driver is set) and the device appears bound in sysfs. 

/* drivers/base/dd.c — the heart of binding */
static int call_driver_probe(struct device *dev, const struct device_driver *drv)
{
        if (dev->bus->probe)
                return dev->bus->probe(dev);   /* bus wrapper, e.g. platform */
        return drv->probe(dev);                /* else the driver directly  */
}

The most important modern addition—absent from the 2.6-era model—is deferred probe. A driver's probe() may discover that a resource it needs (a clock, a regulator, a GPIO, an interrupt controller) is provided by another device that has not been probed yet. Rather than fail, it returns -EPROBE_DEFER. The core then places the device on a deferred probe list and retries it later, after some other driver successfully probes (drivers/base/dd.c). This solves a problem that is pervasive on device-tree systems, where drivers load in an order unrelated to the dependency graph among on-SoC blocks: without deferred probe, boot order would have to be manually choreographed. It is one of the clearest examples of the model absorbing complexity so individual drivers stay simple.

Deferred probe is, however, a retry loop: it discovers dependencies by failing at them. The kernel's answer to that is device links, a mechanism the 2.6 model had no counterpart for. A call to device_link_add(consumer, supplier, flags) records an explicit supplier → consumer edge between two devices; the edges hang off the links.suppliers and links.consumers lists in struct device. Once the edge exists, the driver core enforces it in four places at once: a consumer is not probed until its suppliers have bound, a supplier's driver cannot be unbound while a consumer is still using it, suspend/resume and shutdown are ordered along the edges rather than merely by parent-child nesting, and—if the link carries DL_FLAG_PM_RUNTIME—a runtime-resumed consumer keeps its suppliers resumed too. Links are managed by the core by default; the DL_FLAG_STATELESS variant hands lifetime back to the caller.

What makes links matter for ordinary drivers is that they are usually created for them, before any probing happens, by fw_devlink. During device registration the core walks the firmware description—device-tree phandle references, ACPI dependencies—and turns each reference into a device link, so the dependency graph is known in advance rather than discovered by repeated failures (drivers/base/core.c). In 7.0.12 this is on by default at the fw_devlink=rpm level, meaning links are created and also carry runtime-PM semantics; the boot parameter accepts off, permissive (create links but only log violations), on, and rpm, and fw_devlink.strict tightens optional dependencies into enforced ones. The practical effect is that -EPROBE_DEFER has become a fallback for dependencies firmware does not express, rather than the primary ordering mechanism, and that probe order on a large ARM64 SoC is now derived from its device tree instead of from link order in the kernel image.

A known dependency graph also makes a second callback possible. sync_state()—present on both struct bus_type and struct device_driver, with the bus taking precedence—is invoked once all known consumers of a device have probed, and the device's state_synced flag records that it has run. It exists to solve the boot-time handoff problem: a clock or regulator left enabled by the bootloader must stay on until every device that might need it has had a chance to claim it, and only then can the supplier's driver safely release the unclaimed resources. Without the link graph the core would have no way to know when "everyone who cares has been heard from," and suppliers would either shut hardware down too early or never at all.

When ordering nevertheless goes wrong, the driver core is introspectable. The file /sys/kernel/debug/devices_deferred lists every device still sitting on the deferred list along with the reason string its last failed probe supplied, which is normally enough to identify the missing supplier. Because a device whose driver never loads would otherwise wait forever, the core also arms a timer: deferred_probe_timeout= (default 10 seconds when modules are enabled, and zero when they are not) bounds the wait, after which driver_deferred_probe_check_state() converts a would-be deferral into -ETIMEDOUT—or -ENODEV on a modular-free kernel where no further driver can possibly appear—so boot proceeds with the device unbound instead of stalling.

Finally, probing need not be serial. A driver may set .probe_type = PROBE_PREFER_ASYNCHRONOUS in its device_driver, or an administrator may request it per driver at boot with driver_async_probe=name (or driver_async_probe=* for all), and the core then runs those probe() calls on the asynchronous domain in parallel with the rest of initialization. This is how boot time is recovered on machines with many slow-to-initialize devices—disks and storage controllers especially. The cost is a discipline requirement: an asynchronous probe() runs concurrently with other probes, so it must not assume that any particular sibling has already been initialized, and code that genuinely needs everything settled calls wait_for_device_probe() rather than relying on ordering. PROBE_FORCE_SYNCHRONOUS is the escape hatch for drivers that cannot tolerate it.

From the developer's side, probe() is where a device is brought to life, and it follows a recognizable shape: allocate per-device state with devm_kzalloc(), acquire resources (map registers, get the IRQ, fetch clocks and regulators), register the device with its subsystem, and stash the state with dev_set_drvdata(). Every acquired resource should come from a devm_* helper so that a failure midway—or a later unbind—unwinds automatically; a fully managed driver often needs no remove() at all. The one place deferred probe shows up in code is error handling: when a resource getter returns -EPROBE_DEFER, the driver must propagate it unchanged, and the idiom for that is dev_err_probe(), which logs a real error but stays quiet for the expected deferral case. Devices that need a firmware blob fetch it here too, with request_firmware(), which reads the file from /lib/firmware—or, when that filesystem may not be mounted yet and waiting would stall the boot, with request_firmware_nowait(), which returns immediately and delivers the blob to a callback once it is available. The SoC resources section later in this chapter covers both, along with the clock, regulator, and reset calls.

Device Classes and the Driver Core

Where a bus groups devices by how they connect, a class (struct class, include/linux/device/class.h) groups them by what they do. All TTYs appear under /sys/class/tty, all network interfaces under /sys/class/net, regardless of whether they hang off PCIe, USB, or a platform bus. A class provides a natural home for the attributes and the /dev node policy shared by devices of a kind, and it supplies the dev_uevent and devnode callbacks that tell userspace what device node to create and with what name. The bus and the class give a device two independent views, and this is directly visible in sysfs. Each device has exactly one canonical directory under /sys/devices/…, positioned by its parent links to mirror the physical topology. Its bus and its class then each add a symlink back to that one directory: a network card appears under /sys/bus/pci/devices/… (the topological view) and under /sys/class/net/eth0 (the functional view), both pointing at the same underlying object. The bus answers "where is it plugged in?"; the class answers "what kind of thing is it?" A developer navigating sysfs uses whichever view fits the question, and the full device-model diagram (Figure 13-3) shows these symlinks explicitly.

For a developer, a class is also the simplest way to make a /dev node appear. A driver creates a class once with class_create(), then calls device_create() for each device, passing a dev_t; that call builds a struct device, sets its devt, and thereby triggers the devtmpfs/uevent machinery that materializes the node. This is the manual counterpart to the miscdevice shortcut shown later—useful when a driver manages several minors under a class of its own. 

my_class = class_create("mychar");                 /* /sys/class/mychar */
device_create(my_class, NULL, devno, NULL, "mychar%d", i);  /* /dev/mychar0 … */
...
device_destroy(my_class, devno);
class_destroy(my_class);

In practice most drivers never call class_create() at all. The class their device belongs to already exists, owned by a subsystem, and the driver registers with that subsystem instead: a serial driver calls tty_register_device(), an input driver input_register_device(), a NIC register_netdev(), and each of those creates the class device, the sysfs entries, and (where applicable) the /dev node on the driver's behalf. Creating your own class is reserved for introducing a genuinely new category of device. Either way, class-wide sysfs attributes are declared with the CLASS_ATTR_RO/CLASS_ATTR_RW macros, the class analogue of the per-device DEVICE_ATTR macros seen earlier, so a subsystem can publish knobs that apply to all its members.

The device model also carries the kernel's power-management state, and it is worth seeing where it lives because a modern driver is expected to participate. The power member of struct device holds a dev_pm_info block, and the pm pointer on a driver, bus, or class supplies struct dev_pm_ops—which contains both the system-sleep callbacks (suspend, resume, and their freeze/thaw variants for hibernation) and the runtime PM callbacks runtime_suspend, runtime_resume, and runtime_idle. Runtime PM is usage-counted rather than scheduled: a driver enables it once with devm_pm_runtime_enable(), takes a reference around each access with pm_runtime_resume_and_get(), and drops it with pm_runtime_put_autosuspend(). When the count reaches zero the core calls runtime_suspend()—immediately, or after the grace period set by pm_runtime_set_autosuspend_delay() for devices whose resume is expensive enough that idling them instantly would cost more than it saves. The device links described earlier extend this across the dependency graph, so resuming a consumer resumes its suppliers first, and a parent cannot suspend beneath an active child. Chapter 21 takes the runtime PM core and the system-sleep phases apart.

Underneath all of this sits the driver core in drivers/base/—the code that implements device_add(), driver_register(), bus_register(), and the probing machinery. In 7.0.12 each bus keeps its device and driver lists in a private subsys_private structure using reference-counted klist lists, and the whole apparatus is what turns "register an object" into the visible effects a user sees: a new directory under /sys, a uevent sent to udev, and—if the device has a devt—a node created under /dev.

That uevent pipeline is worth making concrete, because it is how the kernel and userspace stay in sync. On every add, remove, or change, the core emits a uevent—a small text message with keys such as ACTION=add, DEVPATH=, SUBSYSTEM=, and, for a device node, MAJOR=/MINOR=—over a netlink socket to whichever daemon is listening; what userspace then does with it is the subject of the device-files section later in this chapter. What matters here is the symmetry: hotplugging a USB device and unplugging it later run the exact same machinery as a boot-time device—device_add() and device_del()—which is why removable and built-in hardware behave uniformly.

Two details of that pipeline differ from the 2.6-era design. The netlink socket is now the only path that matters: the original mechanism, in which the kernel forked /sbin/hotplug once per event, survives as CONFIG_UEVENT_HELPER but is normally disabled, because a modern boot generates thousands of events in a few seconds and a process fork per event is untenable. The other detail is that uevents can be replayed on demand: every device directory carries a writable uevent file, and writing to it emits a synthetic event for that device (kobject_synth_uevent()). This is the mechanism behind udevadm trigger, and it is how userspace recovers when a daemon starts after the devices it cares about already exist.

The whole flow, from the driver filling in a structure to a file appearing in /dev and a service reacting to it, is the payoff of the model: the developer's leverage over it is small and declarative—fill in the driver struct, provide probe()—and the core turns that into hardware brought up and files created. The next sections follow the two remaining threads: how devices are enumerated in the first place, and how a device number becomes a file in /dev.

Device Enumeration: Device Tree and ACPI

The device model explains how a device, once it exists as a struct device, is matched to a driver. It says nothing about where that struct device comes from in the first place. Enumeration— discovering what hardware is present and creating a device object for each piece—is where x86_64 and ARM64 diverge most sharply, and handling both cleanly is one of the modern kernel's real achievements. This section covers the two firmware mechanisms and the platform bus that ties them to the driver model.

In the 2.6 era, a non-discoverable board's peripherals were hard-coded in architecture C files—the so-called board files under arch/arm/mach-*—that manually created a platform_device for each on-board block. That approach did not scale: every new board meant new kernel code, and one binary could not serve many boards. Modern Linux replaced it with firmware-provided data: the kernel parses a description handed to it at boot and builds the device objects from that, so a single kernel image supports any board whose firmware describes compatible hardware. The two description formats are the device tree and ACPI.

The parenthetical architectures in the two headings below name the common case, not a rule. Device tree is the enumeration mechanism for ARM64, but also for RISC-V, PowerPC, and MIPS, and ACPI is not confined to x86_64: ARM64 servers built to the vendor specifications use ACPI, which is why drivers/acpi/arm64/ exists. On ARM64 the choice is made at boot, and the mechanism is worth knowing because it surprises people. The bootloader always hands the kernel a device-tree blob; if that blob is a stub—a tree carrying little more than a /chosen node—acpi_boot_table_init() takes it as a signal to look for ACPI tables instead and, if they pass a sanity check, enables ACPI and enumerates from it (arch/arm64/kernel/acpi.c). A non-empty device tree wins by default, and acpi=on, acpi=force, and acpi=off override the decision explicitly. Everything above the firmware layer is unaffected either way, which is the point of the fwnode abstraction described at the end of this section.

Discoverable versus Non-Discoverable Buses

Buses fall into two camps. A discoverable bus lets software enumerate its devices by reading standardized identity registers. PCIe is the archetype: the kernel walks configuration space bus by bus, and each function reports a vendor and device ID, a class code, its register windows through base address registers (BARs), and its interrupt needs. The kernel assigns each BAR an address, records the windows as resources, and creates a struct device for the function— all without any external description. USB and Thunderbolt work the same way, which is what makes them hot-pluggable: plug in a device and its identity registers are simply read.

A non-discoverable bus has no such protocol. The blocks integrated into a system-on-chip—its UARTs, I2C and SPI controllers, clocks, pin controllers, and interrupt controllers—sit at fixed physical addresses and cannot be probed blindly; reading an unmapped address may simply hang the bus. Something outside the kernel must therefore declare that these devices exist, at what addresses, and with which interrupts and clocks. That declaration is the job of firmware, and the two forms it takes are the device tree and ACPI.

The two camps are not mutually exclusive; real systems layer them. An ARM64 SoC's PCIe host controller is itself a non-discoverable block described in the device tree, but the PCIe devices behind it are discovered normally. Likewise, an I2C controller is enumerated from firmware, while the sensors on its bus are declared as child nodes because I2C has no discovery protocol of its own. So a single machine routinely uses both mechanisms at once, and the device model presents the result uniformly regardless of how each device was found.

The Device Tree (ARM64)

A device tree is a compact, hierarchical data structure—compiled from a human-readable .dts source into a .dtb blob—that the bootloader hands to the kernel. Each node describes one device: its compatible string (which identifies the driver that can handle it), its register range (reg), its interrupt, and references to the clocks, regulators, and other resources it depends on. During boot the kernel parses the blob and creates a struct device for each node, attaching the node to the device's of_node field. 

/* A UART node in an ARM64 device tree */
uart0: serial@9000000 {
        compatible = "arm,pl011", "arm,primecell";
        reg = <0x0 0x9000000 0x0 0x1000>;   /* MMIO base and size */
        interrupts = <GIC_SPI 1 IRQ_TYPE_LEVEL_HIGH>;
        clocks = <&uartclk>, <&apb_pclk>;
        clock-names = "uartclk", "apb_pclk";
        status = "okay";
};

A few structural conventions make the tree work. Nodes are named name@address, and the parent's #address-cells/#size-cells properties define how many 32-bit cells make up the address and size in each child's reg—which is why the example's reg has four cells (a 64-bit base and a 64-bit size). Cross-references between nodes use phandles: the &uartclk in clocks points at the clock-provider node elsewhere in the tree, and at boot the kernel follows that link to find the clock the UART depends on—precisely the kind of dependency that triggers deferred probe if the clock provider has not registered yet. The status property enables or disables a node, so a board file can switch peripherals on or off without deleting them. Table 13-10 lists the properties a driver author meets most often.

A driver advertises which nodes it supports through an of_device_id table listing the compatible strings it recognizes, referenced from its device_driver.of_match_table. Matching prefers the most specific string: a node compatible with both "arm,pl011" and the generic "arm,primecell" binds to whichever driver claims the more specific ID. Inside probe(), the driver reads the resources the node described—typically via platform_get_resource()/devm_platform_ioremap_resource() for the register window and platform_get_irq() for the interrupt, and device_property_read_u32() or the of_* helpers for named properties—so the same driver works on any board whose device tree lists a compatible UART, regardless of the exact addresses. This indirection is why one ARM64 kernel image can boot on many different boards: the code is fixed, the device tree varies.

Nodes that are not devices. Not every node describes hardware a driver binds to. Three in particular are read by the kernel long before any driver runs. /chosen carries the boot arguments in bootargs—which is where the kernel command line comes from on a device-tree system—along with the initrd's location and a stdout-path that names the console node, which is how earlycon finds a UART before any driver has probed (drivers/of/fdt.c). /memory declares the physical RAM banks, so the memory map itself arrives as device-tree data. And /reserved-memory carves out regions the kernel must leave alone or manage specially: a child with no-map is kept out of the linear mapping entirely, typically because a co-processor or secure firmware owns it, while one compatible with "shared-dma-pool" becomes a CMA area (kernel/dma/contiguous.c) that the DMA allocator later draws large contiguous buffers from—the firmware-side half of the coherent allocations discussed later in this chapter.

Bindings are an ABI. A compatible string and the properties that go with it form a contract between firmware and kernel, and because a deployed board's DTB may never be updated, that contract has to be stable: the kernel cannot rename a property and fix all the callers, the way it can with an internal API. This is what Documentation/devicetree/bindings/ is for, and it is no longer prose. In 7.0.12 the directory holds more than five thousand machine-readable YAML schemas—against roughly seven hundred surviving legacy .txt files—and the build can check against them: make dt_binding_check validates the schemas themselves and make dtbs_check validates every compiled DTB against the schema for each node's compatible. A new binding is now reviewed and merged as a schema before the driver that consumes it, which is a substantial change in how device-tree support is written.

Inspecting the live tree. The unflattened tree is visible from userspace, which makes it easy to check what firmware actually said rather than what the source says. Each device_node gets a kobject under /sys/firmware/devicetree/base, so the tree appears as directories whose files are the raw property values, with the familiar /proc/device-tree path kept as a symlink to it (drivers/of/base.c). The unmodified blob is also exported whole as /sys/firmware/fdt, which can be pulled off a running machine and decompiled with dtc -I dtb -O dts—invaluable when the bootloader patches the tree before handing it over, since the DTB the kernel received may differ from the one that was built.

Which nodes become devices. A common surprise is that the kernel does not create a platform_device for every node in the tree. of_platform_default_populate() creates devices for the root's children and then descends only into nodes that are compatible with "simple-bus", "simple-mfd", "isa", or "arm,amba-bus" (drivers/of/platform.c)—that is, into containers that are pure address-translation groupings with no controller of their own. The children of a real bus controller are deliberately left alone, because creating them is the controller driver's job: the I2C core walks its controller's child nodes and registers an i2c_client for each, the SPI core does the same for spi_device, and each gets a bus-appropriate device type rather than a generic platform device. This is also why a node under an unprobed controller produces no device at all until that controller binds.

Holding a node reference. struct device_node is reference-counted, and the of_* lookup functions—of_find_node_by_name(), of_parse_phandle(), and the for_each_child_of_node() iterators—return a node with its count already incremented. The caller must of_node_put() it, including when breaking out of a loop early, and forgetting that has been a durable source of leaks. Modern code avoids the problem with the cleanup-attribute helpers added during the 6.x series: for_each_child_of_node_scoped() and a struct device_node *np __free(device_node) declaration attach the of_node_put() to the variable's scope, so any return path releases it (include/linux/of.h). New code should prefer the scoped forms.

Source layout: .dtsi and .dts. Device-tree source is split so that hardware common to a whole SoC is written once and reused by every board that uses that SoC. Shared fragments live in include files with the .dtsi extension (the SoC's UARTs, I2C controllers, GIC, clocks—everything on the chip), and each board has a small .dts that pulls in the SoC .dtsi with #include and describes only what is board-specific: which peripherals are wired up, what is connected to them, and how much RAM is present. The files sit under arch/arm64/boot/dts/<vendor>/, and a typical stack is three layers deep—a generic SoC .dtsi, an optional SoC-variant .dtsi, and the board .dts on top.

Overriding by label. A board customizes the SoC's shared nodes rather than copying them. Every node can carry a label (the uart0: before serial@9000000 earlier), and a later file reopens that node by writing &label { … } to add or change properties. This is how a board enables a peripheral the SoC .dtsi left disabled, or attaches a device to a bus the SoC merely declared: 

/* board.dts */
#include "soc.dtsi"                 /* brings in uart0, i2c1, … (status = "disabled") */

&uart0 {
        status = "okay";            /* enable the SoC's UART on this board */
};

&i2c1 {
        status = "okay";
        touchscreen@38 {            /* a device only this board has */
                compatible = "edt,edt-ft5406";
                reg = <0x38>;
                interrupts-extended = <&gpio2 5 IRQ_TYPE_EDGE_FALLING>;
        };
};

Building the DTB. The source is not consumed directly. The kernel build first runs the C preprocessor over the .dts—that is what resolves the #include directives and the symbolic constants such as GIC_SPI and IRQ_TYPE_LEVEL_HIGH—and then feeds the result to the device-tree compiler dtc (scripts/dtc/), which flattens it into a binary Flattened Device Tree blob, the .dtb. Each board .dts produces its own independent .dtb; the build directory ends up with one blob per board, and each is a complete, self-contained description of that machine.

Inside the DTB. The flattened blob has a fixed layout: a header beginning with the magic value 0xd00dfeed, a memory-reservation block (regions the kernel must not allocate), a structure block, and a strings block. The structure block encodes the tree as a stream of tokens—FDT_BEGIN_NODE, FDT_PROP, FDT_END_NODE—so it can be walked without random access. The strings block is the answer to "what is the device-tree index in the DTB": because property names like compatible and reg repeat thousands of times, each name is stored once in the strings block, and every FDT_PROP token refers to it by a byte offset into that block rather than repeating the text. At boot the kernel unflattens this blob into the live tree of struct device_node objects that of_node pointers reference.

Selecting a board's DTB. The kernel does not pick among boards at runtime—selection happens before it starts. The bootloader is built or configured for a specific board, loads that board's single .dtb into memory, and passes its physical address to the kernel (on ARM64 the boot protocol delivers it in register x0). The kernel then reads the root node's model and compatible to know which machine it is on—of_flat_dt_get_machine_name() logs the model string, and drivers or arch code test board identity with of_machine_is_compatible() for machine-specific quirks. Some setups (notably U-Boot FIT images) bundle several DTBs together with named configurations and let the bootloader choose the right one from a hardware ID it reads at runtime—but even then, exactly one DTB reaches the kernel.

Packaging many boards in one image. A single .dtb describes exactly one machine—it never contains several boards. When a product must ship one firmware image that boots many hardware variants, the DTBs are bundled inside a container format that carries an index the bootloader searches. Each entry in that index is tagged with hardware identifiers—SoC ID, platform/board ID, and revision—and at boot the bootloader reads the running hardware's actual IDs, selects the one matching entry, and passes that single DTB to the kernel. So "many board device trees in one image" is always a bootloader-level container with a lookup table, not one flat DTB. Table 13-11 lists the formats seen most often.

Overlays. Fixed hardware is described by the base DTB, but pluggable add-ons—expansion capes, HATs, mezzanine cards—are not known when the board .dts is written. A device-tree overlay handles these: it is a separately-compiled fragment (a .dtbo) that names a target node in the base tree and supplies an __overlay__ block of properties and child nodes to graft onto it. Overlays can be applied by the bootloader before boot or, at runtime, through the kernel's overlay interface, and applying one triggers the same probing path as any other device addition—so plugging in a supported cape makes its devices appear exactly as if they had been in the base tree.

The runtime path is worth being precise about, because it is narrower than people expect. Applying an overlay in a running kernel means calling of_overlay_fdt_apply(), available only under CONFIG_OF_OVERLAY, and of_overlay_remove() to undo it (drivers/of/overlay.c). There is no general-purpose userspace interface for this: a proposed configfs mechanism was never merged, precisely because grafting arbitrary firmware descriptions onto a live tree is difficult to make safe. In practice runtime overlays are applied by in-kernel code that knows what it just detected—a connector or expansion-bus driver that reads an add-on's identification EEPROM and applies the matching .dtbo—while everything else is applied by the bootloader before the kernel starts.

ACPI (x86_64)

On x86_64 servers and laptops the same role is played by ACPI (Advanced Configuration and Power Interface). Firmware exposes a set of tables—chiefly the DSDT and any SSDTs—containing a namespace of objects that describe the platform's devices, their resources, and their power-management methods. Unlike a device tree, which is pure data, ACPI objects can include bytecode (AML) that the kernel's ACPI interpreter executes, so firmware can compute resources or run power transitions. Devices that are not on a self-describing bus—an embedded controller, an I2C-connected touchpad, a platform sensor—appear as namespace objects with a _HID (hardware ID) or _CID (compatible ID), and drivers match them through an acpi_device_id table referenced from device_driver.acpi_match_table.

The kernel finds all of this by following a chain of firmware tables. At boot it locates the RSDP (Root System Description Pointer), which leads to the XSDT—a table of pointers to every other ACPI table. From there the kernel reads the fixed-hardware and topology tables it needs before any driver runs: the MADT enumerates the interrupt controllers (local APICs and I/O APICs), the MCFG gives the base address of PCIe configuration space so the bus can be walked, the FADT describes fixed power/register features, and the DSDT plus any SSDT tables carry the device namespace itself. The namespace inside the DSDT/SSDTs is a tree of objects with four-character names, rooted at scopes such as \_SB (the system bus). Each device object bundles standard sub-objects the kernel queries by name: besides _HID/_CID for matching, _UID distinguishes multiple instances of the same device, _ADR gives a device's address on its parent bus, and _STA reports whether the device is present and enabled. Because these objects can be AML methods rather than static data, the kernel carries a complete AML interpreter (ACPICA) and executes firmware code to evaluate them—powerful, but also why buggy vendor firmware so often surfaces as kernel ACPI workarounds.

Each ACPI device object also carries standard methods the kernel reads during enumeration: _CRS reports the device's current resource settings (its address ranges and interrupts, the ACPI analogue of a device tree's reg and interrupts), while _DSD (Device-Specific Data) carries named properties in the same key/value form device tree uses. There is even a bridge in the other direction: a device with the special _HID value PRP0001 tells the kernel to match it using a device-tree-style compatible string found in its _DSD, so a driver originally written for device tree can bind on an ACPI system with no changes.

Enumeration itself is done by acpi_bus_scan() (drivers/acpi/scan.c), which walks the namespace and creates an acpi_device for each object. That acpi_device becomes the companion of a struct device (retrievable with ACPI_COMPANION()), so the object joins the same unified device model used everywhere else. Depending on the device, the scan either binds a native ACPI driver or spawns a bus-specific device—acpi_create_platform_device() for a memory-mapped block, or an i2c_client/spi_device for a peripheral on a described I2C or SPI controller—so from the driver's side an ACPI-enumerated device looks just like a device-tree-enumerated one. ACPI additionally owns runtime and system power management: per-device power states through _PS0…_PS3, system sleep states (S0–S5), and general-purpose events (GPEs) that deliver hardware notifications such as lid or battery changes and bus hotplug.

Enumeration order has a firmware-declared form here too. An ACPI device object may list _DEP, an explicit set of objects it depends on, and acpi_bus_scan() counts the unmet entries in dep_unmet and holds the device back from enumeration until its suppliers are ready (drivers/acpi/scan.c). It is the ACPI analogue of a device-tree phandle reference, and it feeds the same device-link machinery that fw_devlink builds from device tree, so the probe-ordering discussion earlier in this chapter applies unchanged on an ACPI system. The kernel also maintains lists of hardware IDs whose _DEP entries are ignored or honored specially, a reminder that this data comes from vendor firmware and is not always meant literally.

Because ACPI tables come from firmware a developer usually cannot rebuild, the kernel makes them both visible and replaceable. Every table the kernel loaded is exported in raw form under /sys/firmware/acpi/tables/, with runtime-loaded ones under its dynamic/ subdirectory, so a table can be pulled off a running machine and disassembled with iasl to see exactly what the firmware said. More useful still, CONFIG_ACPI_TABLE_UPGRADE—enabled by default—lets a corrected table be packed into the initrd and substituted for the firmware's copy at boot, which is how a suspected DSDT bug is tested without reflashing the machine, and the acpi_configfs module can load an SSDT into a running kernel. One caution about patching in this area: drivers/acpi/acpica/ is not Linux code but an upstream reference implementation imported wholesale, so fixes belong upstream in ACPICA rather than in the kernel tree.

On ARM64 servers, ACPI additionally carries the platform topology that a device tree would otherwise describe, through a set of tables the x86_64 world never sees (drivers/acpi/arm64/). The IORT (I/O Remapping Table) maps device IDs through SMMUs and to the GIC's interrupt translation services—the information an IOMMU driver needs to know which stream ID belongs to which device; the GTDT describes the architected timers and watchdogs; the PPTT gives CPU and cache topology to the scheduler; and the SPCR names the serial console so earlycon works without a command-line argument. The MADT is present as on x86_64 but carries GICC entries describing GIC redistributors rather than local APICs. These are the tables that make an ARM64 server bootable from firmware description alone.

The kernel deliberately hides the difference between device tree and ACPI behind the fwnode abstraction introduced earlier. Generic property helpers such as device_property_read_u32() read a named property from whichever firmware described the device—_DSD on ACPI, a node property on device tree—so a well-written driver reads "clock-frequency" the same way regardless of the source. This is what lets a single I2C or SPI peripheral driver serve both an ARM64 board and an x86_64 laptop with no architecture-specific code, and it is why modern drivers are written against fwnode/device_property_* rather than the older of_*-only helpers.

Platform Devices and Drivers

Devices that are enumerated from firmware rather than a discoverable bus are attached to the platform bus, a synthetic bus whose whole purpose is to host memory-mapped, directly-addressed devices. Such a device is a struct platform_device (which embeds a struct device plus an array of struct resource), and its driver is a struct platform_driver (include/linux/platform_device.h).

The developer almost never creates these platform devices by hand—the firmware layer does it. On a device-tree system, of_platform_populate() walks the tree at boot and creates a platform_device for each eligible node, copying its reg and interrupts into the resource array and attaching the node as of_node. On ACPI, acpi_create_platform_device() does the equivalent from a namespace object. This is the direct replacement for the 2.6-era board file that used to call platform_device_register() by hand: the same platform_device objects now come from data, not code. 

static const struct of_device_id my_of_ids[] = {
        { .compatible = "vendor,my-uart" },
        { }
};
MODULE_DEVICE_TABLE(of, my_of_ids);

static struct platform_driver my_driver = {
        .probe  = my_probe,
        .remove = my_remove,
        .driver = {
                .name           = "my-uart",
                .of_match_table = my_of_ids,
        },
};
module_platform_driver(my_driver);

The driver structure gathers the device's whole lifecycle. probe() brings a matched device up; remove() tears it down; shutdown() quiesces it at system power-off; and power management flows through driver.pm. One detail catches developers moving between kernel versions: in Linux 7.0.12 remove() returns void, not the historical int—the old signature let drivers "fail" removal, which the core could not honor anyway, so it was removed. The two macros in the example also do real work: MODULE_DEVICE_TABLE(of, …) emits the match strings into the module's modalias metadata, and module_platform_driver() generates the module init/exit. Together they let udev, on seeing an uevent whose MODALIAS= matches a device-tree or ACPI ID, autoload the right driver module on demand—so a driver need not be built in to be found.

The platform bus's match function, platform_match() (drivers/base/platform.c), tries several strategies in a fixed order: an explicit driver_override, then a device-tree (of_match_table) match, then an ACPI (acpi_match_table) match, then a platform id_table, and finally a plain name comparison. That single ordered function is how one platform driver can be bound by device tree on ARM64 and by ACPI on x86_64. Inside probe() the driver pulls its resources from the platform device—platform_get_irq() returns the interrupt (already translated into a Linux IRQ number, as the next section explains), devm_platform_ioremap_resource() maps its registers, and platform_get_resource_byname() fetches a named range—so the driver body is identical no matter which firmware enumerated it.

Two details of that resource retrieval trip people up. platform_get_irq() returns a negative errno on failure and never zero, so the test is if (irq < 0), not a check against zero; it also logs the failure itself, so a driver that adds its own dev_err() produces the message twice. When an interrupt is genuinely optional, the right call is platform_get_irq_optional(), which stays silent. And because the lookup can legitimately fail early—the interrupt controller may not have probed yet—the returned error must be propagated, which is exactly the dev_err_probe() idiom shown earlier.

The platform_driver structure carries a few flags worth naming. driver_managed_dma declares that the driver handles its device's DMA itself rather than through the DMA API—set by VFIO-style drivers, and the reason the IOMMU layer permits them to own the device's address space; it pairs with the driver_override mechanism described earlier as the other half of assigning a device to userspace. prevent_deferred_probe suppresses the deferred-probe retry for drivers that must not be retried. And for drivers that can never be modules, builtin_platform_driver() replaces module_platform_driver(), generating only the initcall.

A common idiom builds on the match table to support many hardware variants from one driver. Each of_device_id (or acpi_device_id) entry has a .data pointer, and a driver sets it to a per-variant configuration structure—register offsets, feature flags, FIFO sizes—for each compatible string it lists. Inside probe() the driver retrieves the entry that actually matched with device_get_match_data() (the firmware-agnostic form; of_device_get_match_data() is the device-tree-only equivalent) and configures itself from it. This is how a single UART or I2C driver cleanly handles a whole family of chip revisions without a chain of string comparisons. For genuinely slow-to-probe devices, a driver can also set driver.probe_type = PROBE_PREFER_ASYNCHRONOUS so the core probes it on a worker thread, letting boot proceed in parallel.

Not every platform device comes from firmware. Kernel code can create one directly with platform_device_alloc()/platform_device_add() or the platform_device_register_simple() helper—used, for example, by multifunction-device (MFD) drivers that split one chip into several sub-devices, or by board glue that needs a device the firmware did not describe. These synthetic platform devices bind to platform drivers by the same platform_match() path, so from the driver's perspective there is no difference between a device the firmware declared and one the kernel manufactured.

The platform bus is only the simplest case of a broader pattern. Other non-discoverable buses have their own device and driver types— i2c_client/i2c_driver, spi_device/spi_driver—but each follows the same recipe: a bus-specific device created from a firmware node, a bus-specific driver with an of_match_table/acpi_match_table, and a probe() that acquires resources through managed helpers. Once you understand the platform bus, the others are variations on it, which is exactly the uniformity the device model was built to provide.

Device Files

So far a device is an object inside the kernel. For a user program to reach it, there must be a name in the filesystem that resolves to the driver—a device file (or device node) such as /dev/ttyS0 or /dev/sda. A device file is a special inode that carries a device number instead of data blocks; opening it connects the calling process to the driver identified by that number. This section follows the chain from the number, through the VFS, to the driver's own file_operations, and finally to how the node in /dev gets created. The mechanism has evolved—from a static /dev of thousands of pre-made nodes, through the short-lived devfs, to today's devtmpfs-plus-udev arrangement—but the inode-and-number model at its core has not.

It is worth saying at the outset what the model does not cover, since the device file is often mistaken for the universal kernel interface. Whole subsystems have no per-device node at all: a network interface is reached by socket and by name, not through /dev. Others invert the ratio, exposing a single control node and returning further descriptors from ioctl() on it—/dev/kvm yields VM and vCPU descriptors, and DRM, VFIO, and io_uring follow the same shape. Those secondary descriptors are usually anonymous inodes, created with anon_inode_getfd(), which have file operations and no name anywhere in the filesystem. So the material below describes the classic and still dominant path from a name to a driver, not the only one.

Device Numbers

Every device file is identified by a dev_t—a 32-bit value split into a major number, which selects the driver, and a minor number, which selects one specific device among those the driver manages. In Linux 7.0.12 the split is 12 bits of major and 20 bits of minor (include/linux/kdev_t.h): MINORBITS is 20, and the MAJOR(), MINOR(), and MKDEV() macros pack and unpack the two halves. 

#define MINORBITS       20
#define MINORMASK       ((1U << MINORBITS) - 1)

#define MAJOR(dev)      ((unsigned int) ((dev) >> MINORBITS))
#define MINOR(dev)      ((unsigned int) ((dev) & MINORMASK))
#define MKDEV(ma,mi)    (((ma) << MINORBITS) | (mi))

The split is visible from userspace. A long listing shows a leading c or b (character or block) where a regular file shows -, and in place of a size it prints the major, minor pair. The list of majors currently claimed by drivers is exported at /proc/devices. 

$ ls -l /dev/null /dev/sda
crw-rw-rw- 1 root root 1,  3 /dev/null    # char, major 1  minor 3
brw-rw---- 1 root disk 8,  0 /dev/sda     # block, major 8 minor 0

Majors come in two flavors. A handful are statically assigned by the Linux device-number registry (for example major 1 for memory devices like /dev/null, major 4 for TTYs), and a driver claims one with register_chrdev_region(). Most modern drivers instead take a dynamically allocated major with alloc_chrdev_region(), which the kernel picks from the high end of the space at registration time; the driver then reads back the number it was given rather than hard-coding one. The minor number identifies the instance—ttyS0, ttyS1, and so on share a major and differ only in minor. Nodes can also be made by hand with mknod (mknod /dev/foo c 42 0), though on a modern system the kernel and udev create them automatically.

The dynamic allocation is more constrained than "the kernel picks a free number" suggests, and the ranges are worth knowing when reading /proc/devices on a live system. find_dynamic_major() (fs/char_dev.c) searches downward from 254 to 234—the block of majors deliberately left unassigned by the registry—and only if that segment is full does it fall back to a second range, 511 down to 384, with CHRDEV_MAJOR_MAX capping the space at 512. This is why dynamically allocated character devices on a typical machine cluster in the 240s and count downward as more drivers load. The registry of static assignments in Documentation/admin-guide/devices.txt is effectively frozen; new drivers are expected to take a dynamic major, or to avoid consuming one at all by registering as a misc device and receiving a minor under the shared major 10, as the character-driver section describes.

One detail routinely trips up code that moves numbers between kernel and userspace: the dev_t a program sees in st_rdev is not laid out the way the kernel's internal dev_t is. Internally the value is simply major << 20 | minor, but on its way out through stat() it is repacked by new_encode_dev() (include/linux/kdev_t.h), which puts the low 8 bits of the minor first, then the major, then the remaining minor bits—a layout inherited from historical Unix encodings and kept for compatibility. The practical rule is that neither side should ever shift the value by hand: use MAJOR()/MINOR()/MKDEV() in the kernel and major()/minor()/makedev() in userspace, and never assume the two produce the same bit pattern.

Device numbers are also a security boundary, which matters as soon as containers are involved. Because a dev_t names a driver directly, the ability to call mknod is the ability to reach any driver in the kernel—so an unprivileged user namespace is not permitted to create device nodes, and container runtimes instead populate a small hand-picked /dev from the host. Access to the nodes that do exist is then restricted per-container: under cgroup v2 this is done by attaching a BPF program of type CGROUP_DEVICE, which is consulted on each open and receives the major, minor, and access type—the replacement for cgroup v1's devices controller and its devices.allow file.

There are two device-file kinds, distinguished by the inode's mode bits: character devices (S_IFCHR), whose driver is spoken to as a byte stream, and block devices (S_IFBLK), which are accessed in fixed-size blocks through the block layer and page cache. This chapter concerns character devices; block devices are the subject of later chapters. A driver acquires a major/minor range with alloc_chrdev_region() (dynamic major) or register_chrdev_region() (fixed major), as the character-driver section details.

VFS Handling of Device Files

When any filesystem creates an inode for a device file, it calls init_special_inode() (fs/inode.c). Rather than install the filesystem's own file operations, this routine records the device number in i_rdev and points the inode's i_fop at a stub table— def_chr_fops for a character device, def_blk_fops for a block device. The character stub is nearly empty: its only real method is .open = chrdev_open. 

/* fs/inode.c */
void init_special_inode(struct inode *inode, umode_t mode, dev_t rdev)
{
        inode->i_mode = mode;
        if (S_ISCHR(mode)) {
                inode->i_fop = &def_chr_fops;
                inode->i_rdev = rdev;
        } else if (S_ISBLK(mode)) {
                inode->i_fop = &def_blk_fops;
                inode->i_rdev = rdev;
        }
        ...
}

The indirection happens at open() time. chrdev_open() (fs/char_dev.c) takes the inode's i_rdev and looks it up in cdev_map, a kobj_map that indexes every registered character device by its number range. The lookup returns the struct cdev the driver registered; from it, chrdev_open() recovers the driver's real file_operations and installs them into the open file with replace_fops(). It then calls the driver's own .open if present. From that point on, every read(), write(), and ioctl() on the descriptor dispatches straight to the driver—the VFS is out of the path. This is the concrete mechanism behind "everything is a file": the device file is an ordinary inode until it is opened, at which moment its operation table is swapped for the driver's. 

/* fs/char_dev.c — the swap that connects a /dev node to its driver */
static int chrdev_open(struct inode *inode, struct file *filp)
{
        struct cdev *p;
        struct kobject *kobj;
        int idx;

        kobj = kobj_lookup(cdev_map, inode->i_rdev, &idx);   /* number -> cdev */
        p = container_of(kobj, struct cdev, kobj);
        ...
        filp->f_op = fops_get(p->ops);       /* install driver's operations */
        replace_fops(filp, filp->f_op);
        if (filp->f_op->open)
                return filp->f_op->open(inode, filp);  /* driver's own open  */
        return 0;
}

Three details make this robust. First, the lookup is cached: after the first open, chrdev_open() stores the resolved cdev in inode->i_cdev and links the inode onto the cdev's i_devices list, so later opens of the same node skip the kobj_lookup() entirely. Second, fops_get() takes a reference on the driver's module (f_op->owner, which is why a char driver sets .owner = THIS_MODULE): the module cannot be unloaded while any descriptor is open, closed by the balancing fops_put() at release. Third, if no cdev is registered for the number, the cdev_map probe fallback issues request_module("char-major-%d-%d", …) to autoload a driver on demand, so opening a node can pull in its module.

That last mechanism only works if the module advertises the number it answers to. MODULE_ALIAS_CHARDEV(major, minor) and MODULE_ALIAS_CHARDEV_MAJOR(major) (include/linux/device.h) emit exactly the char-major-M-m and char-major-M strings that request_module() asks for, which depmod collects into modules.alias. This is the older, number-based half of module autoloading; the modalias path described earlier—where a uevent carries a device-tree or PCI identifier and udev loads the matching driver—is the modern half. A statically numbered node such as /dev/kvm relies on the former; a discovered PCI device relies on the latter.

Block device files take the parallel path. init_special_inode() points a block node's i_fop at def_blk_fops, whose open routes into the block layer rather than a cdev: it resolves the number to a block_device, attaches the block driver's request handling and the page cache, and exposes the device as a seekable array of blocks. That is why a character device is a raw byte stream to its driver while a block device is mediated by the block layer and buffered—the divergence begins right here, at open() time, from the same init_special_inode() starting point.

The asymmetry runs deeper than "one is buffered," and stating it precisely clears up a common confusion. For a character device the stub table is genuinely a stub—def_chr_fops holds only chrdev_open and a no-op llseek—and it is replaced, so the driver's own file_operations ends up in the open file. For a block device def_blk_fops is a complete implementation living in block/fops.c, and it is never replaced: blkdev_open() resolves i_rdev to a struct block_device and records it on the file, but every subsequent read() or write() still goes through the block layer's own methods. Block drivers therefore have no file_operations at all; they supply a gendisk and a request queue, and the block layer—not the VFS—calls into them. So a character driver is reached by function pointer from the file, and a block driver is reached by the block layer after it has built a request. Only the first few instructions of open() are shared.

devtmpfs and udev

In early 2.6 kernels, /dev was a directory of statically created nodes, and keeping it in sync with actual hardware was a chronic problem. Linux 7.0.12 solves it with two cooperating pieces. Inside the kernel, devtmpfs (drivers/base/devtmpfs.c) is a tmpfs-backed /dev that the driver core populates automatically: whenever device_add() registers a device whose devt is nonzero, it calls devtmpfs_create_node(), which creates a matching character or block node with a sensible default mode. This guarantees that a node exists the moment a device appears, even in early boot before any userspace daemon is running.

Mechanically, devtmpfs is driven by a dedicated kernel thread (kdevtmpfs) that services a queue of create/remove requests. The thread is not there to make the work asynchronous—devtmpfs_submit_req() queues the request and then blocks in wait_for_completion(), so device_add() does not return until the node exists. Its purpose is to provide a fixed, privileged context to act in: creating a node means performing a mknod against the devtmpfs mount with root credentials, which cannot safely be done in whatever arbitrary caller context happened to register the device. The node's name, mode, and owner default to what the driver and its class supply—a bare node is 0600 root:root, but a class's devnode() callback can override the name (adding a subdirectory such as /dev/dri/card0) and the permissions. With CONFIG_DEVTMPFS_MOUNT, the kernel even mounts devtmpfs on /dev itself before init runs, which is what lets an initramfs find its root device. On minimal and embedded systems devtmpfs alone is often the entire /dev, with no udev at all.

Two properties of that filesystem are easy to get wrong. The first is that there is only ever one devtmpfs: its get_tree method does not create a new superblock but takes a reference on the existing one (drivers/base/devtmpfs.c), so mounting devtmpfs a second time—inside a container, say—yields the very same /dev the host has, with every node in it. That is precisely why container runtimes do not mount devtmpfs and instead assemble a small /dev of their own from bind mounts and a private tmpfs. The second is CONFIG_DEVTMPFS_SAFE, which mounts /dev with nosuid and noexec. It is a sensible hardening default, with one documented consequence worth remembering when something breaks mysteriously: /dev/mem can no longer be mapped PROT_EXEC, which some legacy non-KMS video drivers relied on.

On a full system, udev refines what devtmpfs provides. The same device_add() that creates the node also sends a uevent—a message carrying MAJOR=, MINOR=, DEVNAME=, and related keys (drivers/base/core.c)—over a netlink socket to the udev daemon. udev matches it against rules in /lib/udev/rules.d and /etc/udev/rules.d and completes the userspace side: setting permissions and ownership, creating human-friendly symlinks (such as /dev/disk/by-uuid/...), loading firmware, and triggering services. Devices that appeared before udev started are not missed: at boot, udevadm trigger re-emits their uevents (a coldplug pass) so the same rules run for built-in and hot-plugged hardware alike.

The division of labor is clean: devtmpfs guarantees a node exists and does so early, while udev decides what it is ultimately named, who may use it, and what should happen when it appears. Both are driven by the same device-model events described earlier, which is why registering a struct device with a device number is all a driver has to do to make /dev/... appear and be wired up correctly.

Interrupt Handling

A device rarely finishes its work the instant the CPU commands it; a disk seeks, a network packet arrives, a key is pressed—all asynchronously. The kernel learns of these events through interrupts: the device raises a signal, the CPU suspends whatever it was doing, and it runs the driver's registered interrupt handler. This section covers how a driver asks to receive interrupts, the strict discipline handlers run under, how work is deferred out of that context, and the modern framework—irq_domain and message-signaled interrupts—that routes hardware interrupts to Linux handlers. Chapter 4 describes the same path from the CPU's side—vectors and the interrupt descriptor table, irq_desc and its flow handlers, softirqs and tasklets—so this section takes the driver's point of view and refers there for the machinery underneath.

Polling versus Interrupt-Driven I/O

There are two ways to wait for a device. Polling repeatedly reads a status register until the device signals ready; it is simple and has low latency, but it burns a CPU doing nothing useful, so it is reserved for very short waits or for drivers of extremely high-throughput devices (some network and NVMe paths poll deliberately). Interrupt-driven I/O instead registers a handler and lets the CPU do other work until the device raises its line. Almost all drivers are interrupt-driven, because it lets one CPU service many devices and lets idle CPUs sleep. The cost is the discipline interrupt context imposes, which the rest of this section is about.

The two approaches are not exclusive; the highest-performance drivers combine them. Under heavy load, a purely interrupt-driven device can trigger an interrupt storm—so many interrupts that the CPU spends all its time entering and leaving handlers and never makes forward progress. The networking stack's NAPI mechanism solves this by switching modes: the first packet raises an interrupt, the driver then disables that interrupt and asks the kernel to poll the device for more packets, and only when the device drains does it re-enable interrupts. This gives low latency when idle and high throughput when busy, and the same interrupt-then-poll pattern appears in high-rate block drivers. It is the practical reconciliation of the two styles this subsection contrasts.

Polling in 7.0.12 is more than a fallback for trivial waits; it is a first-class path in the storage stack. Block multi-queue supports dedicated poll queues (HCTX_TYPE_POLL), and the NVMe driver exposes a poll_queues module parameter that carves some of the device's queues out of interrupt delivery entirely (drivers/nvme/host/pci.c). Requests on those queues raise no interrupt at all: the submitting thread reaps its own completions, which is how io_uring rings created with IORING_SETUP_IOPOLL reach the lowest achievable latency on fast NVMe. The trade is explicit—a CPU is spent spinning in exchange for removing the interrupt, the context switch, and the wakeup from the critical path—so it pays only when the device is fast enough that the interrupt overhead dominates the transfer. There is also a NAPI analogue for storage in lib/irq_poll.c, used by a handful of storage and s390 drivers to budget their completion processing the same way networking does. Between the two extremes sits hardware interrupt coalescing, where the device itself batches completions before signaling—tuned on a NIC with ethtool -C—which reduces interrupt rate without giving up a CPU.

Requesting and Handling IRQs

A driver connects a handler to an interrupt line with request_irq() or, more commonly today, request_threaded_irq() (kernel/irq/manage.c). It supplies the Linux IRQ number (obtained from platform_get_irq() or pci_irq_vector()), a handler function, a set of flags, a name shown in /proc/interrupts, and a dev_id cookie that both identifies the device to the handler and distinguishes handlers on a shared line. 

int request_threaded_irq(unsigned int irq, irq_handler_t handler,
                         irq_handler_t thread_fn, unsigned long irqflags,
                         const char *devname, void *dev_id);

/* request_irq() is the wrapper with thread_fn == NULL */
#define request_irq(irq, handler, flags, name, dev) \
        request_threaded_irq(irq, handler, NULL, flags, name, dev)

A handler returns an irqreturn_t: IRQ_HANDLED if it serviced the device, IRQ_NONE if the interrupt was not its (important on shared lines), or IRQ_WAKE_THREAD to defer to a threaded handler as described below. The flags in the request tune the line's behavior; Table 13-13 lists the common ones. The managed variant devm_request_irq() frees the interrupt automatically when the device is unbound, matching the devm_* pattern used for memory regions.

Table 13-13. Common IRQ request flags
Flag Meaning
IRQF_SHARED The line may be shared by several devices; each handler must recognize its own device and return IRQ_NONE otherwise
IRQF_ONESHOT Keep the line masked until the threaded handler finishes (required for most threaded IRQs)
IRQF_TRIGGER_RISING / _FALLING Edge-triggered on the rising or falling edge
IRQF_TRIGGER_HIGH / _LOW Level-triggered on a high or low level
IRQF_NO_SUSPEND Leave the interrupt enabled across system suspend
IRQF_NO_AUTOEN Register the handler without enabling the line; the driver calls enable_irq() when it is ready
IRQF_NO_THREAD Never convert this handler into a threaded one, even when forced threading is in effect
IRQF_PERCPU The interrupt is per-CPU (timers, IPIs); no affinity handling and no threading

The handler itself has the signature irqreturn_t handler(int irq, void *dev_id), where dev_id is the same cookie passed to request_irq()—typically the driver's per-device structure, recovered without a global lookup. On a shared line the handler is entered for every device on that line, so its first job is to read its own device's status register and, if the interrupt was not its, return IRQ_NONE immediately; only the device that actually raised the line does the work and returns IRQ_HANDLED. The kernel uses these return values to detect a stuck line: an interrupt that fires repeatedly with every handler returning IRQ_NONE is eventually disabled as spurious. All of this is observable at /proc/interrupts, which lists each virq with its per-CPU count, the controller (chip) behind it, its trigger type, and the devname strings of the handlers registered on it—the first place to look when an interrupt is not arriving or is arriving on the wrong CPU.

The registration call has a few rules that are easy to violate. It may sleep, so it cannot be made from atomic context; the dev_id passed to free_irq() must be the same cookie given to request_irq(), since on a shared line that cookie is the only thing identifying which handler to remove; and free_irq() waits for any in-flight execution of the handler to finish before returning, which is what makes it safe to free the driver's per-device structure immediately afterwards—but also what deadlocks if it is called from within the handler's own path. There is a genuine ordering hazard at registration too: a level-triggered or shared line can fire the instant the handler is installed, before the driver has finished initializing the state that handler touches. IRQF_NO_AUTOEN closes that window by registering the handler without enabling the line, leaving the driver to call enable_irq() when it is actually ready. As elsewhere, the managed forms (devm_request_irq(), devm_request_threaded_irq()) are preferred, and they release the interrupt at unbind in the correct order relative to the rest of the device's managed resources.

Which context a handler actually runs in is not fixed by the driver alone, and this surprises people reading interrupt code for the first time. The kernel can force threading: booting with threadirqs enables the force_irqthreads static key, and under CONFIG_PREEMPT_RT forced threading is unconditional (kernel/irq/manage.c). Any handler not marked IRQF_NO_THREAD, IRQF_PERCPU, or IRQF_ONESHOT is then run in a kernel thread even though its author wrote it as a plain top half—which is the point, since moving handlers out of hard-interrupt context is what bounds interrupt latency on a real-time kernel. Handlers that genuinely must stay in hard-interrupt context, such as timer and IPI handlers, opt out explicitly. The practical consequence for a driver author is that a top half should be written to the strict atomic rules regardless, because it may run either way depending on how the kernel was configured and booted.

Where an interrupt is delivered is separately controllable. Each virq has a /proc/irq/N/smp_affinity mask that userspace—typically irqbalance—can write to steer the line to particular CPUs, and effective_affinity alongside it reports where the hardware is actually delivering, which can be a single CPU even when the requested mask is wide. A driver that knows the right placement, such as a multi-queue NIC pinning each queue's interrupt near the CPU that processes it, publishes that intent with irq_set_affinity_and_hint(); the older irq_set_affinity_hint() is marked deprecated because it conflated setting the affinity with merely hinting it (include/linux/interrupt.h).

Top Halves and Bottom Halves

An interrupt handler runs in a severely constrained context: with the line (and often more) masked, no ability to sleep, and other work on that CPU stalled. So it must be fast. Linux resolves the tension between "respond immediately" and "do substantial work" by splitting handling into a top half that runs in interrupt context and does only the urgent minimum—acknowledge the device, grab the data that would otherwise be lost—and a bottom half that runs later, with interrupts enabled, to do the heavier processing. Figure 13-4 shows the split and the three deferral mechanisms.

Linux offers several bottom-half mechanisms, differing mainly in whether they may sleep. Softirqs are a small, fixed set of high-frequency handlers (networking, block completion, timers, RCU, and the tasklet dispatcher) that run in atomic context, either on the return path from an interrupt or in the per-CPU ksoftirqd thread when the load is heavy (kernel/softirq.c). Tasklets are a simpler per-driver deferral built on the tasklet softirq; they too run atomically, and the kernel now marks them deprecated in favor of threaded IRQs. Workqueues run work in ordinary kernel threads and therefore may sleep, making them the right choice when the deferred work needs to allocate memory, take mutexes, or do more I/O (include/linux/workqueue.h). Table 13-14 compares them.

Threaded IRQ Dedicated kernel thread Yes The modern default bottom half; heavier per-interrupt work that may block

The modern default is the threaded IRQ. With request_threaded_irq(), the top-half handler runs in interrupt context and does the urgent acknowledgement, then returns IRQ_WAKE_THREAD; the kernel wakes the associated thread_fn, which runs in its own kernel thread where it may sleep. This gives a driver a clean two-stage handler—atomic acknowledgement plus sleepable processing—without the driver having to manage a separate tasklet or workqueue, which is why the tasklet API is being retired. Threaded handlers almost always use IRQF_ONESHOT so the line stays masked until the thread completes.

The reason a top half—and any softirq or tasklet—cannot sleep is that it does not run in the context of a schedulable task: there is no process to put to sleep and nothing sensible to switch to, so calling a blocking function (a mutex, a normal allocation, a copy_to_user()) would wedge the CPU. This drives the locking rules for state shared between a handler and the rest of a driver. Because the handler can fire in the middle of the driver's own code, that code must block the interrupt while it touches the shared state—spin_lock_irqsave() disables interrupts on the local CPU and takes a spinlock, so the handler cannot run and find the data half-updated. Sleeping locks are forbidden on either side; only spinlocks (and lockless primitives) bridge process context and interrupt context. Getting this boundary wrong is the classic source of driver deadlocks.

Those rules are a property of the kernel's configuration rather than a law of nature, and it is worth knowing how CONFIG_PREEMPT_RT changes them, because a driver may well be built both ways. On an RT kernel almost everything above moves into task context: interrupt handlers are threaded by default, softirqs run in task context rather than on the interrupt-return path, and spin_lock() becomes a sleeping lock built on rt_mutex, so spin_lock_irqsave() no longer disables interrupts at all—it takes a priority-inheriting mutex, and the "interrupt" it is protecting against is another thread. Code that is correct under the standard rules remains correct there, which is the design goal; what does not survive is code that relies on the side effects rather than the API, such as assuming a spinlock creates a preemption-free region in which per-CPU data is safe. Constructs that must remain truly atomic use raw_spinlock_t, which keeps the classic behavior on every configuration.

A word on tasklets, since the chapter calls them deprecated. They have not been removed in 7.0.12—tasklet_setup() is still exported and roughly 190 files under drivers/ still schedule one—but no new user should be added. The objections are that a tasklet runs in softirq context with no ability to sleep, offers no priority control, and is serialized against itself across all CPUs, so it combines the constraints of atomic context with none of the throughput of a real softirq. Nearly every historical use is better served by a threaded IRQ, and the remaining ones by a workqueue. Note also that the old tasklet_init() form, which passed an unsigned long data value, was converted tree-wide to tasklet_setup(), which passes the tasklet_struct itself and lets the callback recover its container with from_tasklet()—so older code will not match what is in the tree.

IRQ Domains and Chips

The interrupt number a driver passes to request_irq() is a Linux virtual IRQ (virq), not the raw hardware number. Real systems have many interrupt controllers—a GIC on ARM64, IO-APICs on x86_64, plus GPIO expanders and PCIe controllers that themselves generate interrupts—each with its own hardware numbering. The irq_domain (include/linux/irqdomain.h) is the translation layer that maps a controller-local hardware interrupt (hwirq) to a global Linux virq. When firmware describes an interrupt—interrupts = <...> in a device tree, or an ACPI resource—the core resolves it through the right domain (for example via irq_of_parse_and_map() or, for a platform driver, transparently inside platform_get_irq()).

Beneath the domain, each controller is represented by a struct irq_chip whose methods (irq_mask, irq_unmask, irq_ack, irq_eoi) the generic IRQ code calls to manipulate the line, so the core interrupt-flow logic is written once and is independent of the specific controller. Domains can be stacked into a hierarchy—a GPIO controller's domain sitting on top of the GIC's, for instance—which is how a single interrupt can pass through several controllers on its way to the CPU. This whole framework, largely absent from the 2.6 kernel, is what makes one ARM64 kernel able to support boards with wildly different interrupt topologies.

Concretely, when a mapping is created—irq_create_mapping() for a simple domain, irq_domain_alloc_irqs() for a hierarchical one—the core allocates a Linux virq and a struct irq_desc for it, and installs a flow handler appropriate to the line's type: handle_level_irq() for a level-triggered line (mask, run the handler, then unmask and EOI) or handle_edge_irq() for an edge-triggered one (ack the edge first so a new pulse during the handler is not lost). The flow handler calls the per-controller irq_chip methods, so the level/edge policy lives in generic code while the electrical manipulation stays in the chip driver. A GPIO example makes the hierarchy tangible: a sensor's interrupt line enters a GPIO controller, whose own domain maps that pin to a virq and whose parent is the SoC's GIC; when the pin fires, the GIC delivers to the GPIO controller's handler, which demultiplexes to the specific pin's virq and thence to the driver. Controller drivers themselves live in drivers/irqchip/ and register with IRQCHIP_DECLARE().

The domain-creation API has been modernized in a way that mirrors the firmware-agnostic theme of the enumeration section. The old irq_domain_add_* family took a struct device_node and was therefore device-tree-only; it has been replaced by irq_domain_create_*, which takes a struct fwnode_handle and so works equally for an ACPI-described controller. In 7.0.12 those create forms are themselves thin inline wrappers around a single constructor, irq_domain_instantiate(), which takes a descriptor structure rather than a long argument list, with devm_irq_domain_instantiate() as the managed variant (include/linux/irqdomain.h). The irq_domain_ops a controller supplies also come in two generations: .map and .xlate for a simple flat domain, and .alloc, .free, and .translate for a hierarchical one, where allocating a virq must propagate up through every parent domain in turn.

Because this layering is hard to reason about from source alone, the kernel can export it. With CONFIG_GENERIC_IRQ_DEBUGFS, /sys/kernel/debug/irq/ gains a domains/ directory listing every registered domain with its parent and mapping count, and a per-virq file that prints the whole chain—each irq_chip the interrupt traverses, the hwirq number at each level, the flow handler in use, and the effective affinity. It is considerably more informative than /proc/interrupts when an interrupt is misrouted rather than merely absent, and the same option enables interrupt injection, which lets a handler be exercised without the hardware.

MSI and MSI-X

Traditional interrupts are signaled on dedicated wires, which are scarce and force sharing. Modern PCIe devices instead use message-signaled interrupts (MSI, and the extended MSI-X): to raise an interrupt, the device performs a memory write to a special address, and the interrupt controller turns that write into a CPU interrupt. MSI-X lets a single device own many independent interrupt vectors—up to 2048, one per queue—so a multi-queue NIC or NVMe drive can steer each queue's completions to a different CPU without any sharing.

Message signaling also fixes a subtle correctness problem of line-based interrupts. On PCIe, a device's DMA writes and its MSI write travel the same ordered, posted-write path to memory, so the interrupt message cannot overtake the data—by the time the CPU sees the MSI, the DMA buffer is guaranteed to be in memory. A legacy wire interrupt is a separate signal that can race ahead of the still-in-flight DMA, forcing the driver to perform a dummy register read to flush the write path before trusting the data. MSI removes that race and the workaround with it.

A driver requests MSI(-X) vectors with pci_alloc_irq_vectors() (drivers/pci/msi/), which allocates the best available interrupt type (MSI-X, then MSI, then a legacy line) and returns the count; the driver then obtains each Linux IRQ number with pci_irq_vector() and calls request_irq() as usual. Passing the PCI_IRQ_AFFINITY flag asks the kernel to spread the vectors across CPUs automatically, which is how a multi-queue device gets one interrupt per CPU with no manual affinity setup. The older pci_enable_msi() and pci_enable_msix_range() entry points still exist but are considered legacy, and non-PCI devices reach the same machinery through the platform-MSI framework. This ordered, per-CPU interrupt delivery is an important property for the DMA discussion that follows.

Two details of that API matter when reading current code. The type flags are PCI_IRQ_MSIX, PCI_IRQ_MSI, and PCI_IRQ_INTX—the last having been renamed from PCI_IRQ_LEGACY, since "legacy" was ambiguous and INTx is the name the specification uses for pin-based signaling (include/linux/pci.h); PCI_IRQ_ALL_TYPES is the union of the three. And when a device needs its vectors split into groups with different placement rules, pci_alloc_irq_vectors_affinity() takes a descriptor that reserves vectors before and after the automatically spread set. NVMe is the canonical user: the admin queue's vector is excluded from the spread, and the remaining I/O-queue vectors are distributed one per CPU, so each CPU's submissions complete on the CPU that issued them.

Underneath, MSI has been restructured since the 2.6 era and even since the early 5.x kernels. Rather than one global MSI domain per controller, 7.0.12 gives each device its own MSI domain created on demand from a parent domain that advertises what it can provide through struct msi_parent_ops (include/linux/msi.h). The practical benefits are that per-device interrupt descriptors are allocated only for devices that use them, that a device can host several distinct MSI domains at once, and that non-PCI interrupt sources—platform devices, and the device-managed interrupt stores appearing on newer accelerators—plug into exactly the same hierarchy as PCIe MSI-X. A driver author sees none of this; it shows up as the several stacked domains that /sys/kernel/debug/irq/domains/ reports between a PCIe function's vector and the CPU.

Direct Memory Access

Interrupts tell the CPU when a device is ready, but for bulk data having the CPU copy every byte to and from device registers would waste enormous effort. Direct Memory Access (DMA) lets a device read and write system memory on its own, so a disk or NIC moves whole buffers while the CPU does other work and is interrupted only when the transfer completes. The difficulty is that a device does not see memory the way the CPU does: it uses bus addresses, it may be unable to reach all of physical memory, and an IOMMU or CPU cache may sit in between. The kernel's DMA mapping API exists to hide all of that behind one portable interface, and Figure 13-5 shows the three paths it hides.

This is one of the areas where 2.6-era driver code no longer even compiles. The pci_map_single(), pci_alloc_consistent() family that 2.6-era drivers used was a thin PCI-flavored alias for the generic calls; it was deprecated for years and finally deleted, so today there is exactly one spelling—dma_* taking a struct device *—for every bus. The older habit of computing a bus address arithmetically with virt_to_bus() is gone for the same reason: on a system with an IOMMU, an address offset, or memory encryption, there is no arithmetic that produces the right answer, and only the mapping call knows what the platform needs. The rule to carry through this section is that a dma_addr_t is produced, never computed.

The DMA Mapping API

A CPU pointer is a kernel virtual address; a device needs a dma_addr_t, the bus address it should actually drive. Producing the right dma_addr_t for a buffer—and, where necessary, programming an IOMMU or copying through a bounce buffer—is what the mapping API does. Because a device may not be able to address all of memory, a driver first declares its reach with dma_set_mask_and_coherent(), passing a mask such as DMA_BIT_MASK(64) or DMA_BIT_MASK(32); the kernel uses that limit to decide whether a buffer is directly reachable or must be bounced (include/linux/dma-mapping.h).

The API deliberately abstracts over three very different underlying mechanisms, so a driver written once works on all of them: a direct mapping when the device can reach memory as-is; a bounce buffer (SWIOTLB) that copies through a low, reachable staging area when it cannot; and an IOMMU mapping when one is present. The dispatch happens inside kernel/dma/mapping.c, which routes each call to the direct-mapping code, the IOMMU code, or a per-device dma_map_ops, invisibly to the driver.

Two rules trip up newcomers. First, a dma_addr_t is not a CPU pointer—it is only meaningful to the device, and dereferencing it in the kernel is a bug; the CPU keeps using its own virtual pointer to the same buffer, while the device uses the dma_addr_t. Second, only memory that is physically contiguous and cache-line aligned can be DMA'd, so a driver must map memory from kmalloc() (or a page), never a stack variable or a vmalloc() region. Beyond the mask, a device's reach can be further constrained by firmware: a device-tree dma-ranges property describes the window and any address offset between a device's view and physical memory, which the DMA API applies automatically. As with other resources, managed forms exist: the plain dma_alloc_coherent() must be released by hand with dma_free_coherent(), whereas its managed twin dmam_alloc_coherent() (the DMA analogue of devm_kzalloc(), sharing the dmam_ "managed DMA" prefix) frees the buffer automatically when the device unbinds. 

/* Unmanaged: the driver owns the lifetime and must free it itself */
cpu = dma_alloc_coherent(dev, size, &handle, GFP_KERNEL);
...
dma_free_coherent(dev, size, cpu, handle);      /* required, incl. on error paths */

/* Managed: the driver core frees it when the device unbinds */
cpu = dmam_alloc_coherent(dev, size, &handle, GFP_KERNEL);
/* no dma_free_coherent() needed — released automatically on unbind */
  • dma_alloc_coherent() — the unmanaged form. The driver owns the buffer's lifetime and must release it explicitly with dma_free_coherent() on every success and error path. Use it only when the buffer's lifetime is not tied to the device (for example, freed and reallocated at runtime).
  • dmam_alloc_coherent() — the managed form (the dmam_ "managed DMA" family, parallel to devm_kzalloc()). It registers a devres entry so the driver core frees the buffer automatically when the device unbinds, needing no dma_free_coherent() and avoiding leak-on-error bugs. Prefer it in ordinary drivers.

The one rule that no driver may skip is checking that the map actually succeeded. A mapping can fail—the IOMMU address space can be exhausted, the SWIOTLB pool can be full—and the failure is reported not as NULL and not as an ERR_PTR, but as the sentinel DMA_MAPPING_ERROR, defined as (~(dma_addr_t)0), which drivers must test for through dma_mapping_error() rather than comparing by hand (include/linux/dma-mapping.h). Because the sentinel is all-ones rather than zero, an unchecked failure is not a null-pointer dereference that stops immediately; it is a perfectly plausible-looking address programmed into a device, which then writes to it. The list-mapping calls report failure differently again, and inconsistently enough to be worth memorizing: dma_map_sg() returns 0 on failure and a positive segment count on success, whereas the newer dma_map_sgtable() returns 0 on success and a negative errno on failure. Mask setting is fallible too—dma_set_mask_and_coherent() returns an error if the platform cannot satisfy the request—and the conventional response is to retry with a narrower mask before giving up.

The prohibition on mapping vmalloc() memory is enforced rather than merely documented: dma_map_single_attrs() tests the pointer with is_vmalloc_addr() and, if it hits, fires a dev_WARN_ONCE() reading "rejecting DMA map of vmalloc memory" and returns DMA_MAPPING_ERROR. The reason is structural: dma_map_single() resolves the buffer with virt_to_page(), which is only meaningful for the linear map, and a vmalloc region's virtual contiguity hides physical scattering that the device would never see. A vmalloc'd buffer can still be DMA'd, but only by walking it into a scatterlist page by page and mapping that instead. Stack buffers are rejected for a related reason—the stack may be vmalloc-backed under CONFIG_VMAP_STACK, and in any case a DMA transfer must not target memory whose lifetime ends at a function return. 

dma_addr_t handle;

handle = dma_map_single(dev, buf, len, DMA_TO_DEVICE);
if (dma_mapping_error(dev, handle))     /* never compare against NULL */
        return -ENOMEM;
...
dma_unmap_single(dev, handle, len, DMA_TO_DEVICE);

/* Mask negotiation: ask for 64-bit, fall back to 32-bit */
if (dma_set_mask_and_coherent(dev, DMA_BIT_MASK(64)))
        if (dma_set_mask_and_coherent(dev, DMA_BIT_MASK(32)))
                return -EIO;

Every mapping and allocation call has an _attrs variant taking a flags word that tunes what the platform does underneath, and a driver that seems to be fighting the API is often just missing one of them. The most commonly seen are DMA_ATTR_SKIP_CPU_SYNC, which suppresses the cache maintenance when the caller knows the data is already in the right state—page-pool network drivers use it to remap a recycled page without re-flushing it; DMA_ATTR_NO_KERNEL_MAPPING, which tells the allocator that only the device will ever touch the buffer so no kernel virtual mapping need be established at all; DMA_ATTR_WRITE_COMBINE for framebuffer-like memory where write buffering is wanted; DMA_ATTR_FORCE_CONTIGUOUS, which forces a large allocation to come from the contiguous memory allocator described in the device-tree section rather than being scattered; and DMA_ATTR_NO_WARN for callers that will handle failure themselves. Linux 7.0.12 adds DMA_ATTR_MMIO, which marks a mapping whose target is device MMIO rather than normal RAM—the peer-to-peer case discussed below—and therefore must never be cached or cache-flushed.

Coherent allocations are also coarse: the minimum unit may be a whole page, which is wasteful for drivers that need hundreds of small descriptors. The DMA pool API (include/linux/dmapool.h) exists for exactly this. A pool is created with dma_pool_create()—or its managed twin dmam_pool_create()—naming an object size, an alignment, and a boundary the allocator must not let an object straddle, which is how hardware constraints such as "this queue head may not cross a 4 KB page boundary" are expressed. Objects then come from dma_pool_alloc() (or dma_pool_zalloc()) and return both a CPU pointer and a dma_addr_t, exactly like the coherent allocator, but carved out of pages the pool already owns. It is, in effect, a slab cache backed by coherent memory, and USB host controllers and NVMe are its heaviest users.

Between fully coherent and fully streaming sits a family added for large buffers whose coherence cost matters: dma_alloc_pages() and its wrapper dma_alloc_noncoherent() allocate DMA-reachable pages with a normal cached kernel mapping and leave the driver responsible for the dma_sync_* calls, while dma_alloc_noncontiguous() returns an sg_table for a buffer that need only be contiguous in device address space, which an IOMMU can arrange from scattered pages. These matter most on non-coherent ARM64 SoCs, where the "coherent" allocator must hand back an uncached mapping, and where a driver that reads a large buffer with the CPU is far better served by cached memory plus explicit syncs.

Coherent versus Streaming Mappings

The API offers two kinds of mapping for two different lifetimes. Coherent (or consistent) mappings, from dma_alloc_coherent(), allocate a buffer that both the CPU and the device can access at any time without explicit cache management; the kernel guarantees the two views stay coherent. These are for long-lived, frequently-touched structures such as a device's descriptor rings. The call returns both a CPU pointer to use and a dma_addr_t to program into the device. 

/* Coherent: long-lived, CPU and device share it without sync calls */
void *cpu_ring = dma_alloc_coherent(dev, size, &dma_ring, GFP_KERNEL);
...
dma_free_coherent(dev, size, cpu_ring, dma_ring);

Streaming mappings, from dma_map_single() or dma_map_page(), map an existing buffer for a single transfer and unmap it when done. They are cheaper and are the right choice for the payload buffers of individual I/O operations—the page being written to disk, the packet being sent. A streaming map takes a direction (enum dma_data_direction): DMA_TO_DEVICE, DMA_FROM_DEVICE, or DMA_BIDIRECTIONAL. The direction is not advisory—on a non-cache-coherent system it tells the kernel which cache flushes or invalidations to perform—so getting it wrong silently corrupts data. Ownership is strict: between dma_map_single() and dma_unmap_single() the buffer belongs to the device, and the CPU must not touch it unless it first calls dma_sync_single_for_cpu() (and dma_sync_single_for_device() before handing it back). The reason the two exist is the interaction of caching with sharing. A descriptor ring is touched constantly by both sides—the CPU writes new descriptors while the device reads completed ones—so paying for cache management on every access would be ruinous; a coherent mapping instead makes the memory cache-coherent (or uncached) once, so neither side ever needs an explicit flush. A payload buffer, by contrast, is handed to the device, filled or drained once, and handed back, so the cheaper streaming path fits: on a non-cache-coherent system (many ARM64 SoCs), mapping for DMA_TO_DEVICE cleans the CPU cache so the device sees current data, and unmapping for DMA_FROM_DEVICE invalidates it so the CPU does not read stale cached bytes over what the device just wrote. That cache dance is exactly what the ownership handoff enforces, and it is why touching a streaming-mapped buffer while the device owns it corrupts data on precisely the platforms hardest to debug on.

"Coherent" also promises less than the name suggests, in two ways worth knowing before debugging a ring that mysteriously stalls. First, it is not free: on a non-coherent platform the kernel delivers coherence by handing back an uncached (or write-combining) mapping, so every CPU read of that buffer goes to DRAM. That is a fine trade for a descriptor ring the CPU touches a few words at a time and a poor one for bulk data, which is exactly why the dma_alloc_noncoherent() family exists. Second, coherence is not ordering. A coherent mapping guarantees the device will eventually see what the CPU wrote, not that it will see the writes in the order they were issued, so the descriptor stores must be separated from the doorbell write by dma_wmb()—the DMA-specific write barrier—or the device may fetch a descriptor it has been told is ready but which is not yet fully written. Nearly every ring-based driver has this barrier immediately before its doorbell store.

The sync calls have a fast path worth knowing about, because it changes how they should be written. On a cache-coherent system the whole dma_sync_* family compiles down to nothing, and under CONFIG_DMA_NEED_SYNC the kernel further tracks per-device whether any sync is ever required, exposing the answer through dma_need_sync() (include/linux/dma-mapping.h). High-rate drivers use it to skip the bookkeeping around the call rather than the call itself. There is also a partial form, dma_sync_single_range_for_cpu(), for handing back only the portion of a large mapping that was actually written—the common case for a network receive buffer where the packet is much smaller than the page.

Scatter/Gather and the IOMMU

Real I/O buffers are rarely one physically-contiguous chunk; a user-space read spans many scattered pages. Rather than force a copy into a contiguous buffer, DMA-capable devices support scatter/gather: the driver hands the device a list of memory segments and the device walks it. Linux represents such a list as an array of struct scatterlist (include/linux/scatterlist.h), each entry naming a page, offset, and length, and maps the whole list in one call with dma_map_sg(). The mapping fills in each entry's dma_address—and, importantly, an IOMMU may coalesce physically scattered segments into one contiguous device-address range, so the device sees a simpler list than physical memory actually provides.

The segments are collected in a struct sg_table, which is where the coalescing shows up concretely. It carries two counts: orig_nents, the number of segments the driver put in, and nents, the number the device actually sees after dma_map_sg()—which is smaller when an IOMMU merged adjacent segments. The two counts drive two different loops: iterate the CPU pages with for_each_sg() over orig_nents, but program the device from the mapped ranges with for_each_sgtable_dma_sg() over nents. Mixing these up is a classic bug. The modern one-call helper dma_map_sgtable() maps a whole sg_table and reports errors cleanly, and it is preferred over the raw dma_map_sg() in new code. 

struct sg_table sgt;

sg_alloc_table(&sgt, npages, GFP_KERNEL);     /* orig_nents = npages */
/* ... fill each entry with sg_set_page(sg, page, len, offset) ... */

if (dma_map_sgtable(dev, &sgt, DMA_TO_DEVICE, 0))
        return -EIO;                          /* sgt.nents now set (<= orig_nents) */

/* Program the device from the *mapped* ranges */
struct scatterlist *sg;
int i;
for_each_sgtable_dma_sg(&sgt, sg, i)
        push_descriptor(dev, sg_dma_address(sg), sg_dma_len(sg));

/* ... transfer ... then release */
dma_unmap_sgtable(dev, &sgt, DMA_TO_DEVICE, 0);
sg_free_table(&sgt);

Building a scatterlist is not unconstrained, because hardware imposes limits the driver must publish and upper layers must honor. A device declares the largest single segment its engine can describe with dma_set_max_seg_size()—the default, if it says nothing, is a mere 64 KB—and an address boundary its segments must not straddle with dma_set_seg_boundary(). The block layer reads these back through dma_get_max_seg_size() when it decides how much it may merge into one request, which is why a driver that forgets to raise the default sees its transfers silently chopped into small pieces. A separate limit applies to the total: dma_max_mapping_size() reports the largest buffer that can be mapped at once, which is unbounded with an IOMMU but capped by the bounce-buffer slab when SWIOTLB is in play, while dma_opt_mapping_size() reports the largest size that is still efficient. Storage drivers query both when computing the maximum transfer size they advertise.

Not every DMA target is system memory. A transfer can also be aimed at another device's MMIO—a NIC writing straight into an NVMe controller's buffer, or a GPU's memory—and for that the API provides dma_map_resource(), which takes a phys_addr_t for a region that is not RAM and returns a dma_addr_t without ever touching a cache or a struct page. For PCI, the peer-to-peer DMA layer (include/linux/pci-p2pdma.h) decides whether such a transfer is possible at all, classifying each pair as PCI_P2PDMA_MAP_BUS_ADDR when the two devices sit under a switch and can talk without involving the host bridge, PCI_P2PDMA_MAP_THRU_HOST_BRIDGE when the traffic must traverse the root complex, or PCI_P2PDMA_MAP_NOT_SUPPORTED when the topology cannot carry it. The distinction matters because the two cases need different addresses, and because many root complexes route peer traffic badly or not at all.

An IOMMU (Intel VT-d, AMD-Vi, or an ARM SMMU) is to device addresses what the MMU is to CPU addresses: it translates the I/O virtual addresses (IOVAs) a device emits into physical addresses, through per-device page tables. This buys two things. It lets a 32-bit-limited device reach buffers anywhere in physical memory by mapping them below its limit, avoiding bounce-buffer copies; and, more importantly, it isolates devices—a device can only touch memory that has been explicitly mapped for it, so a malfunctioning or malicious device (including a hot-plugged Thunderbolt peripheral) cannot scribble over arbitrary RAM. The DMA API uses the IOMMU transparently: the same dma_map_sg() call that returns raw physical addresses on a simple system returns IOMMU-translated addresses on one with an IOMMU, and the driver code is identical.

Two IOMMU concepts surface when reading kernel logs or tuning a system. An IOMMU group is the smallest set of devices the IOMMU can isolate from one another—often a single function, but sometimes several that share translation because the topology cannot separate them—and it is the unit that must be assigned as a whole to a VM. A domain is a set of translations (a page-table context); a device is attached to a IOMMU_DOMAIN_DMA domain for normal kernel DMA, or an IOMMU_DOMAIN_IDENTITY (pass-through) domain where IOVA equals physical address and translation is effectively off. Two boot parameters tune the trade-off: iommu.passthrough=1 puts kernel drivers in identity mode for maximum performance (no protection), and iommu.strict=0 enables lazy unmapping, which batches the expensive IOMMU TLB flushes at the cost of a short window where a stale mapping remains valid.

Groups are visible at runtime under /sys/kernel/iommu_groups/, where each numbered directory lists the devices it contains and carries a type attribute that reports—and, with the group's drivers unbound, can change—whether the group's default domain is DMA, DMA-FQ (lazy invalidation), or identity. That file is the quickest way to answer "is this device actually translated?" when throughput is unexpectedly low or a device is unexpectedly working despite a too-small DMA mask. A related boot parameter, iommu.forcedac=1, stops the allocator from preferring 32-bit IOVAs, which exposes drivers that quietly depend on getting low addresses.

This isolation is also what makes safe device passthrough possible. VFIO hands a whole physical device to a virtual machine by attaching the device's IOMMU group to a domain the guest controls, so the guest-driven device can only reach memory the host mapped for it—the IOMMU is the hardware that contains an untrusted driver. When no IOMMU is present at all, the SWIOTLB bounce buffer from Figure 13-5 is the fallback that lets an address-limited device still reach high memory, at the cost of a copy.

In Linux 7.0.12 the userspace side of this has a second, newer interface. iommufd (drivers/iommu/iommufd/) exposes IOMMU address spaces to userspace directly as file descriptors, so a VMM builds and populates an I/O address space explicitly and binds device file descriptors to it, rather than working through VFIO's older container-and-group model; CONFIG_IOMMUFD_VFIO_CONTAINER even lets iommufd provide the legacy /dev/vfio/vfio interface for compatibility. The underlying guarantee is unchanged—a passed-through device reaches only what has been mapped for it—but the newer interface handles nested translation and large address spaces far better, which is what modern virtualization needs.

SWIOTLB, meanwhile, has moved from a legacy corner case to a mainstream path, and for a reason that has nothing to do with address width. In a confidential-computing guest—AMD SEV-SNP or Intel TDX—the guest's memory is encrypted and the hypervisor cannot read it, so an emulated or paravirtual device cannot DMA to an ordinary guest page at all. The x86 DMA setup therefore checks cc_platform_has(CC_ATTR_GUEST_MEM_ENCRYPT) and, if it holds, turns on SWIOTLB with SWIOTLB_FORCE (arch/x86/kernel/pci-dma.c): every transfer bounces through a pool of pages that have been explicitly shared with the host. That is why the bounce buffer has recently gained multiple independently-locked areas and the ability to grow on demand; it went from a rarely-taken fallback to the hot path of an entire class of deployments. The same machinery serves a very different purpose on embedded ARM64, where a restricted-dma-pool node in the device tree (kernel/dma/swiotlb.c) confines an untrusted device's DMA to one reserved memory window—an IOMMU's isolation property, obtained by copying, on a SoC that has no IOMMU.

The dmaengine Subsystem

The DMA discussed so far is bus-master DMA, where the device itself contains the DMA engine and drives the bus. Many SoCs instead provide one or more shared, general-purpose DMA controllers that peripherals without their own engine can borrow—for example to feed a simple UART or I2S audio block. The dmaengine subsystem (include/linux/dmaengine.h) is the framework for these. A driver requests a channel with dma_request_chan(), describes a transfer (commonly a scatter/gather slave transfer) with dmaengine_prep_slave_sg(), submits it with dmaengine_submit(), and starts it with dma_async_issue_pending(); a callback fires on completion.

The submission model is worth spelling out because it is asynchronous. A prep call returns a dma_async_tx_descriptor; dmaengine_submit() attaches a completion callback and returns a cookie; and dma_async_issue_pending() actually launches queued transfers. The driver can later query progress with the cookie via dmaengine_tx_status(). Before the first transfer, a peripheral driver configures the channel with dmaengine_slave_config(), supplying its register's DMA address, the transfer width, and the burst size so the engine knows how to feed the device. One capability deserves special mention: DMA_CYCLIC transfers loop over a ring of buffers indefinitely and fire the callback at each period boundary, which is exactly what audio and other continuous streams need.

Where the channel comes from is firmware description again. A peripheral names its channels in the device tree with the dmas and dma-names properties, and dma_request_chan(dev, "tx") resolves that name through the controller's of_dma_xlate() callback into a specific channel of a specific engine; on ACPI systems the same call goes through the CSRT table instead. The older dma_request_slave_channel() is a deprecated wrapper that swallows the error code, so new drivers use dma_request_chan()—which returns an ERR_PTR, and may well return -EPROBE_DEFER because the DMA controller has not probed yet—or devm_dma_request_chan() to have the channel released automatically at unbind.

Stopping a transfer needs the same care as freeing an interrupt, and for the same reason. dmaengine_terminate_async() asks the engine to stop but does not wait, and may be called from atomic context; dmaengine_terminate_sync() additionally waits for any in-flight completion callback to finish, so it may sleep and must not be called from the callback itself. The correct teardown for a driver whose callback runs in tasklet context is therefore to terminate asynchronously, then call dmaengine_synchronize() from a sleepable context before freeing the buffers the callback might touch. Progress reporting has an analogous subtlety: dmaengine_tx_status() fills in a residue, but how meaningful it is depends on the engine's residue_granularity, reported by dma_get_slave_caps() as descriptor, segment, or burst. Audio drivers need burst granularity to report an accurate playback pointer, and an engine that only updates per descriptor cannot support them well. For bringing up a new controller driver, the dmatest client (drivers/dma/dmatest.c) exercises channels from debugfs without needing a real peripheral at all.

The distinction matters when reading driver code: a device with bus-master DMA uses the dma_map_* API directly against its own engine, while a peripheral behind a shared controller uses dmaengine to program a third-party engine on its behalf. Both ultimately move data without CPU copying; they differ only in which hardware block does the moving. Together with the mapping API and the IOMMU, dmaengine completes the modern DMA picture—one that is far more portable and far safer than the arch-specific, physically-addressed DMA of the 2.6 era.

SoC Resources and Firmware Loading

Registers, an interrupt, and DMA are all a driver needs on a PC, where firmware has powered and clocked every device before the kernel starts. On an ARM64 system-on-chip that assumption fails. A UART on an SoC is dead silicon until something ungates its clock, switches on the regulator feeding its power rail, routes its signals to the right package pins through the pin controller, releases it from reset, and powers up the power domain it sits in. Each of those resources belongs to another device—a clock controller, a power management IC (PMIC), a pin controller, a reset block—with its own driver. Many devices also hold their operating code in RAM rather than flash, so the driver must load a firmware image into them before they do anything at all.

Linux 2.6.11 had no common framework for any of this: board files and machine-specific code switched clocks and drove GPIO lines through per-platform interfaces, and GPIOs were integers in one global number space. Each framework this section describes arrived later—regulators in Linux 2.6.27, generic PM domains in 3.1, pin control in 3.2, the common clock framework in 3.4, reset controllers in 3.10, and GPIO descriptors in 3.13—and all of them follow one pattern. A provider driver registers the resource; the firmware description links each consumer to its provider with a phandle; and the consumer driver asks for the resource by name in probe(). This section covers the consumer side, which is what almost every peripheral driver writes, and ends with the firmware loader.

The Consumer Model

The device-tree node of a peripheral names its suppliers alongside its reg and interrupts. Each framework has its own property: clocks with clock-names, a name-supply property per power rail, pinctrl-N with pinctrl-names, a name-gpios property per GPIO line, resets with reset-names, and power-domains: 

uart0: serial@feb30000 {
        compatible = "vendor,my-uart";
        reg = <0xfeb30000 0x100>;
        interrupts = <GIC_SPI 331 IRQ_TYPE_LEVEL_HIGH>;
        clocks = <&cru 12>, <&cru 13>;
        clock-names = "baud", "apb";
        vdd-supply = <&ldo3>;
        pinctrl-names = "default", "sleep";
        pinctrl-0 = <&uart0_pins>;
        pinctrl-1 = <&uart0_sleep_pins>;
        reset-gpios = <&gpio1 5 GPIO_ACTIVE_LOW>;
        resets = <&rst 7>;
        power-domains = <&pd 2>;
};

The names in the node are the consumer's vocabulary: a driver asks for the "apb" clock, the "vdd" supply, or the "reset" GPIO, and the framework finds the property—appending the -supply suffix for regulators, and trying -gpios and then the older -gpio for GPIOs (gpio_suffixes[] in drivers/gpio/gpiolib.c). The names are what keep a driver independent of the board: a different SoC can feed the same UART from a different clock controller without a line of driver code changing.

Three rules hold across every framework. First, a getter returns an ERR_PTR, and one of the errors it can return is -EPROBE_DEFER, meaning the provider exists in the device tree but has not probed. fw_devlink, described earlier with device links, usually prevents that from happening: its supplier table in drivers/of/property.c knows clocks, -supply, pinctrl-N, -gpios, resets, and power-domains, so the consumer is not probed until those providers have bound. When it does happen, the driver propagates the error with dev_err_probe(). Second, each getter has an _optional variant that returns NULL rather than an error when the property is absent, and every operation on a NULL handle is a no-op, so one driver body serves boards that wire the resource and boards that do not. Regulators are the exception that proves the rule: a plain regulator_get() for a supply the device tree does not describe returns a dummy regulator and logs "supply vdd not found, using dummy regulator", on the theory that an undescribed rail is simply always on, while regulator_get_optional() returns -ENODEV for drivers that must know. Third, every getter has a managed devm_ form, and the most common ones also come in a get-and-enable form— devm_clk_get_enabled(), devm_regulator_get_enable(), devm_reset_control_get_exclusive_deasserted()—that turns the resource on at probe and off at unbind in reverse order. Bulk forms (devm_clk_bulk_get_all_enabled(), devm_regulator_bulk_get_enable(), devm_reset_control_bulk_get_exclusive()) claim a whole set in one call.

A typical bring-up sequence shows the order these calls take—power first, then clocks, then release from reset, and only then the first register access: 

static int my_probe(struct platform_device *pdev)
{
        struct device *dev = &pdev->dev;
        struct gpio_desc *rst;
        struct clk *clk;
        int ret;

        ret = devm_regulator_get_enable(dev, "vdd");          /* vdd-supply */
        if (ret)
                return dev_err_probe(dev, ret, "vdd supply\n");

        clk = devm_clk_get_enabled(dev, "apb");               /* clock-names */
        if (IS_ERR(clk))
                return dev_err_probe(dev, PTR_ERR(clk), "apb clock\n");

        rst = devm_gpiod_get_optional(dev, "reset", GPIOD_OUT_HIGH); /* held in reset */
        if (IS_ERR(rst))
                return PTR_ERR(rst);
        fsleep(10);
        gpiod_set_value_cansleep(rst, 0);                     /* release reset */

        /* registers are now safe to touch */
        ...
}

Two of the six resources need no call at all, because the driver core claims them before probe() runs. really_probe() calls pinctrl_bind_pins() (drivers/base/pinctrl.c), which selects the device's "default" pin state—or its "init" state, switching to "default" once probe succeeds—and records the optional "sleep" and "idle" states for later. The platform bus's platform_probe() then applies any assigned-clocks and assigned-clock-rates properties with of_clk_set_defaults() and attaches the device to its power domain with dev_pm_domain_attach(), powering the domain on. A missing pin controller or power-domain provider therefore defers the probe before the driver's own code is ever reached.

The frameworks also share a boot-time clean-up that surprises driver writers. A bootloader often leaves clocks, rails, and domains running so that it can show a splash screen or talk on a console. At late_initcall_sync time the kernel turns off whatever no consumer has claimed: clk_disable_unused() gates unclaimed clocks, genpd_power_off_unused() powers down idle domains, and the regulator core schedules the same sweep 30 seconds later, to give modules loaded from userspace time to claim their supplies (regulator_init_complete()). A driver that forgets to enable a clock it depends on therefore works until late boot and then stops, and the boot parameters clk_ignore_unused, pd_ignore_unused, and regulator_ignore_unused are the standard way to confirm that diagnosis. Providers that implement the sync_state() callback described earlier go further and keep their resources on until every consumer the device tree names has probed. Clocks, regulators, and power domains are mostly absent there, because ACPI firmware powers devices itself through _PS0/_PS3 methods and power resources; drivers use the _optional getters for them, and dev_pm_domain_attach() tries the ACPI PM domain (acpi_dev_pm_attach()) before the generic one. Clocks and Regulators

The common clock framework (drivers/clk/) models an SoC's clock tree—oscillators, PLLs, multiplexers, dividers, and gates—as a tree of struct clk_core nodes, each with a parent, a rate, and prepare_count/enable_count reference counts. A consumer never sees a clk_core. Every clk_get() returns a fresh struct clk handle (drivers/clk/clk.c) that records which device and which name asked, plus that consumer's own min_rate/max_rate request, so the framework can combine the constraints of several consumers that share one clock.

Turning a clock on is split into two steps because the hardware is. clk_prepare() does the slow part—waiting for a PLL to lock, or talking to a clock chip over I2C—and may sleep; it runs under the global prepare_lock mutex. clk_enable() only flips a gate bit, never sleeps, and runs under the enable_lock spinlock, so it may be called from an interrupt handler. A clock must be prepared before it is enabled, and in process context drivers simply call the inline clk_prepare_enable() from include/linux/clk.h, which does both and unwinds the first if the second fails. Both steps propagate up the tree: enabling a leaf gate prepares and enables its parent PLL and oscillator first, and a parent turns off only when the last child's count drops to zero. Rates work the same way. clk_round_rate() asks what the tree can actually produce, clk_set_rate() requests it, and the change may reprogram a shared parent and so move a sibling's rate as well; clk_set_rate_exclusive() locks the rate against other consumers, and clk_notifier_register() lets a driver react before and after a parent changes underneath it.

On the provider side a clock driver describes each node with a struct clk_hw and a struct clk_ops table (prepare, enable, set_rate, recalc_rate, set_parent, and so on), registers it with devm_clk_hw_register(), and exposes the set to the device tree with devm_of_clk_add_hw_provider(). Most SoC clock drivers are built almost entirely from the framework's generic gate, divider, and mux types. The whole tree, with every node's prepare count, enable count, and rate, is printed by /sys/kernel/debug/clk/clk_summary, which is usually the first file to read when a peripheral is silent. x86_64 uses the framework only in a few corners (drivers/clk/x86/ covers Intel LPSS and AMD FCH clocks); a PC's clocks are otherwise firmware's business.

The regulator framework (drivers/regulator/) has the same shape: a struct regulator consumer handle per regulator_get(), over one struct regulator_dev per physical rail. regulator_enable() and regulator_disable() are reference-counted, so a rail shared by a sensor and a codec stays on while either needs it. Voltage requests are ranges rather than values: regulator_set_voltage(reg, min_uV, max_uV) records the consumer's range, and the core picks a voltage inside the intersection of every consumer's range (regulator_check_consumers()), clipped to the board's own limits from the device tree (regulator-min-microvolt, regulator-max-microvolt). A consumer can never push a rail beyond those board constraints, which is the framework's main safety property—only the device tree can say what a board's hardware tolerates. regulator_set_load() lets the core pick an efficient operating mode for the expected current, and regulator_register_notifier() delivers events such as REGULATOR_EVENT_OVER_CURRENT. The provider marks rails that must never turn off with regulator-always-on, and /sys/kernel/debug/regulator/regulator_summary shows every rail with its consumers, use counts, and requested voltages.

The two frameworks meet in power management. A driver's runtime_suspend callback typically calls clk_disable_unprepare() and, where the rail is private to the device, regulator_disable(); runtime_resume reverses both. The get-and-enable helpers suit only resources that stay on for the life of the binding. A device that gates its clock at runtime keeps the handle from devm_clk_get() and turns it on and off itself.

Pins, GPIOs, and Reset Lines

An SoC has far more internal functions than package pins, so each pad is multiplexed: the same ball may be a UART transmit line, an I2C clock, or a plain GPIO, and it also has electrical settings such as pull-up, drive strength, and slew rate. The pin control subsystem (drivers/pinctrl/) owns that configuration. The board's device tree defines named groups of pin settings under the pin controller node, and each consumer lists the groups for each of its states through pinctrl-N and pinctrl-names. As described above, the driver core selects "default" before probe(); a driver only touches pin control when it changes state, calling pinctrl_pm_select_sleep_state() in its suspend path and pinctrl_pm_select_default_state() on resume (or the "idle" state around runtime PM) so that idle pads stop leaking current. A driver that needs a state of its own—say, a bus-recovery mode that turns I2C pins into GPIOs—calls devm_pinctrl_get(), pinctrl_lookup_state(), and pinctrl_select_state() directly. The files under /sys/kernel/debug/pinctrl/, notably pinmux-pins, show which device owns each pad.

When a pad is a GPIO, a driver claims it through the GPIO descriptor interface in include/linux/gpio/consumer.h. The older interface, in which a driver passed a global integer to gpio_request(), is confined to include/linux/gpio.h, which now begins with the warning that it "must not be included" by new code and is built only with CONFIG_GPIOLIB_LEGACY. A descriptor is obtained by function name and claimed in a known state in one call: devm_gpiod_get(dev, "reset", GPIOD_OUT_HIGH) finds reset-gpios, sets the line as an output, and drives it to logical 1 before returning, so there is no window in which a reset line floats. Logical is the key word. The device tree's GPIO_ACTIVE_LOW flag is folded into the descriptor, so gpiod_set_value(rst, 1) means "assert reset" whether the board wires the line active-high or active-low; gpiod_set_raw_value() exists for the rare driver that needs the physical level.

Because GPIO controllers include I2C-attached expanders, some lines cannot be driven without sleeping. gpiod_set_value() and gpiod_get_value() are for controllers that can be accessed from atomic context, and gpiod_set_value() issues a WARN_ON() if the controller's can_sleep flag is set; process-context code that may meet any controller uses the _cansleep forms. Since Linux 6.15 the setters return int (they returned void before), so an expander that fails an I2C transfer can now report it. gpiod_to_irq() maps an input line to a Linux interrupt number for request_irq(). Userspace has its own path to the same lines. The /dev/gpiochipN character devices, with the version-2 line-request ioctls, replaced /sys/class/gpio, which is now deprecated and offered in Kconfig only when CONFIG_EXPERT is set. The kernel's own view of every requested line is in /sys/kernel/debug/gpio.

Many SoC blocks sit behind a dedicated reset line driven by a reset controller instead of a GPIO. The reset controller framework (drivers/reset/, include/linux/reset.h) hands out struct reset_control handles in two flavors, and the choice matters. An exclusive handle, from devm_reset_control_get_exclusive(), gives the driver sole control: reset_control_assert() and reset_control_deassert() act immediately, and reset_control_reset() pulses a self-clearing reset. A shared handle, from devm_reset_control_get_shared(), is for a line that several blocks share. There, deassertion is reference-counted—the line leaves reset when the first consumer deasserts it and returns to reset only when every consumer has asserted it again—so one driver cannot reset a block another is using. Shared consumers must not mix reset_control_reset() with assert and deassert on the same line. The _deasserted getters deassert at claim time and reassert at unbind. Whatever the flavor, the line is released only after power and clocks are stable, which is why it comes last in the bring-up sequence shown earlier.

Power Domains

Large SoCs divide their silicon into power domains—islands such as "the video codec," "the GPU," or "the camera subsystem"—that can be switched off whole when none of their devices is busy. Linux models them with generic PM domains, or genpd (drivers/pmdomain/core.c, moved there from drivers/base/power/). A provider driver describes each island with a struct generic_pm_domain carrying power_on and power_off callbacks, initializes it with pm_genpd_init(), nests domains with pm_genpd_add_subdomain(), and publishes them with of_genpd_add_provider_simple() or of_genpd_add_provider_onecell(). The provider drivers themselves live by vendor under drivers/pmdomain/.

A consumer with a single power-domains entry does nothing to join its domain: platform_probe() calls dev_pm_domain_attach(dev, PD_FLAG_ATTACH_POWER_ON | PD_FLAG_DETACH_POWER_OFF), which tries the ACPI PM domain and then genpd_dev_pm_attach(), installs the domain's dev_pm_domain operations in front of the driver's own, and powers the domain up for probe(). The automatic path stops at one domain: genpd_dev_pm_attach() deliberately returns without attaching when of_count_phandle_with_args() finds more than one entry, because a struct device can belong to only one PM domain. A device that straddles several domains calls devm_pm_domain_attach_list(), which creates a virtual device for each domain and links it to the real one.

From then on the domain follows runtime PM, which the device model section introduced. When a device's usage count reaches zero, genpd runs the driver's runtime_suspend callback, and when every device in the domain is suspended it queues a power-off. A governor can veto that power-off if the domain's wake-up latency would break a device's latency constraint. A driver therefore controls power by bracketing hardware access with pm_runtime_resume_and_get() and pm_runtime_put_autosuspend(), exactly as before; the domain machinery is invisible to it (Chapter 21, §6.2 describes genpd's side). A domain can also take over its devices' clocks (GENPD_FLAG_PM_CLK), so that gating them is part of suspending the device, and it can carry performance states that a consumer requests with dev_pm_genpd_set_performance_state() when the domain's voltage must rise with the device's clock. A domain that the bootloader left on stays on (genpd's stay_on flag) until its provider's sync_state() callback reports that every consumer has probed; only then can it power off when idle. Separately, genpd_power_off_unused(), a late_initcall_sync, queues a power-off for every domain with no active devices unless the pd_ignore_unused boot parameter is given. /sys/kernel/debug/pm_genpd/pm_genpd_summary lists each domain's state and the runtime state of each device in it.

Loading Firmware

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth chips, GPUs, DSPs, many NICs, and most SoC coprocessors run code that is not stored on the device; the driver must load it each time the device is powered. Those images are rarely redistributable as source, so they are not in the kernel tree. They ship as files, usually from the linux-firmware repository, under /lib/firmware. The driver asks for one by name: 

const struct firmware *fw;

ret = request_firmware(&fw, "vendor/my-uart.bin", dev);   /* may sleep */
if (ret)
        return dev_err_probe(dev, ret, "firmware\n");
my_load_image(dev, fw->data, fw->size);                      /* copy into the device */
release_firmware(fw);

The result is a struct firmware (include/linux/firmware.h) holding just a size and a read-only data pointer. A driver names the files it may request with MODULE_FIRMWARE(), which puts them in the module's metadata so that initramfs generators know to copy them in. Where the data comes from is decided by _request_firmware() in drivers/base/firmware_loader/main.c, which tries, in order:

  1. images linked into the kernel with CONFIG_EXTRA_FIRMWARE;
  2. a copy already held by the loader's cache for the same name;
  3. the filesystem, through the fw_path[] list—the directory given by the firmware_class.path= parameter, then /lib/firmware/updates/release, /lib/firmware/updates, /lib/firmware/release, and /lib/firmware, where release is the running kernel's uname -r;
  4. the same names with .zst and then .xz appended, when CONFIG_FW_LOADER_COMPRESS_ZSTD or _XZ is set;
  5. for firmware_request_platform() only, a copy embedded in the platform's EFI boot-services code, as some tablets ship touchscreen firmware;
  6. the sysfs fallback, described below, if the caller and the configuration allow it.

The filesystem read deserves a closer look, because it runs in an unusual context. A driver may request firmware while serving some unrelated process's system call, so the loader reads the file with the kernel's own credentials (scoped_with_kernel_creds()) and resolves the path in the initial mount namespace (kernel_read_file_from_path_initns()), not the caller's. The read also passes through the READING_FIRMWARE security hook, so IMA or LoadPin can insist that firmware is signed or comes from a trusted filesystem. When every source fails, the loader logs "Direct firmware load for name failed with error -2"—a message that is harmless for optional firmware and the first clue for missing firmware.

This direct read is comparatively new. In Linux 2.6 the kernel could not open a file on its own: request_firmware() created a temporary directory under /sys/class/firmware/ with loading and data attributes, sent a uevent, and waited for a hotplug script to copy the file in. Linux 3.7 taught the loader to read the files itself (commit abb139e75c2c), and the sysfs path became an optional fallback, built only with CONFIG_FW_LOADER_USER_HELPER. Even then plain request_firmware() uses it only when CONFIG_FW_LOADER_USER_HELPER_FALLBACK or the kernel.firmware_config.force_sysfs_fallback sysctl forces it; otherwise only request_firmware_nowait() called with uevent false opts in. The fallback waits 60 seconds by default for userspace to answer.

Three situations account for most firmware bugs. A built-in driver that probes before the root filesystem is mounted finds no /lib/firmware; the fixes are to put the file in the initramfs, to build it into the kernel, or to request it asynchronously with request_firmware_nowait(), whose callback runs from a workqueue when the load completes. A driver that reloads firmware on resume runs before filesystems are back, so the loader registers a PM notifier (fw_pm_notify()) that caches, before suspend, every image a device has loaded, and a driver can call firmware_request_cache() to request the same treatment. And very large images that the driver only needs to stream into the device are better loaded with request_firmware_into_buf() or request_partial_firmware_into_buf(), which use a caller-provided buffer and bypass the cache. The reverse direction—userspace pushing a new image into a device that flashes itself, such as an FPGA card—is served by firmware_upload_register() (CONFIG_FW_UPLOAD), which exposes a sysfs upload interface instead.

Character Device Drivers

The chapter's final section draws the threads together into the simplest complete driver kind: the character device driver. A character driver presents its device as a byte stream reached through a node in /dev—the model behind serial ports, /dev/random, input devices, and countless custom peripherals. Building one means claiming a device number, publishing a cdev, and supplying the file_operations that implement read, write, and ioctl. The section builds one such driver and then looks at two families that put a whole subsystem between the character device and the hardware: the tty layer behind terminals and serial ports, and the input subsystem behind /dev/input.

It is worth pausing on when not to build one. A character device node is a userspace ABI: once it ships, its name, its permissions and above all its ioctl commands must keep working forever, and the kernel's own guidance (Documentation/driver-api/ioctl.rst) lists the alternatives that are usually better. Exposing the state of an in-kernel object that is not tied to an open file belongs in sysfs; configuring network objects belongs in netlink; ad-hoc interfaces for debugging that nobody should depend on belong in debugfs; and complex configuration that outgrows sysfs's one-value-per-file rule belongs in configfs. What a character device is uniquely good at is what the rest of this chapter has been building toward: a file-descriptor-oriented interface, where the descriptor itself carries state, can block, can be polled, can be mapped, and can be passed between processes.

Registering a Character Device

Registration has two steps. First the driver reserves a device-number range: alloc_chrdev_region() asks the kernel to pick a free major and return it (the modern default), while register_chrdev_region() claims a specific, pre-agreed major (fs/char_dev.c). Second, the driver initializes a struct cdev with its file_operations and adds it to the kernel's character-device map with cdev_add(), which inserts the cdev into the same cdev_map that chrdev_open() later consults. From that moment, opening a device file with a matching number reaches this driver. 

struct cdev {
        struct kobject kobj;                    /* refcount + lifetime */
        struct module *owner;                   /* pins the module while open */
        const struct file_operations *ops;      /* the driver's methods */
        struct list_head list;
        dev_t dev;                              /* first device number */
        unsigned int count;                     /* how many minors it covers */
};
dev_t devno;
struct cdev my_cdev;

/* 1. reserve a dynamic major, one minor */
alloc_chrdev_region(&devno, 0, 1, "mychar");

/* 2. publish the cdev with our operations */
cdev_init(&my_cdev, &my_fops);
my_cdev.owner = THIS_MODULE;
cdev_add(&my_cdev, devno, 1);

A cdev can cover a range of minors, not just one: the count argument to cdev_add() is how a driver that manages many identical devices publishes them all under one major with a single call, then uses the minor number (available in open() from iminor(inode)) to tell them apart. Real drivers rarely use a standalone cdev as shown; they embed it inside their own per-device structure and recover that structure in every method with container_of(), which is how a method finds its device state without a global table. There are also two allocation styles—cdev_init() for a cdev embedded in a larger struct, and cdev_alloc() for a standalone heap one—and a combined helper, cdev_device_add(), that registers the cdev and its struct device together.

Ordering and cleanup matter. cdev_add() must be the last step, because the instant it returns the device is live and a concurrent open() can call into the driver—so all state the methods rely on must be initialized first. Teardown reverses registration exactly: cdev_del() removes the cdev from the map (after which no new opens reach it), then unregister_chrdev_region() releases the numbers. The legacy one-call register_chrdev() still exists and folds all of this into a single function, but it grabs all 256 minors of a major and is discouraged in new code in favor of the explicit pair above—or the miscdevice shortcut shown shortly.

The half of that teardown story most often missed is what cdev_del() does not do. Its kernel-doc says so explicitly: the call guarantees the device can no longer be opened, but "any cdevs already open will remain and their fops will still be callable after cdev_del returns" (fs/char_dev.c). Unregistering is therefore not a barrier. A process holding a descriptor can call straight into read, ioctl or mmap after the driver believes it has unregistered, which is why the owner field matters—it pins the module so the code at least still exists—and why a driver whose hardware is disappearing (a hot-unplugged USB device, or any device being unbound) needs its own flag or reference count so that surviving methods return -ENODEV instead of touching an iounmap'd register window. Getting this wrong produces exactly the use-after-free that unbinding a driver under an open descriptor is used to hunt for.

Two smaller conventions round out registration. A struct cdev is marked __randomize_layout, so its field order may be shuffled by the structure-layout randomization plugin: it must always be prepared with cdev_init() or cdev_alloc() and never with a designated-initializer literal or a memcpy. And when a driver publishes both a cdev and a struct device, cdev_device_add() is preferred not only for brevity but because it gets the order right: it calls cdev_set_parent() to tie the cdev's lifetime to the device's kobject and adds the cdev before device_add(), so that by the time the uevent fires and udev creates the node, an open of that node is already routable.

Note what registration does not do: it does not create the /dev node. Reserving a number only makes the driver reachable if a node with that number is opened. To make the node appear automatically, the driver creates a struct device under a class with device_create(), which sets the device's devt and thereby triggers the devtmpfs/uevent machinery from the device-files section. This is the join between the raw cdev mechanism and the device model. Table 13-19 collects the device-number and cdev functions a driver uses at registration.

Assigning Device Numbers

Registration reserves a dev_t, but it is worth understanding where that number comes from, where the kernel records it, and how a driver should choose between a fixed and an allocated major. The number itself is the major/minor pair packed by MKDEV() from the device-files section; assigning one means claiming a range of it—one major plus a run of count minors starting at a base.

Static versus dynamic majors. A few majors are permanently reserved by the kernel's device-number registry, documented in Documentation/admin-guide/devices.txt; a driver for such a device passes the fixed number to register_chrdev_region(). Every other driver should let the kernel choose, by calling alloc_chrdev_region() with a zero major and reading back the assigned dev_t. Dynamic allocation avoids collisions as the number of drivers grows and is the strong default; hard-coding a major is reserved for the handful of historically-fixed devices.

Where the assignment lives. Reserving a range does not touch any device file; it records the claim in a kernel table so a later cdev_add() and open() can find the owner. Character majors are tracked in a static hash array, chrdevs[] in fs/char_dev.c, whose entries (struct char_device_struct) record each reserved major/base-minor/count and the owner's name. When a driver later publishes a cdev, cdev_add() inserts the number range into a second structure, the cdev_map, which is what chrdev_open() consults to turn an opened node's number back into a cdev. The reservation table and the lookup map are thus distinct: one says "this major is taken," the other says "these numbers route to this driver." Userspace sees the reservations in /proc/devices.

How a dynamic major is picked. alloc_chrdev_region() asks find_dynamic_major(), which scans the reservation table from the top of the primary dynamic range downward—from major 254 down to 234 (CHRDEV_MAJOR_DYN_END)—and, if that is exhausted, falls back to an extended high range, 511 down to 384. Allocating from the top downward keeps dynamically-assigned numbers clear of the low, statically-assigned majors. Because the number is not known until allocation, a dynamic driver cannot ship a pre-made /dev node; it must publish the number—through the class and device_create() so udev makes the node—or a user reads it from /proc/devices and runs mknod.

That extended range is itself a piece of history worth reading. The original character-major space was a single byte, and the reservation table chrdevs[] still has only 255 entries, which is why the primary dynamic pool—the twenty-odd numbers between 234 and 254—can run out on a system with many dynamically-registered drivers. The extension to majors up to 511 (CHRDEV_MAJOR_MAX is 512) added headroom without disturbing any existing assignment, and it is handled by a slightly different loop: because chrdevs[] is a hash of 255 buckets, majors above 254 share buckets with low majors and must be matched by walking the chain rather than by indexing. None of this is visible to a driver, which simply gets a dev_t back, but it explains why the two ranges are searched by different code.

Choosing among the three registration calls. The two *_chrdev_region functions only reserve numbers—the driver still builds and adds its own cdev afterward—and they differ only in whether the major is fixed (register_chrdev_region) or kernel-chosen (alloc_chrdev_region). The older register_chrdev() is a different kind of call: it does everything in one step—reserves a whole major (all 256 minors), allocates a single cdev, and adds it—so the driver never touches a cdev directly, but it also gives up all the flexibility the split API provides (a chosen minor range, several cdev objects, or embedding the cdev in per-device state). The Driver's file_operations

The heart of a character driver is its file_operations table—the same structure the VFS chapter described, now filled in by a driver instead of a filesystem. After chrdev_open() swaps this table into the open file, every syscall on the descriptor lands in one of these methods. A minimal driver implements open, release, read, write, and usually unlocked_ioctl for out-of-band control. The read and write methods move data between the device and user space using copy_to_user() and copy_from_user(), which safely cross the kernel/user boundary and update the file position.

Because the device file goes through the VFS, a character driver automatically inherits the whole file model: its descriptors can be passed to poll()/epoll (if it implements poll), memory-mapped (if it implements mmap), and inherited across fork(). The driver only implements the methods that make sense for its hardware; the VFS supplies sensible behavior for the rest.

Two conventions make the methods practical. First, per-open state: the open method typically recovers the device from the inode's embedded cdev (via container_of()) and stashes it in file->private_data, so every later read/write/ioctl on that descriptor retrieves it in one step—and two independent opens of the same node can keep separate per-open state there. Second, the ioctl command numbers are not arbitrary integers: they are built with the _IO, _IOR, _IOW, and _IOWR macros, which pack a "magic" type byte, a command number, and the size and direction of the argument into the 32-bit command. Encoding the direction and size lets the kernel and userspace catch mismatched or wrong-device ioctls, and it is why a driver defines its commands in a shared header rather than hard-coding numbers.

A read that has no data yet must not spin. The idiom is a wait queue: the method calls wait_event_interruptible() to sleep until the device (usually from its interrupt handler, via wake_up_interruptible()) signals data is ready—unless the file was opened O_NONBLOCK, in which case it returns -EAGAIN immediately. The same wait queue backs the poll method, which reports readiness with poll_wait() plus a mask of EPOLLIN/EPOLLOUT bits, so the driver plugs cleanly into select/poll/epoll. This blocking-plus-poll pair is the backbone of almost every interrupt-driven character driver.

Three parts of the modern file_operations have no counterpart in the 2.6-era table and are worth naming. The BKL-era ioctl method is gone entirely; only unlocked_ioctl remains, and the name is a standing reminder that the kernel takes no lock on the driver's behalf—whatever serialization the commands need, the driver provides. Alongside read and write sit read_iter and write_iter, which take a struct kiocb and an iov_iter rather than a single user buffer; they are what readv/preadv2, asynchronous I/O and io_uring actually call, so a driver that implements only read/write works everywhere but is confined to the simple synchronous path. And file_operations now carries a fop_flags field of FOP_* bits—FOP_UNSIGNED_OFFSET for files whose offsets span the full 64-bit range, FOP_DONTCACHE, FOP_DIO_PARALLEL_WRITE and others—replacing an assortment of ad-hoc per-file mode hacks the VFS previously used to ask the same questions. Two newer hooks are appearing in drivers as well: uring_cmd, the io_uring passthrough entry point that lets a device define fire-and-forget commands submitted through a ring instead of via ioctl (NVMe passthrough and ublk are the flagship users), and mmap_prepare, a newer mmap hook that describes the mapping through a vm_area_desc before the VMA is created rather than mutating one that already exists.

Seeking deserves a note because the traditional answer no longer exists. Most character devices are streams for which a file position is meaningless, and for years such drivers set .llseek = no_llseek. That symbol has been removed; a NULL llseek now means the same thing, but the explicit and better approach is to call nonseekable_open() from the open method, which clears FMODE_LSEEK, FMODE_PREAD and FMODE_PWRITE so the VFS rejects the operations before the driver sees them. There is a stronger variant, stream_open(), which additionally drops FMODE_ATOMIC_POS: an ordinary file descriptor serializes readers and writers on the shared file position, so two threads reading the same descriptor take a mutex, and a driver whose read blocks indefinitely can therefore wedge an unrelated write on the same descriptor. Declaring the file a stream removes the position entirely and lets read and write run concurrently—the right choice for any pipe-like device.

Finally, an unlocked_ioctl handler is incomplete on its own. A 64-bit kernel running 32-bit userspace dispatches to compat_ioctl instead, and if that pointer is NULL the call simply fails, so a driver with no compat_ioctl is quietly broken for half its potential users. If the driver's commands take only pointers to structures whose layout is identical in both ABIs, the fix is one line: .compat_ioctl = compat_ptr_ioctl, a generic helper that runs the argument through compat_ptr()—which matters on s390, where 31-bit pointers have an ambiguous top bit—and then calls the native handler. Making that one-liner sufficient is what the structure rules in Documentation/process/botching-up-ioctls.rst are for: use only fixed-width __u32/__u64 types, never long, size_t or a pointer, because their widths differ; pad every structure explicitly to a multiple of 64 bits and check that the padding is zero so it can be used later; and pass pointers as __u64 to be cast with u64_to_user_ptr(). The size and direction that _IOR/_IOW encode help but do not save a badly-laid-out structure, since the same command number then means two different layouts. 

/* Command numbers carry direction + arg size (shared header) */

Miscellaneous Devices

For a driver that needs just one minor number, the full alloc_chrdev_region/cdev_add/device_create sequence is more ceremony than the job warrants. The miscdevice framework (drivers/char/misc.c) packages it all: many small drivers share one major (10), and each registers a struct miscdevice giving a name and a file_operations. A single misc_register() call allocates a minor (or uses MISC_DYNAMIC_MINOR), creates the /dev node via the device model, and wires up the cdev—so a whole char driver can be a few lines. 

static struct miscdevice my_misc = {
        .minor = MISC_DYNAMIC_MINOR,
        .name  = "mychar",           /* appears as /dev/mychar */
        .fops  = &my_fops,
};
misc_register(&my_misc);            /* creates the node, shares major 10 */

Under the hood a misc device is still an ordinary character device—it just shares the misc major (10) among all its users, allocating a distinct minor to each. Setting .minor = MISC_DYNAMIC_MINOR lets the core pick a free minor, which is what almost every driver does; well-known devices such as /dev/fuse or /dev/kvm instead request a fixed, reserved minor. After misc_register() returns, the this_device field points at the struct device the core created, so the driver can hang sysfs attributes off it or use it for dev_err()-style logging. misc_deregister() undoes everything in one call.

The trade-off is scope. The miscdevice framework is ideal for a driver that exposes a single control node—a sensor, a watchdog, a virtual device—and it removes essentially all the boilerplate. A driver that manages many minors (dozens of ports, a whole class of disks) or that needs a class of its own for grouping and per-class policy should use the full alloc_chrdev_region/cdev_add/class_create path instead. Most custom peripheral drivers fall into the first camp, which is why miscdevice is so common in practice and why the worked example below uses it.

A few struct miscdevice fields beyond minor/name/fops repay setting. parent should point at the underlying struct device—the platform or PCI device the driver bound to—so the misc node appears beneath the real hardware in the sysfs tree instead of dangling at the root of the class, which is what lets userspace walk from /dev/mychar back to the bus address that produced it. groups attaches an attribute group to the created device, giving sysfs files without any extra registration. nodename overrides the path udev uses, and may contain a slash, which is how devices land in a /dev subdirectory. mode sets the node's default permission bits for the rare device that should not default to root-only. For a module whose entire job is one node, module_misc_device() collapses the module init and exit functions into a single line, and MODULE_ALIAS_MISCDEV() emits the char-major-10-N alias that lets an open of a fixed-minor node autoload the module.

The minor-space limit that once made drivers hesitate to use miscdevice is gone. Historically the dynamic pool was a small bitmap of a few dozen minors below 255, and busy systems really did exhaust it. In Linux 7.0.12 misc_minor_alloc() uses an IDA and allocates dynamic minors from MISC_DYNAMIC_MINOR + 1 (256) all the way up to MINORMASK, while the misc core registers the entire minor range of major 10 (drivers/char/misc.c). Fixed minors below 255 remain available for the registry-assigned devices such as /dev/fuse and /dev/kvm, and dynamic users are effectively unlimited.

One gotcha runs against the grain of everything else in this chapter: there is no devm_misc_register(). A misc device must be released explicitly with misc_deregister() from the driver's remove callback or module exit, even in a driver where every other resource is managed. The reason is the one from the previous subsection—an open descriptor keeps the file operations reachable—so the unregistration has to happen at a point the driver controls rather than wherever devres decides to unwind.

A Minimal Character Driver

The following sketch ties the pieces together: a platform driver that, on probe(), maps its device's registers (from device tree or ACPI), requests its interrupt, and exposes a /dev node through the misc framework. It is deliberately skeletal, but every call in it has appeared earlier in the chapter, which is the point—the modern driver is assembled from the shared mechanisms, not written from scratch. 

struct my_dev {
        void __iomem   *regs;
        struct miscdevice misc;
};

static irqreturn_t my_isr(int irq, void *data)
{
        struct my_dev *d = data;
        u32 st = readl(d->regs + STATUS);   /* top half: ack the device */
        writel(st, d->regs + STATUS);
        return IRQ_HANDLED;
}

static const struct file_operations my_fops = {
        .owner = THIS_MODULE,
        .read  = my_read,
        .write = my_write,
        .unlocked_ioctl = my_ioctl,
};

static int my_probe(struct platform_device *pdev)
{
        struct my_dev *d;
        int irq, ret;

        d = devm_kzalloc(&pdev->dev, sizeof(*d), GFP_KERNEL);
        if (!d)
                return -ENOMEM;

        d->regs = devm_platform_ioremap_resource(pdev, 0);   /* MMIO window */
        if (IS_ERR(d->regs))
                return PTR_ERR(d->regs);

        irq = platform_get_irq(pdev, 0);                     /* virq */
        if (irq < 0)
                return irq;

        ret = devm_request_irq(&pdev->dev, irq, my_isr, 0, "mychar", d);
        if (ret)
                return ret;

        d->misc.minor = MISC_DYNAMIC_MINOR;
        d->misc.name  = "mychar";
        d->misc.fops  = &my_fops;
        platform_set_drvdata(pdev, d);
        return misc_register(&d->misc);                      /* /dev/mychar */
}

static const struct of_device_id my_of_ids[] = {
        { .compatible = "vendor,mychar" },
        { }
};
MODULE_DEVICE_TABLE(of, my_of_ids);

static struct platform_driver my_driver = {
        .probe  = my_probe,
        .driver = { .name = "mychar", .of_match_table = my_of_ids },
};
module_platform_driver(my_driver);

Every managed (devm_*) call in the example is released automatically when the device unbinds, so there is almost no cleanup code—a stark contrast to the manual unwinding that 2.6-era drivers required. The driver names the hardware it supports through its of_match_table, gets its register window and interrupt from whatever firmware enumerated the device, and publishes a byte-stream interface through /dev. That is a complete, if minimal, modern character driver.

Two details the sketch elides are exactly where a real driver's remaining work sits. Because misc_register() has no managed form, the driver needs a remove callback that calls misc_deregister()—and in Linux 7.0.12 that callback returns void, not int, following the tree-wide conversion that removed a return value the driver core never had any way to act on. Second, ordering: misc_register() is deliberately the last statement in probe(), for the same reason cdev_add() must be last. The instant the node exists, udev may open it, so every field the file operations touch—the register window, the wait queue, any locks—must already be initialized. The managed calls above it help here too: because devm_kzalloc(), devm_platform_ioremap_resource() and devm_request_irq() unwind themselves in reverse order on a failed probe, each early return in the example is a complete and correct error path with no goto ladder behind it. That, more than any single API, is what separates this driver from its 2.6-era ancestor.

What the sketch leaves out is the real work: the my_read/my_write bodies that move bytes through the device's FIFO, the wait queue and wake_up_interruptible() that let my_isr hand data to a blocked reader, and the locking that protects shared state between the interrupt handler and the file methods (a spin_lock_irqsave() around the FIFO, as the interrupt section described). Those details are specific to each device, but the frame—probe acquires resources, file_operations expose them, managed helpers clean up—is identical across the vast majority of character drivers. That uniform frame, assembled from the mechanisms of this chapter, is what the modern device model buys a driver author.

The tty Layer

The terminal—tty, after the teletypes Unix first ran on—is the oldest character-device family and still one of the most used: every serial console, every SSH session, and every terminal emulator window is a tty. It is also the clearest example of a subsystem that sits between the character-device layer and the drivers. A tty driver does not supply file_operations. The tty core in drivers/tty/ installs tty_fops (drivers/tty/tty_io.c) on every tty node, and the driver supplies only a struct tty_operations table of hardware-facing callbacks— write, write_room, set_termios, hangup, and so on. Between the two sits a line discipline, a pluggable protocol layer that decides what the bytes mean. Figure 13-7 shows the stack.

A tty driver is described by a struct tty_driver (include/linux/tty_driver.h), allocated with tty_alloc_driver() and registered with tty_register_driver(), which reserves a major and range of minors just as register_chrdev_region() does for a plain character driver. Serial ports use major 4 (TTY_MAJOR) for ttySN; many SoC UART drivers take a dynamic major. Each open device is a struct tty_struct (include/linux/tty.h), created on first open and shared by every file that opens the same node. It carries the driver and line index, the termios settings, the window size, the current line discipline, the controlling session and foreground process group, and a link pointer used by ptys. Hardware state that outlives an open—the receive buffer, the open count, carrier detection and the wait for it, and the transmit kfifo that the serial core has used since Linux 6.10—lives in a struct tty_port embedded in the driver's per-port structure. tty_port arrived in Linux 2.6.27 and moved the common open, close, and hangup logic out of each driver.

The two directions of data take different paths. A write() goes through the line discipline's write method, which for the default discipline handles output processing such as turning \n into \r\n, and then into the driver's write callback. Received bytes travel the other way through the flip buffer. The driver's interrupt handler calls tty_insert_flip_char() or tty_insert_flip_string() to append to the port's buffer, then tty_flip_buffer_push(), which queues flush_to_ldisc() (drivers/tty/tty_buffer.c) on a workqueue. The worker hands the bytes to the discipline's receive_buf in process context, so the discipline can take mutexes and the interrupt handler does no more than copy.

The default discipline, N_TTY (drivers/tty/n_tty.c), implements everything POSIX calls the general terminal interface. In canonical mode it collects a line in a 4 KB buffer (N_TTY_BUF_SIZE), handles erase and kill characters, and echoes input. It also turns the interrupt, quit, and suspend characters into SIGINT, SIGQUIT, and SIGTSTP sent with kill_pgrp() to the terminal's foreground process group—the mechanism behind job control described with process groups and sessions in Chapter 1 and with signals in Chapter 11. Other disciplines reuse the same drivers for different protocols: N_HCI turns a UART into a Bluetooth HCI transport, N_GSM0710 multiplexes a cellular modem's channels, and N_PPP carries PPP. Userspace switches a tty's discipline with the TIOCSETD ioctl; each discipline is a struct tty_ldisc_ops registered under a number from include/uapi/linux/tty.h.

Most hardware drivers in this family are UART drivers, and they do not use tty_operations directly either. The serial core (drivers/tty/serial/serial_core.c) implements the tty side once for all UARTs: it maps termios to baud rate, parity, and flow control, owns the transmit kfifo, and manages modem-control lines. A UART driver registers a struct uart_driver with uart_register_driver(), adds each port's struct uart_port with uart_add_one_port(), and fills a struct uart_ops with register-level callbacks such as startup, shutdown, set_termios, start_tx, stop_rx, and tx_empty. The PC-compatible 16550 family is handled by drivers/tty/serial/8250/; ARM64 SoCs mostly use the PL011 (amba-pl011.c) or a vendor UART. Since Linux 6.5 the serial core also creates serial-base bus devices for each controller and port, so a UART can use runtime PM like any other device.

Two departures from the classic model matter on embedded systems. First, when a UART is permanently wired to another chip—a Bluetooth or GNSS module on a phone board—the device tree describes that chip as a child of the UART node, and serdev (drivers/tty/serdev/, Linux 4.11) binds a kernel driver to it. tty_port_register_device_attr_serdev() then creates no /dev/ttySN node, and the child driver talks to the UART through serdev_device_write_buf() and a receive callback; the line discipline is bypassed. Second, a pseudo-terminal (pty) is a tty pair with no hardware. Opening /dev/ptmx (major 5, minor 2) allocates a master and creates the matching slave /dev/pts/N in the devpts filesystem. The pty driver's write (drivers/tty/pty.c) simply pushes the bytes into the flip buffer of the other side through tty->link, so whatever the terminal emulator or sshd writes to the master arrives at the slave as if typed, passes through N_TTY, and is read by the shell. The Input Subsystem

Keyboards, mice, touchscreens, game controllers, and the power button on a phone all report the same kind of data—a stream of key presses and axis movements—and the input subsystem (drivers/input/) gives them one interface. It is split in three. A device driver describes the hardware with a struct input_dev (include/linux/input.h) and reports events. An input handler (struct input_handler) consumes events: evdev exports them to userspace, while others feed the console keyboard (drivers/tty/vt/keyboard.c), the magic SysRq key, or the legacy /dev/input/mice and joystick nodes. The input core in drivers/input/input.c matches the two. When either side registers, input_attach_handler() compares the device's capabilities with each handler's id_table and, on a match, calls the handler's connect method, which creates an input_handle linking the pair.

Every event is a triple of type, code, and value. Types come from include/uapi/linux/input-event-codes.h: EV_KEY for keys and buttons (value 1 for press, 0 for release, 2 for autorepeat), EV_REL for relative motion, EV_ABS for absolute axes such as touch coordinates, and EV_SYN. A driver reports a batch of events that describe one moment and closes it with input_sync(), which sends EV_SYN/SYN_REPORT. The core buffers events until that marker and passes the whole packet to the handlers, so a client never sees an X coordinate without its Y. Because input_event() takes only the device's event_lock spinlock, a driver may report from interrupt context, though most do so from a threaded handler. The following driver reports one GPIO-connected button: 

struct my_button {
        struct input_dev *input;
        struct gpio_desc *gpio;
};

static irqreturn_t my_button_irq(int irq, void *data)
{
        struct my_button *b = data;
        int v = gpiod_get_value_cansleep(b->gpio);       /* logical level */

        if (v < 0)
                return IRQ_NONE;
        input_report_key(b->input, KEY_POWER, v);       /* EV_KEY */
        input_sync(b->input);                           /* EV_SYN/SYN_REPORT */
        return IRQ_HANDLED;
}

static int my_button_probe(struct platform_device *pdev)
{
        struct device *dev = &pdev->dev;
        struct my_button *b;
        int irq, ret;

        b = devm_kzalloc(dev, sizeof(*b), GFP_KERNEL);
        if (!b)
                return -ENOMEM;
        b->gpio = devm_gpiod_get(dev, NULL, GPIOD_IN);    /* "gpios" */
        if (IS_ERR(b->gpio))
                return dev_err_probe(dev, PTR_ERR(b->gpio), "gpio\n");
        irq = gpiod_to_irq(b->gpio);
        if (irq < 0)
                return irq;

        b->input = devm_input_allocate_device(dev);
        if (!b->input)
                return -ENOMEM;
        b->input->name = "my-button";
        input_set_capability(b->input, EV_KEY, KEY_POWER);

        ret = devm_request_threaded_irq(dev, irq, NULL, my_button_irq,
                        IRQF_ONESHOT | IRQF_TRIGGER_RISING | IRQF_TRIGGER_FALLING,
                        "my-button", b);
        if (ret)
                return ret;
        return input_register_device(b->input);         /* creates eventN */
}

The interrupt handler is threaded because the GPIO might sit on an I2C expander, so reading it may sleep. The capability call matters too: the core silently drops events of a type or code that the device did not declare (is_event_supported() in input_event()), and the declared capabilities are what handlers match against. Because the input device was allocated with devm_input_allocate_device(), it is unregistered automatically when the driver unbinds. The kernel's own gpio-keys driver (drivers/input/keyboard/gpio_keys.c) is the full version of this example, with debouncing, wake-up support, and a device-tree binding for any number of buttons.

On the userspace side, evdev (drivers/input/evdev.c) creates /dev/input/eventN under major 13 (INPUT_MAJOR), starting at minor 64 and moving to dynamic minors from 256 once the first 32 are used. Each open file gets its own evdev_client with a ring buffer sized from the device's hint_events_per_packet, at least 64 events, and read() returns whole struct input_event records, each with a timestamp, type, code, and value. A client that falls behind does not block the device: evdev discards the queue and inserts a SYN_DROPPED event so the client knows to resynchronize its state with EVIOCGKEY or EVIOCGABS. EVIOCGRAB gives one client exclusive use of a device, which is how a compositor or a kiosk keeps keystrokes away from the text console. Display servers and the libinput library are evdev clients, and /proc/bus/input/devices lists every input device with its handlers. The reverse path also exists: /dev/uinput (drivers/input/misc/uinput.c) lets a userspace program create an input device and inject events, which is how remote-desktop tools and user-space drivers present themselves as ordinary keyboards.

Summary

This chapter traced the path from a filesystem's method call down to real hardware. The kernel reaches devices over buses through controllers, and it touches their registers not with raw pointers but with the port and MMIO accessors—inb()/outb() where a separate I/O space exists, and readl()/writel() over a window mapped by ioremap() everywhere—after claiming the address range in the resource tree that backs /proc/iomem.

Above that hardware layer sits the unified device model: kobjects give every device, driver, and bus a sysfs identity and a reference count; buses match devices to drivers and drive probing; and device links derived from firmware—together with deferred probe as the fallback—let drivers load in any order. Devices reach the model through firmware enumeration—a device tree on ARM64, ACPI on x86_64, unified behind fwnode so one driver reads its properties the same way on either—or announce themselves on discoverable buses like PCIe. A device number and a cdev turn a driver into a file in /dev, created automatically by devtmpfs and refined by udev; a character driver then exposes the device as a byte stream through a file_operations table, usually via the miscdevice shortcut, and the ioctl commands it defines become userspace ABI the moment they ship. Interrupts are delivered through irq_domain translation and split into a minimal top half and a sleepable bottom half—commonly a threaded IRQ, and always one under CONFIG_PREEMPT_RT—while the highest-rate paths give up interrupts entirely and poll. Bulk data moves by DMA through a portable mapping API that hides bounce buffers and, where present, an IOMMU that both extends a device's reach and confines it. On an SoC a driver first claims what makes the device alive at all—its clocks, supply rails, pin states, reset lines, and power domain—through consumer frameworks that the device tree links to provider drivers, and loads any firmware the device runs from /lib/firmware. Two large character-driver families show the same layering at work: the tty layer, where line disciplines and the serial core sit between /dev/ttyS0 and a UART, and the input subsystem, where evdev turns every keyboard, touchscreen, and button into /dev/input/eventN. The unifying theme is that Linux 7.0.12 has pushed an enormous amount of complexity out of individual drivers and into shared frameworks. A modern character driver declares the hardware it supports through a match table, is handed its register window, interrupt, and DMA resources by the core, and is assembled from managed helpers that clean up on unbind—so the same small body of code runs on a discoverable x86_64 PCIe card and a device-tree-described ARM64 SoC peripheral alike. That portability, invisible to the user who simply opens /dev/..., is the payoff of the device model this chapter has described, and the foundation for the block-device and networking chapters that follow.
