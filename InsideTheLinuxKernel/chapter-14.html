The previous chapter showed how a character driver presents its device as a byte stream reached directly through a node in /dev. Block devices—disks of every kind, from spinning platters to NVMe SSDs—are different in a way that shapes an entire kernel subsystem around them. Their defining trait is the disparity between how fast the CPU can issue requests and how the storage device services them: one transfer takes microseconds to milliseconds, but the device can hold many in flight at once, and the order and batching in which they are issued dramatically affects throughput. A block device is also almost never reached directly. A process reads a file; the filesystem translates that into reads of specific disk blocks; the page cache satisfies most of them from memory; and only the misses become real device I/O. The block layer exists to manage that gap—packaging each unavoidable transfer as a bio, merging and ordering it against other pending I/O, and handing it to a driver.

This chapter follows that path from the top down: the units the layer counts in, the bio that carries a transfer, the gendisk and block_device that represent a disk and its partitions, the multi-queue machinery that turns bios into tagged requests, the schedulers and I/O priorities that order them, the lifecycle that ties the pieces together, the integrity and encryption services that ride along with a bio, and finally what a driver must implement. The distance from Linux 2.6 is unusually large here. Everything that defined the 2.6 block layer—the single per-device request queue and its lock, the request_fn strategy routine, and the noop, deadline, anticipatory, and CFQ elevators—has been deleted from the tree. Linux 7.0.12 has only the multi-queue block layer that replaced it, and that redesign is the heart of this chapter.

The Role of the Block Layer

The block layer is the kernel subsystem that sits between filesystems (and the page cache) above and storage-device drivers below. Its purpose is to turn the stream of small, scattered, unordered I/O requests that come from the rest of the kernel into an efficient sequence of transfers the hardware can execute quickly. Because storage is orders of magnitude slower than memory, and because the cost of a transfer depends heavily on which blocks are accessed and in what order, this middle layer is where much of a system's real-world I/O performance is won or lost. This section frames what makes block devices distinctive, the shape of the stack they live in, and the request-based model that organizes it all.

It is worth having the whole architecture in view before the details. The block layer is not a single component but a small pipeline of them, and Figure 14-1 lays out the pieces this chapter examines one at a time. Above it, several sources generate block I/O: page-cache writeback, direct I/O, filesystem metadata, and swap. All of them funnel through a single entry point, submit_bio(), which hands the layer a bio. From there the work divides into two paths. Most I/O takes the request-based path into blk-mq, where per-CPU software queues, an optional I/O scheduler, and per-hardware-queue dispatch queues turn bios into tagged requests and feed them to a driver's queue_rq. A second, bio-based path lets stacking drivers—device-mapper (LVM, dm-crypt) and MD RAID—intercept bios directly, transform or fan them out, and resubmit them, so a logical volume or a RAID array can layer on top of the real disks below. At the bottom, hardware drivers—NVMe, virtio-blk, and SCSI disks via the SCSI mid-layer—drive the physical devices.

Two things about that picture are worth fixing in mind before the details. The first is how wide the bottom row has become. In Linux 2.6 a block device was, with few exceptions, a spinning disk behind an IDE or SCSI controller; in Linux 7.0.12 the same interface serves NVMe namespaces, virtio-blk disks in virtual machines, eMMC and SD cards in embedded systems, network-backed devices such as NBD and Ceph RBD, purely software devices such as zram and the loop driver, and the virtual devices that stacking drivers synthesize. Everything in this chapter applies to all of them, because the block layer's contract is with the abstraction—an array of numbered sectors reachable by DMA—and not with any particular transport.

The second is that the descent through the block layer is not only about moving data. Several services are attached to a bio on its way down and travel with it: cgroup v2 I/O control can throttle or proportionally share the device between control groups (io.max, io.latency, io.weight and the cost model in block/blk-throttle.c, block/blk-iolatency.c and block/blk-iocost.c); inline encryption can have a crypto engine in the storage controller encrypt the transfer (bi_crypt_context, block/blk-crypto.c); end-to-end data integrity can attach T10 protection information to it (bi_integrity), both described in section 8; and tracing and accounting record it for /proc/diskstats, iostat, and blktrace. None of these belong to the filesystem above or the driver below. Each is implemented once in the block layer, and a driver at most declares whether its hardware can help, which is precisely the argument for having a common block layer rather than letting each driver reinvent the path.

Block Devices versus Character Devices

Chapter 13 treated a character device as a byte stream: a program reads and writes it sequentially, and the driver services each call directly. A block device is fundamentally different in three ways. First, it is randomly addressable—any block can be read or written by number, in any order—so the kernel is free to reorder and batch accesses. Second, it is accessed in fixed-size units (blocks) rather than arbitrary byte counts. Third, and most importantly, it is almost always mediated by the page cache: reads are usually satisfied from cached pages, and writes are usually buffered and written back later, so the device sees only the traffic the cache cannot absorb.

These differences are why block devices get a whole subsystem of their own rather than the thin file_operations path a character driver uses. A character driver's read moves bytes now; a block device's read may be merged with an adjacent one, reordered behind a more urgent request, held briefly to allow further merging, and completed asynchronously by an interrupt long after the submitting thread has moved on. The block layer is the machinery that makes all of that happen while presenting the device upward as a simple array of numbered blocks.

The distinction also shows up in how the two kinds of device are named and represented. Both are exposed as special files with a dev_t (Chapter 13), but a block special file's operations are collected in a struct block_device_operations rather than the character device's file_operations, and the kernel routes I/O to it through submit_bio() instead of a direct ->read/->write call. A block device is also partitionable: a single disk (struct gendisk) can carry several partitions, each surfaced as its own block device with its own dev_t. Character devices have no equivalent notion. Table 14-1 summarizes the contrasts that motivate the rest of the chapter.

Registration is asymmetric too, and in a way that has quietly inverted since Linux 2.6. A character driver must claim its device numbers explicitly—alloc_chrdev_region() followed by cdev_add()—before anything can open it. A block driver does not: add_disk() performs the whole job, and if the driver leaves disk->major at zero the core assigns it the extended block major BLOCK_EXT_MAJOR (259, registered under the name blkext) together with a dynamically allocated minor from a shared pool (block/genhd.c). register_blkdev() still exists, but it no longer gates anything; it only publishes a name in /proc/devices and optionally installs a probe callback for module autoloading. Some drivers still call it out of tradition— virtio-blk takes a dynamic major this way—while NVMe does not call it at all and lets add_disk() supply everything. This is why a modern system has far more distinct block devices than the fixed major-number table of the 2.6 era could ever have accommodated.

The Block I/O Stack

A block I/O travels through a tall stack, and understanding the layers is the key to the rest of the chapter. Each layer exists to absorb work so that the layer beneath it does less, and each speaks to its neighbors through a narrow, well-defined interface. From the top:

  • VFS and the page cache. A process's read()/write() enters the VFS, which usually resolves it against the page cache. A read that hits returns immediately; a miss, or a writeback of dirty pages, becomes real device I/O. This is the single most important layer for performance: on a warm system the overwhelming majority of file I/O never descends past it, which is precisely why the layers below can afford to be elaborate.
  • The filesystem (mapping layer). When the page cache does need backing store, the filesystem translates a file offset into device block numbers—for example through its address_space_operations (readahead, writepages, ->iomap_begin)—producing the actual on-disk locations to transfer. Direct I/O and swap enter here too, bypassing the cache but still using the same mapping machinery to find blocks.
  • The block layer. The blocks to transfer are packaged as one or more bio structures and submitted with submit_bio(). The block layer builds them into requests, merges and orders those requests (optionally through an I/O scheduler), assigns each a tag, and dispatches them to the driver. This is the layer the chapter is really about, and everything from the bio onward lives here.
  • The block device driver. The driver receives each request in its queue_rq callback, programs the hardware to perform the transfer (often by DMA, from Chapter 13), and signals completion—usually from an interrupt handler that calls back into the block layer to end the request and wake whoever was waiting.

Figure 14-2 shows this path as a straight descent. The crucial point is that a single high-level read() may touch every layer or none of them below the page cache: the layers exist precisely so that the expensive bottom—actual device I/O—is reached as rarely as possible and, when it is reached, is as efficient as possible. Where Figure 14-1 laid out the block layer's internal components and the two submission paths through them, Figure 14-2 collapses that detail to emphasize the vertical journey and the point at which most requests turn back.

The layers above are a useful mental model, but the real code makes one more thing clear: a great deal happens to a bio in the few lines between submit_bio() and the point where it becomes a request, and none of it is a layer in the diagram. submit_bio() itself only does accounting and assigns the bio an I/O priority derived from the submitting task's ionice class; the work is in submit_bio_noacct() (block/blk-core.c), which validates the operation against what the device actually supports, rejects a read or write that runs past the end of the device, remaps a partition-relative sector to a whole-disk one (blk_partition_remap()), strips REQ_PREFLUSH and REQ_FUA from a device that has no volatile write cache so that drivers need not handle them, fails a REQ_NOWAIT bio immediately rather than blocking if the device cannot honor it, and finally offers the bio to cgroup throttling (blk_throtl_bio()), which may hold it back entirely. Only a bio that survives all of that reaches the two-path dispatch of Figure 14-1. It is worth reading this one function in the source, because it is the shortest possible answer to the question of what the block layer guarantees a driver: a bio whose operation the device supports, whose sectors are in range and expressed in whole-disk terms, and whose flags the driver is actually obliged to implement.

The Request-Based Model

The organizing abstraction of the block layer is the request. A bio describes a single contiguous transfer as submitted; the block layer gathers bios into struct request objects, each representing a unit of work the driver will execute, and it is requests—not raw bios—that are merged, ordered, queued, and dispatched. This indirection is what enables the layer's two central optimizations: merging adjacent transfers into one larger request, and ordering requests so the device does less seeking or gets a fairer share among competing processes.

Why interpose a request between the bio and the driver at all? Because the two optimizations only make sense on an object that can accumulate work. A lone bio is fixed the moment it is submitted, but a request can grow: when a new bio describes sectors immediately after (or before) those already in a pending request, the layer extends the request to cover both rather than issuing two transfers—merging. Requests also give the layer something to reorder. Rather than sending each transfer to the device the instant it arrives, the layer can hold requests briefly (plugging), sort them, and release them as a batch, so a rotational disk seeks less and a flash device receives larger, better-aligned commands. A request additionally carries the bookkeeping a driver needs—a tag that names it uniquely, the target device and starting sector, the list of bios that make it up, and the completion callback—so that once the block layer hands a request to queue_rq, the driver has everything required to program the hardware and, later, to report the result back.

Not every driver wants this treatment. A stacking driver such as device-mapper or MD RAID (the bio-based path in Figure 14-1) registers a ->submit_bio hook and receives raw bios, which it remaps or splits and then resubmits toward the disks underneath; it forgoes requests, tags, and scheduling because it is a transformer, not the final consumer of the I/O. Ordinary hardware drivers take the request-based path, and it is that path—by far the common case—that the rest of this chapter follows.

There is also a second, quieter source of requests that the bio-to-request story leaves out. A passthrough request carries a device command rather than a file-data transfer: an NVMe admin command, a SCSI CDB submitted through SG_IO, a vendor operation issued from an ioctl. Such a request is built directly with blk_mq_alloc_request(), optionally given a data buffer with blk_rq_map_user(), and run with blk_execute_rq(); it has no bios describing file content and is marked with the driver-private operations REQ_OP_DRV_IN and REQ_OP_DRV_OUT, which blk_rq_is_passthrough() tests for. Passthrough requests share the queue's tags, its dispatch path, and the driver's queue_rq—the driver cannot tell where a request came from except by asking—but they are deliberately excluded from merging and from the I/O scheduler, because reordering or combining device commands would change their meaning. This is what lets tools like nvme-cli and sg_inq reach a device through the same queue that is carrying the filesystem's traffic.

How those requests are queued and dispatched is exactly what changed most since Linux 2.6, and it is worth stating the shift up front because it recurs throughout the chapter. The 2.6-era block layer gave each device a single request queue guarded by one lock, ordered by an elevator scheduler (noop, deadline, anticipatory, or CFQ), and drained by the driver's request_fn strategy routine. On a fast device served by many CPUs, that single lock became the bottleneck: every core that submitted or completed I/O contended for it, and a modern NVMe drive capable of millions of IOPS could spend more time waiting on the lock than on the hardware. Linux 7.0.12 replaces the whole design with the multi-queue block layer (blk-mq): per-CPU software queues feed per-hardware-queue dispatch queues, requests are identified by tags drawn from a per-queue bitmap, and the driver implements a queue_rq callback instead of a strategy routine. The legacy single-queue path, its request_fn interface, and the old elevators were removed entirely in 5.0, so blk-mq is now the only request path in the kernel. Before following that path down, though, it is worth meeting the alternative the block layer also supports for virtual and stacking devices—the bio-based model.

The Bio-Based Model

Not every block device is the bottom of the stack. A stacking driver—a logical volume, an encrypted device, a RAID array—sits between the filesystem and one or more real disks, presenting a single virtual device upward while forwarding I/O to the devices beneath it. Such a driver has no hardware of its own to schedule, so it opts out of the request machinery entirely and works directly in terms of bios. This is the bio-based model, the second path out of submit_bio() in Figure 14-1.

A driver selects it by construction. Instead of allocating its disk with blk_mq_alloc_disk() and a tag set, it calls blk_alloc_disk(lim, node)—passing a struct queue_limits that declares what the virtual device can accept—and supplies a submit_bio method in its block_device_operations (include/linux/blkdev.h). That marks the device with the BD_HAS_SUBMIT_BIO flag, and the block layer's dispatch routine honors it: __submit_bio() (block/blk-core.c) calls blk_mq_submit_bio() for an ordinary device but disk->fops->submit_bio(bio) for a bio-based one—no software queues, no I/O scheduler, no tags, and no struct request are involved.

Inside that callback the driver behaves as a transformer. It examines the incoming bio, retargets it at the device below by resetting bi_bdev and adjusting the start sector bi_iter.bi_sector, and resubmits it downward with submit_bio_noacct(). When one bio does not map cleanly onto a single underlying device—because it straddles a stripe boundary or a device-mapper target—the driver splits it with bio_split() and submits the pieces; when the same data must go to several devices, as in RAID-1 mirroring, it clones the bio with bio_alloc_clone() and sends a copy to each. Completions are threaded back with bio_chain() so the original bio finishes only once every child it spawned has completed. The canonical implementations are device-mapper (drivers/md/dm.c, providing LVM, dm-crypt, dm-thin) and MD RAID (drivers/md/md.c).

It is worth separating two ideas that are easy to conflate, because the overlap is not total in either direction. Bio-based does not imply stacking: a driver whose "device" is memory has nothing to schedule and no seeks to optimize, so zram (drivers/block/zram/zram_drv.c), the RAM disk brd (drivers/block/brd.c), and persistent memory (drivers/nvdimm/pmem.c) all take the bio-based path while sitting squarely at the bottom of the stack, servicing each bio with a memory copy and completing it before submit_bio() returns. Nor does stacking imply bio-based: device-mapper still supports a request-based mode (DM_TYPE_REQUEST_BASED, include/linux/device-mapper.h) used by dm-multipath, which wants to make its path-selection decision on a fully built request rather than on individual bios. The choice is really about whether a driver has a queue worth scheduling, not about where it sits.

Because a stacking driver forwards bios to real disks that do use the request-based path, a single high-level transfer can cross submit_bio() more than once—once into the virtual device and again, after remapping, into each physical device—which is exactly the loop-back arrow in Figure 14-1. Keeping scheduling and tagging at the bottom, next to the hardware, is deliberate: a remapping layer only needs to redirect bios quickly, and interposing a second scheduler in the middle would add latency without helping the device seek less.

That re-entry raises an obvious hazard: a deep stack—a filesystem on LVM on dm-crypt on RAID-5 on several disks—would recurse through submit_bio_noacct() once per level, and each level may need to allocate memory from a mempool before it can forward anything. Under memory pressure a lower level could then block waiting for a bio that an upper level cannot release, and the kernel stack is finite besides. The block layer removes both risks by converting the recursion into iteration. __submit_bio_noacct() (block/blk-core.c) installs a list head at current->bio_list before calling into the first driver; from then on any nested submit_bio_noacct() on this task merely appends to that list and returns, so a driver's recursive submission never actually recurses. The loop then drains the list, and it does so deliberately: bios aimed at a different queue than the one just processed are run before those aimed at the same one, which amounts to finishing the lower levels of the stack first and guarantees forward progress even when every level is waiting on the same exhausted mempool. The rest of this chapter therefore follows the request-based path, by far the common case for hardware drivers, beginning with the units the layer counts in.

Block Device Geometry: Sectors, Blocks, Segments, and Pages

Before following a transfer through the block layer, it helps to fix the four units the layer continually converts between, because each belongs to a different layer of the stack and confusing them is the source of much block-layer bewilderment. A sector is the hardware's unit of addressing; a block is the filesystem's unit of allocation; a segment is the block layer's unit of a contiguous region of memory within a transfer; and a page is the virtual-memory system's unit, the frame in which the page cache holds disk data. A single 4 KB page in the cache can simultaneously be seen as one page by the memory manager, several blocks by the filesystem, one or more segments by the block layer, and a run of sectors by the disk controller—all describing the same bytes from different vantage points. This section defines each unit and the ratios that tie them together.

Figure 14-3 makes the relationship concrete with the classic example. A 4,096-byte page in the page cache is divided by the filesystem into four 1,024-byte blocks. Suppose only the last three blocks need to be written: those 3,072 contiguous bytes form a single segment—one scatter/gather entry the driver can hand to the hardware in one DMA descriptor—which the disk controller in turn addresses as six consecutive 512-byte sectors. The same bytes are thus counted as one page, three blocks, one segment, and six sectors at once; the block layer is the component that knows all four views and translates among them.

The units are only half of what the block layer needs to know about a device. The other half is a set of limits—the largest transfer the hardware will accept, how many scatter/gather entries its DMA engine can take, what alignment it requires—and in Linux 7.0.12 all of them live together in one struct queue_limits (include/linux/blkdev.h). That consolidation is recent and worth noting: where a 2.6-era driver called a series of individual blk_queue_*() setters on a live queue, a modern driver fills in a queue_limits on the stack and passes it to blk_mq_alloc_disk() or blk_alloc_disk(), where blk_validate_limits() checks the whole set for internal consistency before the disk exists. Changing a limit afterward is an explicit transaction: queue_limits_start_update() returns a private copy, the driver edits it, and queue_limits_commit_update() validates and installs it against a frozen queue, so no bio is ever measured against a half-updated set of rules. Every limit discussed below has a read-only counterpart under /sys/block/<dev>/queue/, which makes the whole structure inspectable from the shell.

Sectors and the 512-Byte Abstraction

A sector is the smallest addressable unit of a block device, and throughout the kernel's generic block code a sector is always 512 bytes, regardless of the device's real sector size. This is a deliberate abstraction: the sector_t values in a bio (bi_iter.bi_sector) and a request (__sector) are counts of 512-byte units, so all the generic arithmetic—offsets, sizes, merging decisions—works in one fixed unit. Code shifts between bytes and sectors with SECTOR_SHIFT (9) and SECTOR_SIZE (512).

The device's actual smallest transfer unit may be larger. Modern disks and SSDs commonly use 4096-byte physical sectors ("Advanced Format"), and the block layer records this as the device's logical and physical block sizes in its queue limits, separate from the fixed 512-byte kernel sector. The 512-byte sector is thus a software convention for arithmetic; it is not a claim about the hardware.

Keeping the two apart avoids a common confusion. When a driver reports a 4096-byte logical block size, the kernel still numbers sectors in 512-byte units, so consecutive logical blocks are eight sectors apart: logical block n begins at sector n << 3. A bio that starts at bi_iter.bi_sector = 8 therefore begins at byte offset 4096, not 4096×8. Drivers that speak to hardware in native blocks convert with the device's own shift, but everything in the generic path—merging, splitting, accounting, and the values printed in /sys/block/<dev> and blktrace—stays in 512-byte sectors, which is why the abstraction is worth stating explicitly before any of that arithmetic appears.

Two details of the abstraction are easy to miss. The first is that SECTOR_SHIFT and SECTOR_SIZE are defined in include/linux/blk_types.h rather than anywhere architecture specific, because the 512-byte unit is a property of the block layer and not of any machine. The second is that sector_t is now unconditionally a u64: the 32-bit sector counts of the 2.6 era, and the CONFIG_LBD option that widened them, are gone, so 512-byte numbering addresses eight zebibytes on every configuration and the old 2 TB ceiling on 32-bit systems no longer exists.

The 512-byte unit is an addressing convention, not a permission. A bio must still start and end on a logical block boundary—on a 4Kn device, a bio at sector 3 or of length 1,536 bytes is simply invalid, and the block layer rejects it rather than rounding it. Alignment of the memory side is governed separately by dma_alignment in the queue limits, which defaults to 511 and tells the layer what buffer alignment the controller can tolerate; drivers that require more raise it, and passthrough paths that map user memory consult it before deciding whether a buffer can be used directly or must be bounced.

Logical and Physical Block Sizes

Two block sizes describe a device, and both live in its queue limits (struct queue_limits). The logical block size is the smallest unit the device can address—the granularity of a read or write, often 512 or 4096 bytes. The physical block size is the smallest unit the device actually reads or writes atomically internally, which on a 4Kn or 512e drive is 4096 bytes even when the logical size is 512. The distinction matters for performance and correctness: I/O aligned to the physical block size avoids the expensive read-modify-write cycle a drive must perform when asked to write less than a physical block, so filesystems query these limits (via bdev_logical_block_size() and bdev_physical_block_size()) and align their own block size and layout accordingly.

Above the device, the filesystem block is the unit the filesystem allocates and caches in—typically 4096 bytes to match the page size, and never smaller than the logical block size. A filesystem read of one 4 KB block therefore becomes eight 512-byte kernel sectors in the bio, which the block layer may in turn map to one 4 KB device access. The three sizes—kernel sector, device block, filesystem block—line up through these ratios rather than being the same number.

Two block sizes are not quite enough to describe a modern device, so the queue limits carry three more numbers that together are called the device's topology. alignment_offset records how far the device's first physical block is displaced from sector zero—the classic case is a partition table that starts a partition at sector 63, leaving every 4 KB write straddling two physical blocks for the life of the disk. io_min is the smallest I/O the device prefers, and io_opt the size at which it performs best; on a RAID array these are the chunk size and the full stripe width, so a filesystem that respects them writes whole stripes and never forces a parity read-modify-write. The three appear in sysfs as alignment_offset, minimum_io_size, and optimal_io_size, and drivers and filesystems read them through bdev_alignment_offset(), bdev_io_min(), and bdev_io_opt(). This is why mkfs on a hardware RAID volume picks a different layout than on a single disk without being told to.

Topology also has to survive stacking. When device-mapper or MD builds a virtual device it does not invent limits; it derives them from the devices underneath with blk_stack_limits() and its convenience wrapper queue_limits_stack_bdev() (block/blk-settings.c). The combining rule differs per field and follows from what each field means: maxima such as max_sectors and max_segments take the minimum across members, because the stack can only promise what its weakest member can do; block sizes and io_min take the maximum; and io_opt and alignment_offset are combined with a least common multiple, since an offset that suits all members must be a multiple of each. If the members cannot be reconciled—a stripe whose components start at different physical-block offsets—the function marks the resulting device misaligned rather than silently producing a topology that would make every write a read-modify-write, and the administrator sees it in sysfs.

A concrete rule of thumb captures the hierarchy: logical block size ≤ physical block size ≤ filesystem block size = page size is the common, best-performing arrangement, and each step is a whole-number multiple of the one below. On a typical x86_64 or ARM64 system that means 512-byte logical blocks (or 4096), 4096-byte physical blocks, a 4096-byte filesystem block, and a 4096-byte page—so a single cached page maps to exactly one filesystem block and one physical device block, and no read-modify-write is ever forced. A filesystem is free to use a smaller block (1024 or 2048 bytes, as in the Figure 14-3 example), but then one page holds several blocks and the block layer must track them individually. One line of that rule of thumb has been redrawn since Linux 2.6, and it is worth knowing which. For twenty years a filesystem block could not exceed the page size, because the page cache had no way to hold a block in one piece. Large folios removed that restriction: a filesystem can now declare a minimum folio order for its inodes with mapping_set_folio_min_order(), so the page cache allocates compound pages large enough that one block is still one cache entry. XFS uses this to mount filesystems whose block size exceeds PAGE_SIZE (fs/xfs/xfs_super.c), up to the largest folio the kernel supports. The practical effect for a block-layer reader is that filesystem block = page size is now the common case rather than an invariant, and code that assumes a bio's data arrives one page at a time is wrong twice over—once because of large folios, and once because of the multipage bio_vec described in the next section.

Segments

A single I/O request may gather data from several non-contiguous areas of physical memory—for example, pages scattered by the page cache—even though the data is contiguous on disk. A segment is one such contiguous region of memory participating in a transfer. In the block layer, each segment corresponds to a bio_vec (a page, offset, and length) within a bio; the segments of a request are exactly the scatter/gather list the driver will hand to the hardware's DMA engine, tying this directly back to the scatter/gather discussion of Chapter 13.

Segments are why merging is more than concatenation. When the block layer merges two bios into one request, it also counts how many distinct memory segments result, because every device has a limit on how many scatter/gather entries its DMA engine can handle (max_segments) and on the largest single segment (max_segment_size), both in the queue limits. If a bio's pages happen to be physically adjacent they collapse into one segment; if not, each is its own. A request is therefore bounded on two independent axes—total bytes and segment count—and the block layer respects both when deciding whether a merge is allowed, a constraint the next section's bio details make concrete.

There is a third axis that the segment count and the transfer size do not capture, and it catches driver authors by surprise. Some controllers—NVMe most prominently, because its physical region page lists describe memory as a series of page-aligned entries—cannot express a scatter/gather list in which one entry ends partway through a page and the next begins partway into another. The queue limits express this as virt_boundary_mask, and the block layer enforces it by looking for a gap between consecutive bio_vec entries: bvec_gap_to_prev() and bio_will_gap() (block/blk-merge.c) reject a merge, and force a split, whenever the previous entry does not end on the boundary or the next does not start on it. A transfer can therefore be split even though it is well under max_sectors and well under max_segments, purely because of where its buffers happen to sit within their pages. A related but distinct limit, seg_boundary_mask, forbids a single segment from crossing an address boundary that the controller cannot express—the classic 64 KB DMA boundary of older hardware.

A subtlety worth carrying forward is that a segment is a property of memory, not of the disk. Two bios whose sectors are adjacent on disk merge into one request, but whether that request has one segment or two depends entirely on whether their pages are contiguous in RAM. This is also where the multipage bio_vec described in the next section pays off: because one bio_vec can already span several physically-contiguous pages, a large transfer often needs far fewer segments than the old one-page-per-bio_vec scheme, staying comfortably under max_segments and letting the driver describe the whole transfer in a single, compact scatter/gather list. When memory really is scattered, the IOMMU (Chapter 13) can still present it to the device as one contiguous DMA window, coalescing many segments into one—another reason the segment count is a soft, hardware- and platform-dependent limit rather than a fixed one.

The bio Structure

The bio is the fundamental container of block I/O in flight. One bio describes a single contiguous transfer on the device—a start sector and a length—together with the list of memory segments that supply or receive the data. Every read or write that reaches the block layer, from a page-cache writeback to a direct-I/O request, is expressed as one or more bios. This section covers the structure, how its data is described by an iterator, how a bio is built and submitted, and how large bios are split and chained.

It helps to understand what the bio replaced. In the 2.4 kernel the unit of block I/O was the buffer head (struct buffer_head), which described a single block tied to a single memory buffer; a large transfer meant many buffer heads, each individually submitted. The bio was introduced precisely to break that one-block-one-buffer limitation: a single bio can gather many pages of memory into one device transfer, which is what makes scatter/gather DMA, direct I/O, and efficient writeback possible. Buffer heads still exist—filesystems use them to track the state of cached blocks—but they are no longer the currency of the block layer; the bio is. Keeping this history in mind explains why the structure is organized around an array of memory segments rather than a single buffer.

One more thing to expect before reading the definition. The bio is not only a description of a transfer; it is the anchor for the cross-cutting services introduced at the start of this chapter, and each of them adds a field. A bio carries the cgroup it was charged to (bi_blkg) and when it was issued (issue_time_ns) when CONFIG_BLK_CGROUP is on, an inline-encryption key (bi_crypt_context), an integrity payload (bi_integrity), an I/O priority (bi_ioprio), a write lifetime hint (bi_write_hint), and a back-pointer to the bio_set it must be returned to (bi_pool). Most of these sit behind configuration options, which is why the structure looks different from one kernel build to the next and why the listing that follows is abridged to the fields the block layer always uses.

A detail that changed since Linux 2.6 is the multipage bvec. In older kernels each bio_vec described at most one page, so a large transfer needed many entries. Today a single bio_vec can span several physically-contiguous pages, so one entry often covers a whole range and bios carry far fewer segments—a significant efficiency gain on modern high-throughput devices. The same evolution now extends to folios: bio_add_folio() lets a caller attach a multi-page folio in one step, and iteration helpers hide whether the underlying storage is a single page or a compound folio. The packing of bi_opf is worth a sentence of its own, because it explains an oddity in the source. The low REQ_OP_BITS (eight) bits hold the operation, extracted with REQ_OP_MASK or, more usually, with the bio_op() accessor; everything above them is modifier flags. The operation numbers are not consecutive—REQ_OP_READ is 0, REQ_OP_WRITE is 1, REQ_OP_DISCARD is 3, REQ_OP_SECURE_ERASE is 5—and the gaps are deliberate: every operation that modifies the device is given an odd number, so op_is_write() is a single test of the lowest bit rather than a list of comparisons. A great deal of code on the hot path asks nothing more than "does this change the device?", and this encoding answers it in one instruction.

It is filled in only after a bio has been split to the hardware limits. Its purpose is the virt_boundary_mask test of the previous section: because that test only asks whether any offset bit collides with a power-of-two mask, remembering the lowest such bit is enough to answer it, and the DMA mapping code can decide whether a transfer is eligible for an optimized path by comparing one byte instead of walking the segment list again.

The bvec Iterator

The block layer never consumes a bio all at once; it advances through it. The bvec_iter (include/linux/bvec.h) is the cursor that makes this possible without modifying the underlying bio_vec array, so a partially-completed bio, or one shared between a split parent and child, still has intact segment data. Its fields are the current position expressed four ways: bi_sector (the device sector reached so far), bi_size (bytes remaining to transfer), bi_idx (index of the current bio_vec), and bi_bvec_done (bytes already consumed within that bio_vec). 

struct bvec_iter {
        sector_t        bi_sector;      /* device sector reached */
        unsigned int    bi_size;        /* bytes left to transfer */
        unsigned int    bi_idx;         /* current bio_vec index */
        unsigned int    bi_bvec_done;   /* bytes done in current bio_vec */
};

Keeping the cursor separate from the data is what makes cheap bio operations possible. bio_advance() moves the iterator forward by a number of bytes as a transfer completes; bio_split() produces a child that shares the parent's bio_vec array but has its own iterator covering the first part of the range. The design principle behind this is that the bio_vec array is immutable once the bio is built: all mutable state—how far the transfer has progressed—lives in the iterator, never in the segments themselves. That immutability is what lets a split child and its parent safely share one bio_vec array, and what lets a partially-completed bio be re-inspected without loss. It is also why there are two families of iteration macro. bio_for_each_segment() yields one page-sized bio_vec at a time and is what a driver building a scatter/gather list for hardware that works in pages should use; bio_for_each_bvec() yields whole multipage bvecs and is used inside the block layer where larger chunks are wanted. A third form, bio_for_each_segment_all(), walks the raw array directly and may only be used before submission or after completion—when no iterator is advancing—because it ignores bi_iter entirely. Choosing the wrong one is a classic source of bugs: _all on an in-flight bio, or a page iterator where a bvec iterator was meant, silently mis-walks the data.

There is a mechanical detail behind that warning that explains why the mistake is so easy to make. The two in-flight macros yield a struct bio_vec by value—the loop variable is a copy synthesized from the immutable array and the current iterator position, clipped to what remains of the transfer—whereas bio_for_each_segment_all() yields a struct bio_vec * pointing straight into bi_io_vec. The declarations therefore differ, and writing through the loop variable means one thing in one form and something entirely different in the other. A fourth macro, bio_for_each_folio_all(), walks the same raw array a folio at a time and is what filesystem completion handlers use when they want to mark whole folios uptodate or dirty without caring how many pages each one holds.

Building and Submitting a bio

A bio is built, submitted, and then completed asynchronously. The submitter allocates it (bio_alloc() or bio_alloc_bioset() from a dedicated mempool, so I/O can proceed even under memory pressure), sets the target device and operation, appends memory with bio_add_page(), and installs a bi_end_io completion callback. It then calls submit_bio(), and control returns immediately—the transfer happens later, and bi_end_io runs when it finishes. 

bio = bio_alloc(bdev, nr_pages, REQ_OP_READ, GFP_KERNEL);
bio->bi_iter.bi_sector = start_sector;      /* 512-byte units */
bio_add_page(bio, page, len, offset);       /* add a segment */
bio->bi_end_io = my_endio;
bio->bi_private = ctx;
submit_bio(bio);                            /* returns void; completes later */

A change worth noting is that submit_bio() now returns void (block/blk-core.c). In the 2.6 era it returned a value and the device was reached through a per-queue make_request_fn; today it simply hands the bio to submit_bio_noacct(), which after accounting and any needed splitting calls __submit_bio()—dispatching either to blk_mq_submit_bio() for a normal request-based driver or to the driver's own submit_bio method for a stacking, bio-based driver such as device-mapper or a RAID target. Completion always flows back through bio_endio(), which invokes bi_end_io.

The way memory is attached deserves more than the one line of the sketch, because bio_add_page() does more than fill the next slot. It returns the number of bytes it accepted—zero if it could not take them—and it is marked __must_check for that reason. Before consuming a slot it tries to extend the last bio_vec in place: if the new page is physically adjacent to the memory that entry already describes, the entry's bv_len simply grows and bi_vcnt does not move. This is where multipage bvecs actually come from—not from a caller asking for them, but from ordinary page-by-page appending collapsing naturally whenever the pages happen to be contiguous. bio_add_folio() does the same for a folio and returns a bool, while __bio_add_page() and bio_add_folio_nofail() are the no-fail variants for callers that already sized the array correctly and would treat a refusal as a bug.

Direct I/O fills a bio by a different route entirely, and it is the one path where a bio points at memory the kernel does not own. bio_iov_iter_get_pages() takes an iov_iter describing a user buffer, pins the pages behind it, and appends them to the bio in one call, setting the BIO_PAGE_PINNED flag. The pin is what keeps the pages from being migrated, swapped, or freed while the device is writing into them; the completion path must undo it with bio_release_pages(), which also marks the pages dirty for a read. A bio built this way is capped at BIO_MAX_VECS (256) entries, so a large direct-I/O request becomes several bios rather than one enormous one.

Not every bio comes from bio_alloc(), either. A caller that already has storage—a bio embedded in a larger driver structure, or one on the stack—initializes it with bio_init(), supplying its own bio_vec array, or with bio_init_inline() to use the entries that follow the structure. bio_reset() re-arms a bio for another transfer while preserving the bi_io_vec allocation, which is how a driver that issues a long series of similar transfers avoids repeated allocation altogether, and bio_kmalloc() serves the rare caller that wants a bio from the general allocator rather than from a bio_set.

Two aspects of the lifecycle are easy to get wrong and worth stating plainly. First, ownership of the bio passes to the block layer at submission: after submit_bio() the submitter must not touch the bio again except from within its bi_end_io callback, because the transfer may already have completed on another CPU by the time the call returns. The callback is where the submitter reads bi_status (BLK_STS_OK on success, or an error such as BLK_STS_IOERR), does any post-processing, and typically calls bio_put() to release its reference. Second, the gfp_mask passed to bio_alloc() matters: I/O issued from a writeback or reclaim path must use GFP_NOIO so that allocating the bio cannot recurse back into the filesystem and deadlock, whereas ordinary submission contexts can use GFP_KERNEL. Because the allocation draws from a dedicated bio_set mempool, it is guaranteed to make forward progress even when memory is scarce—the reason block I/O can proceed during the very memory pressure that triggered it. For callers that want to skip the allocator on the hot path entirely, a per-CPU bio cache recycles bios for high-IOPS workloads such as direct I/O to NVMe: a bio_set created with BIOSET_PERCPU_CACHE hands out bios marked REQ_ALLOC_CACHE, and bio_put() returns them to the current CPU's free list rather than to the allocator.

Splitting and Chaining

A submitted bio can be larger than the target device is willing to handle in one request—it may exceed max_sectors, cross a chunk boundary on a RAID device, or contain more segments than the DMA engine allows. The block layer therefore splits it. bio_split() allocates a child bio covering the first part of the range, sharing the parent's bio_vec array but with its own iterator, and advances the parent past that part; __bio_split_to_limits() applies this automatically against the queue's limits before a bio becomes a request. The pieces are submitted independently.

Two properties of bio_split() in Linux 7.0.12 differ from what a reader of 2.6 code, or even of a recent kernel, might assume. First, it can fail, and it reports failure as an error pointer: callers must test the result with IS_ERR() rather than for NULL, since besides running out of memory it rejects a nonsensical split point outright. Second, not every bio may be split at all. A REQ_OP_ZONE_APPEND bio cannot be, because the device chooses the write position and a fragment would land somewhere the caller never learns about; a REQ_ATOMIC write cannot be, because splitting is precisely the all-or-nothing violation the flag exists to prevent. Both are refused rather than quietly divided, which is why the queue limits carry a separate family of atomic_write_* and max_zone_append_sectors values—a transfer that cannot be split has to be validated as acceptable before it is ever submitted.

Splitting requires a way to complete a logical operation only once all its pieces finish, and that is what chaining provides. bio_chain() links a child bio to a parent so that the parent's completion is deferred until every child has completed, using the parent's __bi_remaining counter. A caller can thus submit an arbitrarily large or fragmented transfer as one parent bio, let the block layer split it into device-sized children, and receive a single completion callback when the whole thing is done. This split-and-chain machinery is invisible to the filesystem above and to the driver below, each of which sees only appropriately-sized bios—the block layer absorbs the mismatch.

The mechanics are worth making precise because __bi_remaining is easy to confuse with the reference count. When bio_chain() attaches a child, it points the child's bi_end_io at bio_chain_endio() and increments the parent's __bi_remaining. Each child that completes decrements that counter through bio_endio(); only when it reaches zero does the parent's own bi_end_io finally run. The parent is therefore not completed by the submitter directly—it is completed by the last child to finish. A consequence is error propagation: if any child fails, bio_chain_endio() copies its bi_status up to the parent, so a single failed fragment surfaces as a failure of the whole logical transfer, exactly as a caller would expect.

That design has a hazard of its own, and the implementation of bio_endio() shows how it is handled. Completing a child calls the parent's completion, which on a long chain would call its parent, and so on; a deeply split transfer could therefore unwind through as many stack frames as it had pieces. bio_endio() avoids this by recognizing that a bio's bi_end_io is bio_chain_endio() and looping back to the top of itself with the parent instead of calling into it—a hand-written tail call. The same function is also where a bio's cross-cutting state is finally released: cgroup accounting is closed out, the integrity payload is verified, zone bookkeeping is updated, and the completion is traced, all before the owner's bi_end_io is allowed to run.

Splitting also interacts with the plugging mechanism covered later in the chapter. Rather than submitting each split child straight to the driver, the block layer can stage them on the current task's plug list and flush them as a batch, which both preserves their natural order and gives the merge logic a chance to recombine adjacent pieces before they reach the hardware. Splitting to satisfy a hardware limit and merging to build larger requests are thus two sides of the same coin—the layer continually breaks transfers down to what the device accepts while building them back up to the largest efficient unit—and the bio, with its shared bio_vec array and private iterators, is the structure that makes both cheap.

Representing Disks: gendisk and block_device

Two structures represent storage to the kernel. A gendisk ("generic disk") represents a whole physical device—a disk, an SSD, an NVMe namespace—while a block_device represents something that can be opened: either the whole disk or one of its partitions. Roughly, the gendisk is the hardware and the block_device is the handle userspace holds. This section covers both, the operations table that drives them, and how a driver registers a disk.

The relationship is best seen as a small object graph, shown in Figure 14-4. Each driver instance owns one gendisk per device it exports—for example sda for a SATA disk and nvme0n1 for the first namespace of an NVMe controller. Every gendisk owns exactly one request_queue (its blk-mq queue, through which all of that device's I/O flows) and a table of block_device objects: one for the whole disk—named after the disk itself (sda, nvme0n1) and reachable through the gendisk's part0 pointer—plus one entry in the part_tbl xarray for each partition (sda1, sda2, nvme0n1p1). Each of those block_device objects is what a dev_t in /dev resolves to and what a filesystem opens; the whole-disk object spans the entire device, while each partition object carries its own start sector and length, so the same underlying disk presents several independent, zero-based address spaces upward while sharing one queue and one driver downward. The figure makes the fan-out concrete for two devices at once: the top row is the /dev nodes, below them the block_device objects (the whole-disk one labeled with the disk name), then the owning gendisk, and finally the single shared request_queue that carries every partition's I/O to the driver.

A gendisk is more than a passive record; it is the anchor that ties the device into three different kernel subsystems at once. The private_data pointer is the driver's hook back to its own per-device structure, retrieved in every operation and in queue_rq.

The flags and state fields deserve unpacking, because both have been pruned hard since Linux 2.6. Only three GENHD_FL_ flags remain. GENHD_FL_REMOVABLE marks media that can be changed; GENHD_FL_NO_PART says the device is never partitioned, which suppresses scanning altogether; and GENHD_FL_HIDDEN makes the disk invisible—no /dev node, no uevents, no partition scan—which is how NVMe multipath hides the individual paths to a namespace behind the single nvme0n1 device that userspace is meant to use. Everything else that used to be a flag is now a bit in state, set and tested at runtime: GD_ADDED once add_disk() has succeeded, GD_READ_ONLY for a write-protected disk, GD_NEED_PART_SCAN to request a rescan at the next open, GD_DEAD once the device has gone away, and GD_OWNS_QUEUE to record whether the disk must tear its request queue down with it.

Two fields in the real structure are worth naming even though they rarely appear in driver code. Each gendisk carries its own bio_split bio_set: the mempool the block layer draws from when it has to split a bio for this device. Giving every disk a private pool is what keeps a split on one device from being starved by traffic on another—the forward-progress argument of section 1, applied per device. And diskseq is a globally monotonic sequence number stamped on every gendisk ever created. It exists because a device name is not a stable identity: unplug a USB disk and plug in a different one, and the second may well be called /dev/sda too. A tool that recorded the disk's diskseq—from /sys/block/sda/diskseq, from the DISKSEQ= variable in the uevent, or through the BLKGETDISKSEQ ioctl—can tell the two apart, closing a race that plagued udev rules for years.

struct block_device and Partitions

struct block_device (include/linux/blk_types.h) represents an openable block object. Every disk has at least one—part0, the whole device—and each partition is another block_device with a nonzero partition number. The key fields tie it back to its disk and locate it on the media: bd_disk points at the owning gendisk, bd_dev is its dev_t, bd_start_sect and bd_nr_sectors give the partition's offset and length within the disk, and bd_openers counts how many times it is currently open. 

struct block_device {
        sector_t          bd_start_sect;   /* partition start within disk */
        sector_t          bd_nr_sectors;   /* partition size */
        struct gendisk    *bd_disk;        /* owning disk */
        struct request_queue *bd_queue;
        dev_t             bd_dev;
        atomic_t          bd_openers;      /* current open count */
        void              *bd_holder;      /* exclusive owner, if any */
        ...
};

This partition model is the reason a filesystem on /dev/sda2 transparently reads only within that partition. When a request is submitted to a partition's block_device, the block layer remaps its sector by adding bd_start_sect before the I/O reaches the driver (in blk_partition_remap()), so the driver always works in whole-disk sectors while each filesystem sees a private, zero-based address space. Partitions are discovered by scanning the disk (bdev_disk_changed()), which parses the partition table and calls add_partition() to create a block_device for each, inserting it into the disk's part_tbl. Per- partition I/O statistics (the numbers behind /proc/diskstats and iostat) are kept through the part_stat accounting on each block_device.

The bd_holder field implements exclusive ownership, which matters whenever a subsystem must not share a device with anyone else. When a filesystem mounts /dev/sda2, or LVM claims a physical volume, it opens the device with a holder token (bdev_file_open_by_dev() with BLK_OPEN_EXCL, which replaced the older blkdev_get_by_dev() and FMODE_EXCL); a second exclusive open with a different holder then fails with -EBUSY. This is why one cannot, for instance, mkfs a partition that is currently mounted—the mount holds it exclusively. The bd_openers count, by contrast, tracks all opens (shared and exclusive) so the driver's release method runs only when the very last user closes the device, whether that user was a mount, a blkid probe, or a raw open("/dev/sda") from userspace.

Claiming a device turns out to require more than a token, and this is where Linux 7.0.12 differs most from Linux 2.6. Alongside bd_holder the structure carries a bd_holder_ops pointer—a small table of callbacks the holder supplies when it claims the device. The important one is mark_dead(), which the block layer invokes through bdev_mark_dead() when the device disappears, with a flag saying whether the removal was a surprise. A mounted filesystem can therefore be told that its storage has gone rather than discovering it through a storm of I/O errors, and it can shut itself down cleanly. The same table carries sync(), freeze(), and thaw(), which let the block layer drive a filesystem freeze from below—device-mapper uses them to quiesce a mounted filesystem before swapping a table underneath it. A driver announces a vanished device with blk_mark_disk_dead(), and del_gendisk() does the same on the orderly path.

Exclusivity has also grown a second, gentler form. BLK_OPEN_EXCL is all or nothing: one holder, and every other exclusive open fails. But that is stronger than a mounted filesystem actually needs—it has no objection to blkid reading the device, only to something writing over it. BLK_OPEN_RESTRICT_WRITES expresses exactly that: it excludes other writers while leaving readers alone, tracked by the bd_writers count, and it is what the VFS now uses when mounting a filesystem. The result is that dd onto a mounted partition is refused while a read-only probe of the same partition still succeeds.

One detail of the real structure will surprise a reader comparing it with the listing above: the partition number and several booleans are not separate fields. They are packed into a single atomic word, __bd_flags, whose low eight bits hold the partition number (BD_PARTNO) and whose upper bits carry BD_READ_ONLY, BD_HAS_SUBMIT_BIO, and friends, all read and written through accessors. Similarly, the page cache for a block device is reached through bd_mapping, an address_space pointer, rather than through the bd_inode back-pointer older kernels carried. Partition rescanning is likewise indirect: setting GD_NEED_PART_SCAN—which the BLKRRPART ioctl and media-change detection both do—defers the actual work to the next open, where bdev_disk_changed() runs it.

It is worth being explicit about the division of labor between the two structures, because they are easy to conflate. The gendisk is created and destroyed by the driver and models the hardware; the block_device objects are created by the block core as partitions are discovered and model the openable views. A driver almost never allocates a block_device itself—it calls set_capacity() and lets partition scanning populate part_tbl. Conversely, userspace and filesystems almost never see a gendisk directly; they hold a block_device (via an open file) and reach the gendisk only indirectly through bd_disk. The submit_bio entry deserves emphasis because it is what splits the two driver styles introduced in section 1. Two further points are easy to miss: open and release in modern kernels take a struct gendisk * (not a block_device), reflecting that they concern the whole disk rather than a single partition; and check_events is the modern replacement for the old media_changed/revalidate_disk pair, polled by the block core to notice removable-media insertion or an eject button so the disk can be revalidated and its partitions rescanned.

The table is also longer than a driver usually needs, and the extra entries are worth knowing because each corresponds to a capability the kernel cannot infer. A zoned device must supply report_zones; without it the block layer has no way to learn where the write pointers are. And pr_ops exposes persistent reservations, the mechanism a cluster uses to fence a failed node off from shared storage. None of these are optional in the sense of being cosmetic; a driver that omits one is declaring that the device does not have that capability.

Registering a Disk

Bringing a disk online is a two-call sequence in modern kernels, and it differs markedly from the 2.6-era alloc_disk() + blk_init_queue() pair. First the driver allocates the gendisk and its blk-mq request queue together with blk_mq_alloc_disk(), passing a tag set (introduced in the next section) that configures the queue's hardware queues and depth, and a queue_limits that declares the device's geometry. It fills in the disk's name, fops, and capacity (set_capacity()), then publishes it with add_disk() (or device_add_disk()). Only after add_disk() returns is the disk visible in /dev and open to I/O—so, as with a character device's cdev_add(), everything the disk needs must be ready first. 

struct queue_limits lim = {
        .logical_block_size = 512,
        .max_hw_sectors     = 2048,
};

disk = blk_mq_alloc_disk(&mydev->tag_set, &lim, mydev);  /* disk + queue */
if (IS_ERR(disk))
        return PTR_ERR(disk);

disk->major        = my_major;
disk->first_minor  = 0;
disk->minors       = 16;
disk->fops         = &my_bdops;
disk->private_data = mydev;
snprintf(disk->disk_name, DISK_NAME_LEN, "mydisk");
set_capacity(disk, nr_sectors);                 /* size in 512-byte sectors */

ret = add_disk(disk);                           /* go live */
...
/* teardown: */
del_gendisk(disk);
put_disk(disk);

Teardown reverses this: del_gendisk() removes the disk and its partitions from the system so no new opens or I/O can start, and put_disk() drops the reference that frees it once the last user is gone. The disk's request queue is torn down along with it. With the disk represented and registered, the remaining question is how its request queue actually moves I/O—the subject of the multi-queue block layer.

Two ordering constraints in this sequence are the source of most registration bugs. First, everything the disk exposes must be set before add_disk(): the moment it returns, userspace can open the device, partition scanning may run, and I/O can arrive, so a capacity, fops, or queue limit filled in afterward races with real users. This mirrors the character-device rule that cdev_add() must come last. Second, add_disk() can now fail (it returns an int and is marked __must_check)—a change from the void add_disk() of older kernels—so a driver must check the return and unwind, because a half-added disk left behind on error corrupts the block core's state. The pairing is strict: blk_mq_alloc_disk() is undone by put_disk(), and a successful add_disk() is undone by del_gendisk(); on the error path after a failed add_disk() only put_disk() is called, never del_gendisk(). Because blk_mq_alloc_disk() allocates the queue from a tag set, the gendisk and its blk-mq queue are created and destroyed as a unit, which is why teardown never touches the queue directly.

Two conveniences round out the sequence. add_disk() is a thin wrapper over device_add_disk(parent, disk, groups), and the two extra arguments matter for anything more than a toy driver: the parent device is what places the disk under its controller in the sysfs tree, so that /sys/block/sda is a symlink into the SCSI or PCI hierarchy rather than an orphan, and groups attaches driver-specific sysfs attributes before the add uevent fires, so a udev rule never sees a half-populated directory. Second, capacity is not fixed for the life of the disk: a device that grows—a resized logical volume, a thin-provisioned LUN—calls set_capacity_and_notify() instead of set_capacity(), which updates the size and emits a change uevent so that userspace and any mounted filesystem learn about it.

Teardown has an ordering of its own once a device can vanish without warning. On the orderly path del_gendisk() is enough: it marks the disk dead, stops new opens, drains outstanding I/O, and notifies every holder through the mark_dead() callback described earlier. On a surprise removal the driver should call blk_mark_disk_dead() first, as soon as it knows the hardware is gone. That immediately fails I/O that would otherwise wait forever for a device that will never answer, letting the subsequent del_gendisk() complete instead of hanging on a drain that cannot finish—the difference between a cleanly reported unplug and a stuck umount.

The Multi-Queue Block Layer

The multi-queue block layer, universally called blk-mq, is the core of the modern block subsystem and the largest single change from Linux 2.6. It replaced a design that could not scale, and doing so touched every driver. This section explains why the change was necessary, the two-level queue structure at its heart, the tag-based request model, and the operations a driver implements.

Before the details, it helps to see how blk-mq's pieces fit together as software. Figure 14-5 lays out the architecture. On the left are the two objects a driver supplies: a blk_mq_tag_set that declares the device's queue geometry (how many hardware queues, how deep, how much per-request scratch) and a blk_mq_ops table of callbacks, chief among them queue_rq. From these the block core builds a request_queue—the blk-mq engine—whose internal stages are the same for every driver: bios are turned into requests, staged on per-CPU software queues, optionally ordered by an I/O scheduler, and dispatched through per-hardware-queue contexts that draw a tag from an sbitmap pool before calling the driver's queue_rq. The driver supplies only the two left-hand objects and the hardware-specific body of queue_rq; everything inside the engine is shared kernel code. The subsections that follow open up each stage in turn.

Why blk-mq Replaced the Single Queue

The 2.6-era block layer gave each device one request_queue, protected by a single spinlock (the "queue lock"). Every I/O submission, every merge, every dispatch, and every completion took that one lock. When disks were slow and systems had one or two CPUs, this was fine—the lock was rarely contended. But two trends broke it. Devices became enormously faster: an NVMe SSD can service well over a million I/O operations per second and exposes multiple independent hardware submission queues. And systems grew to dozens or hundreds of CPUs, all submitting I/O concurrently. Funneling that through one lock serialized the whole system on a single contended cache line, wasting most of the device's capability.

blk-mq removes the single lock by giving I/O submission per-CPU locality and matching the kernel's queues to the device's real hardware queues. There is no global queue lock on the fast path; a CPU submits into its own software queue, and each hardware queue is dispatched independently. Alongside this, the old request_fn strategy routine and the single-queue elevator were deleted outright: as of Linux 5.0 there is no legacy path left, and every block driver is either request-based (implementing blk-mq's queue_rq) or purely bio-based (implementing the submit_bio method for stacking drivers). The rest of this chapter describes only the blk-mq world because it is the only one that exists.

It is worth appreciating that the benefit is not merely "more queues." The single-queue design serialized independent work: two CPUs operating on unrelated sectors of the same device still collided on the same lock and the same cache line, so throughput flattened long before the hardware was saturated. blk-mq's insight is that most of the block layer's per-request work—allocation, merging, plugging, tag assignment—can be done with data structures that are private to a CPU or to a hardware queue, so it needs no shared lock at all. Locks reappear only where genuinely shared state is touched, such as a scheduler's global ordering, and even those are per-hardware-queue rather than per-device. The result scales with both the CPU count and the device's own queue count, which is exactly what a million-IOPS NVMe drive on a 64-core server demands. This architecture also mirrors how the hardware itself works: NVMe defines paired submission/completion queues per CPU, so blk-mq's hctx-per-hardware-queue model maps onto the silicon almost one-to-one.

One consequence is easy to overlook: blk-mq is not only for fast devices. A SATA disk with a single hardware queue, an SD card, a loop device, and a network block device all go through the same engine, and the two-level structure simply degenerates—every software queue maps onto the one hctx, and the tag pool is as shallow as the hardware demands. The design accommodates slow devices as gracefully as fast ones, and it accommodates one more case the single-queue layer never could: a driver whose submission path must sleep. A device reached over a network or a bus with its own blocking API sets BLK_MQ_F_BLOCKING in its tag set, and blk-mq then calls queue_rq from a context where sleeping is legal, taking the SRCU-based synchronization that makes queue freezing safe in that case. NBD, the MMC block driver, and virtio-over-vsock all rely on this; without it they would need a private thread between the block layer and the hardware.

Software and Hardware Queues

blk-mq is a two-level queue system, shown in Figure 14-6. At the top are the software staging queues (struct blk_mq_ctx), one per CPU. A thread submitting I/O inserts its request into the software queue of the CPU it is running on, with no cross-CPU lock—this is where the per-CPU locality comes from, and where merging and plugging happen. At the bottom are the hardware dispatch queues (struct blk_mq_hw_ctx, called the "hctx"), one per hardware queue the device actually exposes: a simple disk has one, a multi-queue NVMe drive has one per CPU. Each hctx is what feeds the driver.

The mapping from software queues to hardware queues is fixed at queue setup by the driver's map_queues callback (or a default), so each CPU always dispatches to the same hctx—preserving cache locality and, on NVMe, letting a CPU submit to and be interrupted by the same hardware queue. When there are fewer hardware queues than CPUs, several software queues share one hctx; when the device has one hardware queue per CPU, the mapping is one-to-one and there is effectively no sharing at all.

The division of labor between the two levels is deliberate. The software queue is a per-CPU staging area: because only one CPU touches it, a thread can insert a request, attempt a merge with an adjacent one, and accumulate a plug list without taking any cross-CPU lock. The hardware queue is the dispatch point: it owns the tag pool, holds the list of requests ready for the device, and is the single place from which queue_rq is called for its hardware queue. Moving requests from the software level to the hardware level (blk_mq_run_hw_queue()) is what actually drives dispatch, and it happens when a plug is flushed, when the scheduler releases requests, or when the driver signals it can accept more after a previous BLK_STS_RESOURCE. blk-mq can also run a hardware queue on a specific CPU to keep completion interrupts local. In the common modern case—an NVMe drive with one hardware queue per CPU and no separate scheduler—a request is submitted, tagged, and dispatched almost entirely within one CPU's own structures, which is the whole point of the design.

The picture of "one hctx per hardware queue" is also a simplification, because a device may expose several kinds of hardware queue. The tag set carries an array of queue maps, map[HCTX_MAX_TYPES], with nr_maps saying how many are in use, and the three types are HCTX_TYPE_DEFAULT, HCTX_TYPE_READ, and HCTX_TYPE_POLL. An NVMe controller configured with dedicated read queues gets a second set of hctxs used only for reads, so a burst of large writes cannot head-of-line block a latency-sensitive read behind it in the same hardware queue. A third set can be reserved for polling: those queues have no interrupt at all, and completions are reaped by the submitter through the poll callback. The CPU-to-hctx lookup therefore takes the operation into account as well as the CPU, which is why the mapping is an array of maps rather than a single table.

Building that mapping well matters more than it might appear, and drivers rarely do it by hand. blk_mq_map_hw_queues() derives the mapping from the controller's own interrupt affinity, so the CPU that submits a request is the CPU the completion interrupt is delivered to—the request, its data, and its completion then stay in one CPU's caches from beginning to end. The mapping is not permanent, either: a device whose queue count changes, or a system where CPUs come and go, calls blk_mq_update_nr_hw_queues() to rebuild it, and when a CPU is taken offline blk-mq drains the hctxs that were mapped to it before letting the hotplug proceed, so no request is stranded on a queue nothing will run.

struct request and Tags

Where a bio is a transfer as submitted, a request (struct request, include/linux/blk-mq.h) is a unit of work as dispatched to the driver. A request carries a chain of one or more merged bios (bio … biotail), the start sector (__sector) and total length (__data_len), the operation flags (cmd_flags), and—crucially—a tag. The request also records which software and hardware queue it belongs to (mq_ctx, mq_hctx) and a completion callback (end_io).

The tag is the mechanism that replaces the old queue lock's role of tracking outstanding work. Every hardware queue owns a fixed-size pool of tags—a bitmap sized to the queue's queue_depth—and each request, when it is about to be dispatched, is assigned a free tag from that pool. The tag is a small integer that uniquely identifies the request while it is in flight, so the driver and device can refer to it cheaply (an NVMe command, for example, carries its tag as the command identifier). Because the tag pool is bounded, tags also provide natural flow control: when every tag is in use the queue is at its depth and new dispatch waits, without any global counter or lock. Allocating and freeing tags from a per-CPU-friendly sbitmap is what lets many CPUs get requests in flight concurrently. Figure 14-7 shows the tag pool and how a tag maps to a preallocated request through its allocate–dispatch– complete–free cycle.

There are two further points that make the request/tag model click into place. First, requests are preallocated: blk_mq_alloc_tag_set() allocates one struct request per tag up front, each with cmd_size bytes of driver scratch appended inline, so "allocating" a request on the fast path is really just claiming a free tag—no memory allocation happens during I/O. The tag therefore indexes directly into the preallocated request array, which is why the tag is effectively the request's identity. Second, completion uses the tag to find its way back: when the device finishes and raises an interrupt carrying the command identifier, the driver calls blk_mq_complete_request() (using blk_mq_tag_to_rq() to turn the tag back into the struct request), and the tag is freed for reuse. The __sector and __data_len fields carry the request's own position and size independently of the bios, because as bios complete the bio chain is consumed while the request must still know what it originally covered. A driver reads the request's segments for DMA by walking its bios with rq_for_each_segment() or by building a scatter/gather list with blk_rq_map_sg().

There is more than one tag pool, and the distinction explains a great deal about how scheduling works. Without an I/O scheduler a request takes a driver tag from the hardware queue's pool, sized by queue_depth, and holds it from allocation to completion. With a scheduler attached, a request first takes a scheduler tag from a separate, larger pool sized by the queue's nr_requests—stored in internal_tag—and takes a driver tag only when it is actually dispatched. The reason is simple: a scheduler cannot reorder requests it has not been given, so it must be able to hold more of them than the device will accept at once. The two-pool arrangement is exactly that headroom. A small number of tags is also held back from the driver pool entirely: reserved_tags keeps a few available for internal commands, so that a saturated device can still be sent an abort, a reset, or an admin command that will unstick it.

Tags need not belong to one queue, either. A SCSI host adapter with a hundred LUNs has one pool of command slots, not a hundred, so its tag set sets BLK_MQ_F_TAG_QUEUE_SHARED and every request queue built from it draws from the same shared_tags. blk-mq then divides the depth fairly among the queues that are actually active, so an idle LUN reserves nothing while a busy one is not allowed to starve its neighbors. The tag_list in the tag set is the list of queues sharing it, which is also how a change to the hardware queue count can be applied to all of them at once.

Finally, the tag's role in error handling deserves more than a mention, because it is the reason a dispatched request cannot simply be forgotten. Every request carries a deadline, and blk-mq runs one timeout worker per queue that scans for expired requests rather than arming a timer per request—at a million IOPS, per-request timers would cost more than the I/O. When a request expires, the driver's timeout callback runs and returns either BLK_EH_RESET_TIMER, meaning the request is still making progress and should be given more time, or BLK_EH_DONE, meaning the driver has taken ownership and will complete it itself. The race this creates—a device completing a request at the same instant the timeout fires—is resolved by the atomic state field: whichever path succeeds in moving the request from MQ_RQ_IN_FLIGHT to MQ_RQ_COMPLETE owns it, and the other simply stops. Without that, a wedged device would either leak tags forever or complete the same request twice.

blk_mq_ops and the Tag Set

A blk-mq driver is defined by two objects: a blk_mq_tag_set that configures its queues, and a blk_mq_ops table of callbacks. The tag set (struct blk_mq_tag_set) is the shared configuration for one or more request queues—it declares how many hardware queues the device has (nr_hw_queues), how deep each is (queue_depth), how much per-request scratch space the driver needs (cmd_size, allocated inline with each request), the NUMA node, and the ops table. A driver initializes it once with blk_mq_alloc_tag_set() and then creates disks against it with blk_mq_alloc_disk(). 

struct blk_mq_tag_set {
        const struct blk_mq_ops *ops;

};

The blk_mq_ops table is where the driver does its work. Its central method is queue_rq: the block layer calls it to hand the driver one request to submit to the hardware now. The driver programs the device (typically building a command and kicking off DMA) and returns a status (BLK_STS_OK if accepted, BLK_STS_RESOURCE or BLK_STS_DEV_RESOURCE if it is temporarily full and the layer should retry later). Other methods handle completion (complete), batched submission (commit_rqs), per-request and per-hctx setup (init_request, init_hctx), the queue mapping (map_queues), and timeouts (timeout). This pair—tag set plus ops—is the whole driver-facing contract. A driver declares its queue geometry in the tag set, implements queue_rq to talk to the hardware, and calls the block layer's completion helpers when the device is done; blk-mq supplies everything in between. The worked example later in the chapter shows how little code that takes.

Two design choices in this contract repay a closer look. The tag set is deliberately separable from the request queue: one blk_mq_tag_set can back several request queues, which is how a SCSI host adapter presents many LUNs, or an NVMe controller many namespaces, while sharing a single pool of tags and hardware queues. The cmd_size field is the other quiet workhorse—by reserving per-request scratch inline, it lets a driver keep its hardware command descriptor (an NVMe command, a SCSI CDB) right next to the request with no extra allocation, retrieved with blk_mq_rq_to_pdu(). On the completion side, the driver never ends a request directly; it calls blk_mq_complete_request(), and the core runs blk_mq_end_request() to finish the bios and free the tag. The timeout callback is the safety net for this asynchronous model: because a dispatched request may never complete if the hardware wedges, the queue's timeout worker eventually notices the expired deadline and calls timeout so the driver can abort and recover rather than leak the tag forever. Together these turn a small, declarative interface into a scalable, fault-tolerant I/O engine.

Two of those callbacks exist purely to amortize per-request cost, and they matter at NVMe speeds. queue_rqs is the batched form of queue_rq: when a plug is flushed, blk-mq can hand the driver the whole list at once, letting it fill several submission-queue entries and ring the hardware doorbell a single time instead of once per request. Its older sibling commit_rqs achieves part of the same effect by separating "prepare" from "notify." The get_budget and put_budget pair solves a different problem, and only SCSI implements it: there, the limiting resource is not the queue depth but the host adapter's and the target's own command budgets, which are shared across LUNs. Acquiring a budget before a driver tag avoids the wasteful sequence of taking a scarce tag only to discover the command cannot be issued and having to give it back.

Completion has a matching batching path and a steering policy. A driver that reaps several completions at once—the normal case when polling, or when an NVMe interrupt covers a run of finished commands—can accumulate them with blk_mq_add_to_batch() into an io_comp_batch and end them together with blk_mq_end_request_batch(), paying the bookkeeping cost once for the group. Where the completion runs is a separate question. blk_mq_complete_request() does not necessarily finish the request on the CPU that took the interrupt; section 7 describes the mechanism, but the policy that drives it is a per-device tunable, exposed as /sys/block/<dev>/queue/rq_affinity: zero completes wherever the interrupt landed, one steers to the submitting CPU's cache domain, and two insists on the submitting CPU exactly. On a device fast enough that a cache miss costs more than the transfer, this is not a micro- optimization.

I/O Scheduling

Between a request entering a software queue and being dispatched to the hardware, an I/O scheduler may reorder, merge, and throttle it to improve throughput or fairness. blk-mq has its own scheduler framework, distinct from the deleted single-queue elevator, and a small set of concrete schedulers suited to different device speeds. This section covers the framework, the schedulers, and the merging and plugging that happen alongside them.

It is worth being clear about what a scheduler is trying to buy, because the goals conflict. Throughput favors dispatching requests in ascending-sector order so a rotational disk seeks as little as possible, and favors merging small transfers into large ones. Latency favors sending an urgent read to the device immediately, even if that means a seek. Fairness favors giving each process a comparable share of the device rather than letting one streaming writer monopolize it. No single policy optimizes all three, which is exactly why Linux offers several schedulers and lets each device use a different one. On slow, seek-bound media the trade-offs are large and a smart scheduler wins real performance; on a fast, seek-free NVMe drive the same reordering mostly just adds CPU cost, which is why the modern default is often to do no scheduling at all.

It also helps to be clear about what an I/O scheduler is not responsible for, because on a modern system it is only one of three mechanisms that shape the stream of requests. The elevator reorders and merges requests that are already in the queue. Beneath it sits the rq_qos chain (block/blk-rq-qos.c), a list of hooks that can delay a bio before it ever becomes a request: writeback throttling (block/blk-wbt.c), which holds back buffered writeback when it detects that reads are being hurt; the latency controller behind the cgroup file io.latency; and the I/O cost model behind io.cost.qos and io.cost.model. Earlier still, blk_throtl_bio() enforces the hard bandwidth and IOPS caps of io.max. This division matters because the fastest devices run with no elevator at all: on an NVMe drive set to none, proportional sharing between cgroups is not done by a scheduler but by the cost model, and the per-process fairness that bfq would provide on a rotational disk is simply not needed. Reordering is a property of slow media; fairness is a property of the system.

The blk-mq Scheduler Framework

A blk-mq scheduler is a struct elevator_type whose elevator_mq_ops callbacks (block/elevator.h) hook into the path between the software queues and dispatch. The important ones are insert_requests (accept requests into the scheduler's own data structures), dispatch_request (choose the next request to hand to a hardware queue), and bio_merge (try to merge a new bio into a request the scheduler already holds). A scheduler registers with elv_register(), and each request queue can run a different one, selected at runtime.

The framework threads more callbacks through the request's life than those three. limit_depth lets a scheduler cap how many requests a context may have in flight—the lever kyber pulls to hold latency down; prepare_request and finish_request bracket a request so the scheduler can attach and release its own per-request state; requeue_request handles a request the driver could not accept and returned; and has_work tells the dispatch machinery whether the scheduler is holding anything ready. Turning a scheduler on is also what activates the two-stage tagging described in section 5: the request takes a scheduler tag from sched_tags at submission and a scarce driver tag only at dispatch, so the scheduler can hold—and therefore sort—far more requests than the device's queue depth would allow. Figure 14-8 shows how these callbacks and the two tag pools fit into the path between the software queues and the driver.

The rest of elevator_mq_ops falls into three groups. init_sched and exit_sched, together with init_hctx and exit_hctx, build and tear down the scheduler's state for a queue and for each hardware context, and depth_updated tells it that nr_requests has changed so it can resize. A second group is entirely about merging: allow_merge vetoes a merge the scheduler does not want, request_merge searches the scheduler's own structures for a merge candidate, and request_merged and requests_merged notify it afterwards so that it can repair its sort keys, while former_request and next_request let the generic merge code walk the scheduler's sorted order. The third group is per-process state: init_icq and exit_icq bracket an io_cq, the per-queue half of a task's I/O context, which is how bfq finds the queue belonging to the submitting process. A scheduler implements only what it needs—mq-deadline leaves the io-context hooks null, while bfq uses nearly all of them, which is much of why it costs so much more per request.

The active scheduler for a device is read and set through /sys/block/<dev>/queue/scheduler; writing a name switches it live. A special value, none, means no scheduler at all: requests go straight from the software queues to hardware dispatch with no reordering. This is not a degenerate case but the right choice for the fastest devices—on an NVMe drive that already does hardware-level queuing and can service millions of IOPS, the CPU cost of software scheduling exceeds any benefit, so none is the default there. The switch itself (elevator_change()) quiesces the queue, tears down the old scheduler's tags and state, and initializes the new one, so it is safe to do on a live device—though changing it under heavy load briefly stalls new dispatch. The kernel picks the boot-time default per device: mq-deadline for a single-hardware-queue device and none for a multi-queue one (block/elevator.c).

That default deserves stating precisely, because it is not simply "rotational gets a scheduler." elevator_init_mq() installs mq-deadline when the queue has a single hardware queue or when it draws from a shared tag set—the SCSI case, where many LUNs behind one host share a depth and still benefit from sorting—and leaves everything else on none. A driver that knows better can opt out entirely with BLK_MQ_F_NO_SCHED_BY_DEFAULT. Two related details often trip people up. The old elevator= boot parameter no longer does anything; the kernel prints a warning and ignores it, because the choice is per-device and belongs in a udev rule, not on the command line. And a queue can temporarily forbid switching at all: QUEUE_FLAG_NO_ELV_SWITCH is set while a device is being set up or torn down, so a write to scheduler at the wrong moment fails rather than racing.

One historical constraint is worth retiring explicitly, because older material still repeats it. Zoned block devices—SMR disks and ZNS SSDs—require that writes reach each sequential zone in strict order, and for several kernel releases this was achieved by requiring mq-deadline, which alone held a per-zone lock across dispatch. That is no longer true. Write ordering now lives below the scheduler entirely, in the zone write plugging machinery of block/blk-zoned.c: a per-zone plug holds back any write that would overtake another, and the request carries RQF_ZONE_WRITE_PLUGGING to mark it. Because correctness no longer depends on the elevator, a zoned device may run any scheduler, including none.

mq-deadline, kyber, bfq, and none

Linux ships four options, each matched to a class of device and workload. Table 14-10 summarizes them.

Table 14-10. blk-mq I/O schedulers
Scheduler Idea Best for
none No scheduling; software queues feed hardware directly Very fast multi-queue devices (NVMe) where scheduling overhead outweighs benefit
mq-deadline Read/write FIFOs with expiry deadlines; batches sorted requests but guarantees no request starves The general-purpose default; single-queue SATA SSDs and rotational disks
kyber Latency targets: throttles queue depth to keep read/write latency under configured bounds Fast multi-queue devices where bounded latency matters more than raw throughput

mq-deadline (block/mq-deadline.c) is the workhorse. It keeps requests in two FIFOs, one for reads and one for writes, each entry stamped with an expiry deadline; normally it dispatches in sorted (sector) order to minimize seeking, but when a request's deadline passes it is dispatched immediately, bounding worst-case latency and preventing the write starvation that pure sorting can cause. bfq (block/bfq-iosched.c) instead gives each process a fair share of disk time weighted by I/O priority, with heuristics that boost latency-sensitive interactive tasks—valuable on a desktop with a slow disk, at a CPU cost that is not worth it on a fast one. kyber (block/kyber-iosched.c) is the lightest active scheduler: it simply adjusts how many requests it lets through to hold measured latency under a target, which suits fast multi-queue hardware.

A few practical details tie the choice together. mq-deadline exposes tunables in /sys/block/<dev>/queue/iosched/—read_expire and write_expire (the deadlines), writes_starved (how many read batches may run before a write batch is forced), and fifo_batch (how many sorted requests dispatch together)—so its read-favoring bias and latency bound are directly adjustable. It read-favors on purpose: reads are almost always synchronous, with a thread blocked waiting, whereas writes are usually buffered writeback that no one is waiting on. bfq carries the most machinery—per-process queues, budgets, and idling heuristics—so it delivers the best interactivity on rotational disks and cheap flash but consumes noticeably more CPU per request, which is why it is not chosen for high-IOPS devices. kyber and none sit at the opposite end: none does nothing at all and lets the per-CPU software queues feed hardware directly, while kyber adds only a thin latency-targeting throttle. As a rule of thumb, rotational and single-queue SATA devices benefit from mq-deadline or bfq, while multi-queue NVMe is best left on none (or kyber when latency bounds matter). Each scheduler is a separate Kconfig option, so a given kernel only offers the ones built in, listed in the scheduler sysfs file with the active one in brackets.

Two of the three have internal structure worth knowing, because it shows up directly in their tunables. mq-deadline is not one pair of FIFOs but three, one per I/O priority class: requests from IOPRIO_CLASS_RT, IOPRIO_CLASS_BE (and unset priority), and IOPRIO_CLASS_IDLE are kept in separate FIFOs and separate sector-sorted red-black trees, and dispatch prefers the highest non-empty class. That would starve the lower classes outright, so a fourth deadline exists: prio_aging_expire (ten seconds by default, and tunable in the same iosched directory) forces a request that has waited that long to be dispatched regardless of class. Priority is therefore a strong preference with a hard bound, not an absolute ordering. This is also the reason mq-deadline is the scheduler that makes ionice and the cgroup file io.prio.class mean something.

kyber works differently again: it does not sort at all. It divides requests into four domains—read, write, discard, and everything else—and gives each a token budget, then measures completion latency and shrinks or grows those budgets to keep the measured latency under a target. The targets are the scheduler's only real tunables, read_lat_nsec and write_lat_nsec, defaulting to two and ten milliseconds; the asymmetry is the same read-favoring reasoning mq-deadline uses. Because throttling depth is much cheaper than maintaining a sorted structure, kyber can be run on hardware where mq-deadline would already be a measurable tax. bfq, at the other extreme, is cgroup-aware in its own right: it exports io.bfq.weight and a family of bfq.* statistics, and distributes device time rather than bandwidth—which is what makes it fair on a device whose service time depends on where the head happens to be.

Merging and Plugging

Independently of which scheduler runs, the block layer tries hard to merge a newly submitted bio into an existing request, because one larger transfer is far cheaper than two adjacent small ones. A bio can be absorbed into a request whose sectors it adjoins, subject to the request not exceeding the device's size and segment limits. blk-mq first offers the bio to the scheduler's bio_merge hook and, if that declines, tries to merge against the current CPU's plugged requests (blk_attempt_plug_merge() in block/blk-merge.c).

Merging works far better when several requests are visible at once, which is the purpose of plugging. When a thread is about to submit a batch of related I/O, it brackets the work with blk_start_plug() and blk_finish_plug(); while the plug is active, its requests accumulate on a per-task list instead of being dispatched immediately. This gives the merge logic a window in which adjacent bios can coalesce, and it amortizes the cost of running the hardware queue. When the plug is finished—or the task blocks—blk_mq_flush_plug_list() drains the batch to the hardware queues at once, dispatching directly when there is no scheduler and through the scheduler when there is. Plugging is why a loop that reads many sequential blocks issues a handful of large requests rather than hundreds of tiny ones.

There are three merge geometries, and a fourth special case. A back merge appends a bio to a request whose end sector the bio continues—by far the common case, since files are usually written and read forwards. A front merge prepends a bio to a request it immediately precedes, which the scheduler must look for separately because it cannot be found by simply checking the last request. And a request merge occurs when, after two requests have each grown, they become adjacent and are combined into one. Every merge is still bounded by the same queue limits from the geometry section—max_sectors, max_segments, and max_segment_size—so the layer merges only up to what the hardware will accept in one command.

Plugging is largely automatic. The kernel starts a plug around common batch operations—readahead, writeback, and many filesystem paths—without the caller doing anything, and the plug is flushed not only at blk_finish_plug() but whenever the task is about to sleep, so a blocked thread never leaves its I/O stranded on the plug list. The plug list has a soft length cap (BLK_MAX_REQUEST_COUNT) that forces an early flush when many requests accumulate, keeping latency bounded even for a very large batch. On the fast path plugging pairs naturally with blk-mq's per-CPU design: because the plug list is private to the submitting task, filling it and merging within it need no locks, and only the final flush touches the shared hardware queue—so batching improves both merge quality and lock efficiency at once.

The fourth case is discard. Because a discard carries no data, several discard ranges can be combined into a single request even when they are not adjacent on the device—the request then carries a special payload listing the ranges, which is what RQF_SPECIAL_PAYLOAD marks. The elevator reports it as ELEVATOR_DISCARD_MERGE, distinct from the front and back cases, precisely because the usual "sectors must be contiguous" rule does not apply.

Adjacency is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. Before any merge is attempted, blk_rq_merge_ok() (block/blk-merge.c) checks that the two are compatible in every respect that would otherwise be silently lost: the same operation, the same cgroup, compatible data-integrity and inline-encryption contexts, the same write hint and write stream, the same I/O priority, and—for atomic writes—the rules that keep an all-or-nothing request all-or-nothing. A bio marked REQ_NOMERGE is excluded outright. One asymmetry left over from these checks is RQF_MIXED_MERGE: when bios with different failfast policies are merged, the request records that its parts do not share error semantics, so that a failure is reported against the right bios. Merging can also be disabled by hand through /sys/block/<dev>/queue/nomerges: zero is the normal setting, one disables only the more expensive scheduler-level lookups (QUEUE_FLAG_NOXMERGES), and two disables merging entirely (QUEUE_FLAG_NOMERGES), which is useful for benchmarking but for essentially nothing else.

The plug itself is a small structure (struct blk_plug in include/linux/blkdev.h) and its fields explain the policy. mq_list is the batch being accumulated; rq_count and the caps that bound it are what force an early flush—BLK_MAX_REQUEST_COUNT is 32, doubled to 64 when the plug spans more than one queue, and a batch is also flushed once its trailing request reaches BLK_PLUG_FLUSH_SIZE (128 KB), so a few large requests do not sit waiting for a count that will never be reached. multiple_queues and has_elevator record what the batch contains, so the flush path can take the cheapest route: a single-queue batch with no scheduler can be handed straight to the driver, often through queue_rqs. cached_rqs is a further optimization—a caller that knows how many I/Os it is about to issue can say so with blk_start_plug_nr_ios(), and blk-mq will allocate tags and requests in one batch and hand them out from the plug, turning a per-request tag allocation into a per-batch one. cb_list holds unplug callbacks for stacking drivers such as MD, which need to know when the batch above them ends.

I/O Priorities

mq-deadline and bfq both act on an I/O priority, so it is worth seeing where that number comes from. Every bio carries one in bi_ioprio, and a request has no separate copy: req_get_ioprio() reads it from the request's first bio, which is one reason the merge rules above refuse to combine bios of different priority. The interface arrived in 2.6.13, and at first only CFQ read the value. Today the priority is not tied to any one scheduler. Schedulers read it, cgroups rewrite it, and several device drivers pass it to the hardware.

The two system calls in block/ioprio.c work on a single thread, a process group, or every task owned by a user, depending on which. glibc has no wrapper for them, so programs call them through syscall(2) or use ionice(1). ioprio_check_cap() sets the rules for the new value. The real-time class requires CAP_SYS_ADMIN or CAP_SYS_NICE. Best-effort and idle are open to any task at any level, so, unlike nice, an unprivileged task may move itself to best-effort level 0. A NONE class with a nonzero level is rejected with EINVAL. The value is stored in the task's io_context, which set_task_ioprio() allocates if necessary. A child created by fork() gets its own copy of the parent's value, and a thread created with CLONE_IO shares the parent's context outright.

Most tasks never call ioprio_set(), and their class stays NONE. When the priority is needed, __get_task_ioprio() (include/linux/ioprio.h) derives it from the CPU scheduler. The class follows the scheduling policy: SCHED_IDLE gives the idle class, a real-time or deadline policy gives the real-time class, and everything else is best-effort. The level is (nice + 20) / 5, so the common nice 0 is best-effort level 4 and nice 19 is level 7. ioprio_get() on a single thread returns the stored value, which is NONE if nothing was set. For a process group or a user it returns the highest effective priority among the matching tasks.

The priority is attached to an I/O at two points. Paths that build a kiocb, such as direct I/O, AIO, and io_uring, copy the caller's priority into ki_ioprio when the kiocb is initialized. A single request can override it, with aio_reqprio and IOCB_FLAG_IOPRIO for AIO or the ioprio field of an io_uring submission entry, and that value is checked with the same ioprio_check_cap(). Every other bio reaches submit_bio() with class NONE, and bio_set_ioprio() (block/blk-core.c) fills in the priority of the submitting task. That detail limits what ionice can do. Buffered writes are written back later by a flusher kworker, so their bios carry the kworker's default best-effort priority, not the priority of the process that dirtied the pages. Lowering a process's I/O priority therefore mainly affects its reads and its direct I/O.

bio_set_ioprio() then calls blkcg_set_ioprio() (block/blk-ioprio.c, added in 5.14), which applies the cgroup v2 file io.prio.class. Its policies are no-change (the default), promote-to-rt (with none-to-rt as an alias), restrict-to-be, and idle. promote-to-rt raises any non-real-time bio to real-time level 4. The other two keep whichever of the bio's priority and the policy's class is lower, so a cgroup can demote its members' I/O but not promote it. Writeback bios are charged to the cgroup that owns the dirty pages (through wbc_init_bio()), not to the flusher's cgroup. As a result, io.prio.class does apply to buffered writes, even though a per-task ionice does not.

The Request Lifecycle

Having seen the pieces, it helps to trace one I/O end to end. This short section follows a request from the submit_bio() that starts it to the completion that wakes the waiter, tying the bio, the queues, the tag, and the driver's queue_rq into a single sequence. Figure 14-9 shows the path.

Two properties of the path are worth keeping in mind as we trace it. First, it is asynchronous: the thread that calls submit_bio() almost never waits inline for the device—it returns as soon as the request is queued, and a completely different context (a device interrupt, a softirq, sometimes another CPU) finishes the work later. Second, the path is not fixed: the same call may complete in the page cache without any device I/O, may merge into an existing request and add no new work at all, or may traverse every stage below. The lifecycle described here is the full path taken when a bio really does reach the hardware; the earlier layers exist precisely to make that the exception rather than the rule.

It is also a path that can be watched rather than merely imagined, which is worth knowing before tracing it on paper. Each stage below has a tracepoint in include/trace/events/block.h that fires as it happens: block_bio_queue when a bio enters the layer, block_bio_frontmerge and block_bio_backmerge when it is absorbed into an existing request, block_rq_insert when a request is staged, block_rq_issue when it is handed to the driver, and block_rq_complete when the device is done. blktrace and blkparse consume these directly, and the same events are what tools such as biosnoop and biolatency attach to. A request also carries two timestamps of its own, start_time_ns taken when it is allocated and io_start_time_ns taken when it is dispatched, so the time it spent queued and the time the device actually took can be separated—a distinction that /proc/diskstats and iostat both depend on, and one that matters when diagnosing whether a slow disk or a deep queue is to blame.

From submit_bio to queue_rq

Submission begins when some kernel code—a filesystem writeback, a direct-I/O path, a swap-out—calls submit_bio(). After accounting, the bio reaches blk_mq_submit_bio() (block/blk-mq.c), which does the real work: it splits the bio against the queue limits if needed, tries to merge it into an existing plugged or scheduled request, and, if no merge is possible, allocates a fresh struct request and a tag for it and copies the bio into it (blk_mq_bio_to_request()). The request is then either held on the task's plug (to be flushed later) or issued.

One step happens before any of this and is easy to overlook: entering the queue. submit_bio_noacct() takes a reference on the queue's q_usage_counter (blk_queue_enter()) and holds it for the duration of the submission, which is what prevents the disk from being removed or the queue frozen out from under an in-flight bio—del_gendisk() and a queue freeze both wait for this counter to drain. Only once inside does the request-building described above run. Whether the new request is issued now or staged depends on context: if the task holds an active plug the request joins the plug list for later batch flushing; otherwise, and when a scheduler is present, it is inserted into the scheduler (insert_requests) to be dispatched in the scheduler's chosen order; with no scheduler a lone request can be issued directly to hardware, skipping the staging entirely.

Issuing runs the hardware queue: blk-mq selects the target hctx, obtains a driver tag, and calls the driver's queue_rq with the request. The driver builds its device command—an NVMe submission-queue entry, a virtio descriptor, a SCSI CDB—points it at the request's segments for DMA, rings the device's doorbell, and returns BLK_STS_OK. If the driver is momentarily out of resources it returns BLK_STS_RESOURCE and blk-mq retries the request later. At this point the request is in flight: the submitting thread has long since returned, and the request sits in the tag table awaiting the device.

The two "busy" return codes are worth distinguishing because they lead to different retry behavior. BLK_STS_DEV_RESOURCE means the driver ran out of a device-specific resource that an in-flight request will free on completion—so blk-mq simply requeues the request and knows a later completion will trigger a rerun, needing no timer. BLK_STS_RESOURCE means a wider resource failed (a memory allocation, a DMA mapping) that pending I/O may not free, so blk-mq reruns the queue after a short delay rather than relying on a completion that might never come. A driver that returns the wrong one either stalls (using a timer where a completion would have sufficed) or spins (rerunning immediately when only a completion can help), so the distinction is part of the queue_rq contract, not a mere hint.

The moment a request becomes the driver's responsibility is marked explicitly. The first thing queue_rq must do is call blk_mq_start_request(), which moves the request from MQ_RQ_IDLE to MQ_RQ_IN_FLIGHT, records its deadline for the queue's timeout worker, and starts I/O accounting; only after that may the request be completed at all. Section 9 returns to why the ordering is a hard requirement rather than a convention. When the driver instead declines the request, blk_mq_requeue_request() puts it back on the queue's requeue list and the queue is run again later—asynchronously, on the kblockd workqueue, or after an explicit delay through blk_mq_delay_run_hw_queue(). A requeued request keeps its position in the ordering the scheduler chose and keeps any driver state it has already been given, which is why cleanup_rq exists to release that state when the request will be retried rather than finished.

One class of request does not travel this path as a single unit at all. A write carrying REQ_PREFLUSH or REQ_FUA—the durability guarantee behind fsync() and behind a journalling filesystem's commit—is decomposed by block/blk-flush.c into a sequence of up to three steps: a preflush, the data write, and a postflush. What the sequence actually contains depends on the hardware. A device with no writeback cache needs neither, so both flags are simply dropped; a device that supports FUA natively gets the data request with REQ_FUA still set and no postflush; and a device with a cache but no FUA support gets a real flush command issued afterwards instead. The interesting part is that flushes are shared. Only one flush may be in progress at a time, so requests needing one queue on a double-buffered pending list and are all advanced together when it completes—which is why a hundred threads calling fsync() at once do not produce a hundred cache flushes. A request in such a sequence is marked RQF_FLUSH_SEQ and is completed twice: once when its data lands, updating the bio without ending it, and again when the whole sequence finishes, so the submitter is only told the write is durable when it truly is.

Completion

When the device finishes, it raises an interrupt. The driver's handler identifies which request completed—usually directly from the tag the device echoes back—and calls blk_mq_complete_request(). To keep cache traffic local, blk-mq can steer the completion to the CPU that submitted the request (via an IPI or softirq), so the finishing work runs where the data is warm. That work is blk_mq_end_request(), which calls blk_update_request() to walk the request's chain of bios, advancing each iterator by the bytes transferred and calling bio_endio() on each bio as it is fully consumed.

bio_endio() propagates the blk_status_t up: it runs each bio's bi_end_io callback, which for a page-cache write marks the pages clean and wakes anyone waiting on them, and for a synchronous read wakes the blocked reader. Partial completion is handled naturally—if the device transferred only part of a request, blk_update_request() ends the bios that finished and leaves the rest to be retried. Finally the request's tag is returned to the pool, freeing a slot for new I/O. The loop from submit_bio() to freed tag is the complete life of one block transfer.

The completion-steering step deserves a closer look, because it is where blk-mq preserves the per-CPU locality it worked so hard to establish on submission. blk_mq_complete_request() asks blk_mq_complete_need_ipi() whether the request should finish on a different CPU than the one the interrupt landed on; if so it sends an inter-processor interrupt whose handler raises the block softirq (BLOCK_SOFTIRQ) on the submitting CPU, and otherwise it raises that softirq locally. Either way the heavy part of completion runs in softirq context on the CPU whose caches hold the request and its data, not necessarily the CPU the device happened to interrupt. This matters most on many-core servers where the device's interrupts may be concentrated on a few CPUs while submissions are spread across all of them.

Not every completion involves an interrupt at all. For very low-latency workloads a request can be submitted with REQ_POLLED, and the submitting thread then polls the hardware queue for its completion with blk_mq_poll() instead of sleeping and waiting for an IRQ—trading CPU cycles for the elimination of interrupt latency, which is worthwhile on the fastest NVMe drives. And when a request never completes at all, the deadline recorded at dispatch expires and the queue's timeout worker—one per queue, scanning for expired requests, not a timer per request—calls the driver's timeout callback, which can abort the command and either complete it with an error or ask for a reset, so a wedged device surfaces as an I/O error rather than a hung task holding its tag forever. Whichever way a request ends—interrupt, poll, or timeout—the closing acts are the same: the bios are ended through bio_endio(), the driver tag is freed, and the queue reference taken by blk_queue_enter() at submission is released, allowing a pending disk removal or queue freeze to finally proceed.

The status a request ends with carries considerably more information than success or failure. blk_status_t distinguishes a medium error on a specific sector (BLK_STS_MEDIUM) from a command the target refused or does not implement (BLK_STS_TARGET, BLK_STS_NOTSUPP), from a thin-provisioned volume that has run out of backing store (BLK_STS_NOSPC), from a data-integrity mismatch caught in flight (BLK_STS_PROTECTION), from a transport failure on the way to an otherwise healthy device (BLK_STS_TRANSPORT), and from a device that has simply gone away (BLK_STS_OFFLINE). The distinction is not cosmetic. blk_path_error() partitions these codes into errors the media itself reported, which no amount of retrying will change, and everything else, which might succeed over a different route—and that predicate is exactly what lets NVMe multipath and device-mapper multipath decide whether to fail an I/O or quietly resubmit it down another path. Two codes are conventions rather than errors: BLK_STS_AGAIN is the answer to a bio submitted with REQ_NOWAIT that would have had to block, and BLK_STS_TIMEOUT is what the timeout path produces. All of them are mapped to ordinary errno values only at the very top, when the result is handed back to userspace.

Polling deserves one further note, because it is not simply a flag on a bio. A queue must advertise QUEUE_FLAG_POLL, which in practice means the driver asked for poll queues—the HCTX_TYPE_POLL hardware contexts of section 5, which have no interrupt assigned at all. The caller must also be one that can poll: submit_bio() returns a cookie identifying the queue the bio went to, and only a direct-I/O or io_uring path holding that cookie (from an iocb marked IOCB_HIPRI) can spin on bio_poll() until it completes. Bio-based stacking drivers are not excluded: a driver that implements the poll_bio method can forward the poll to whatever device it split the bio onto. The cost is real—a polling thread burns a CPU for the duration of the I/O—so it pays only when the device is fast enough that the interrupt itself, rather than the transfer, is the dominant latency.

Data Integrity and Inline Encryption

Two features attach extra state to a bio that travels with it down to the device and back. Data integrity adds per-sector protection information, which lets the kernel or the device detect data that was corrupted between memory and the media. Inline encryption adds a key and an initialization vector so that a crypto engine in the storage controller encrypts the data as it is transferred. Neither feature existed in Linux 2.6.11. The block layer gained integrity support in 2.6.27 and inline encryption (blk-crypto) in 5.8. Both appear in the lifecycle of section 7 only as a few hooks, shown in Figure 14-10. This section also covers how a driver maps a request, including its integrity metadata, for DMA, because the newest mapping helpers were written for exactly these requests.

Protection Information and the Integrity Limits

A disk's own error-correcting code protects data only once it is on the media. It cannot detect a buffer corrupted by a faulty host adapter, a bit flipped on the link, or a firmware bug that writes correct data to the wrong sector. Protection information (PI), defined for SCSI as T10 DIF and adopted by NVMe, covers that path. The drive is formatted with a few extra bytes per logical block, for example 520-byte sectors on SAS or an NVMe LBA format with metadata, and each block carries a small tuple that both ends can check: 

struct t10_pi_tuple {                    /* include/linux/t10-pi.h, 8 bytes */
        __be16 guard_tag;                /* CRC16 (or IP checksum) of the data */
        __be16 app_tag;                  /* opaque; 0xffff disables checking */
        __be32 ref_tag;                  /* low 32 bits of the target LBA */
};

struct crc64_pi_tuple {                  /* NVMe 64-bit guard, 16 bytes */
        __be64 guard_tag;                /* CRC64 of the data */
        __be16 app_tag;
        __u8   ref_tag[6];               /* 48-bit reference tag */
};

struct blk_integrity {                   /* include/linux/blkdev.h */
        unsigned char flags;             /* BLK_INTEGRITY_* */
        enum blk_integrity_checksum csum_type;  /* NONE, IP, CRC, CRC64 */
        unsigned char metadata_size;     /* bytes of metadata per interval */
        unsigned char pi_offset;         /* where the PI tuple sits in it */
        unsigned char interval_exp;      /* log2 of the protection interval */
        unsigned char tag_size;          /* bytes of app + ref tag space */
        unsigned char pi_tuple_size;     /* 8 or 16, or 0 for no PI */
};

The guard detects corrupted data, and the reference tag detects a misdirected write. A block whose data is correct but which landed on the wrong LBA fails the ref-tag check. The PI type sets the rules for the reference tag. In Type 1 it must equal the LBA. In Type 2 it is checked against a value carried in the command. In Type 3 it is opaque, and only the guard is checked. Linux treats Types 1 and 2 alike as "ref tag checked" and Type 3 as "not checked". The CRC64 tuple, added in 5.18 for NVMe, has a stronger guard and a 48-bit reference tag for very large namespaces. Linux keeps all of this metadata in a buffer separate from the data, the arrangement SCSI calls DIX. The host adapter or the NVMe controller combines the two on the wire, so data pages stay plain page-cache pages.

Since 6.11 a device declares its format the same way it declares everything else, through queue_limits. The driver fills in lim.integrity before allocating or updating the disk. Older kernels had drivers register a blk_integrity_profile with generate and verify callbacks. Those callbacks are gone. The block layer now selects the checksum from csum_type, and block/t10-pi.c implements all of them. The NVMe driver's nvme_init_integrity() maps the namespace's PI type and guard size onto these fields and sets BLK_INTEGRITY_DEVICE_CAPABLE. SCSI's drivers/scsi/sd_dif.c does the same for SAS disks. Device-mapper and MD inherit the format with queue_limits_stack_integrity() when all their members agree, and mark it BLK_INTEGRITY_STACKED. Metadata does not have to contain PI. An NVMe namespace may provide bytes of per-block metadata without any tuple (csum_type NONE). The block layer still allocates and transfers that buffer, but it computes and checks nothing. Generating and Verifying Integrity Metadata

A bio's metadata hangs off bi_integrity as a bio_integrity_payload. It is a second, smaller bvec list that is advanced in step with the data, so splitting a bio splits its metadata as well: 

struct bio_integrity_payload {          /* include/linux/bio-integrity.h */
        struct bvec_iter  bip_iter;     /* bi_sector here is the ref-tag seed */
        unsigned short    bip_vcnt;     /* metadata bio_vecs in use */
        unsigned short    bip_max_vcnt;
        unsigned short    bip_flags;    /* BIP_CHECK_GUARD, _REFTAG, _APPTAG,
                                           BIP_BLOCK_INTEGRITY, ... */
        u16               app_tag;
        struct bio_vec    *bip_vec;
};

The payload can come from one of two places. A submitter that manages its own metadata attaches it before submission. Passthrough commands use blk_rq_integrity_map_user(). Since 6.14, an io_uring read or write can carry PI from user space through an IORING_RW_ATTR_FLAG_PI attribute, which block/fops.c maps with bio_integrity_map_iter(). Since 6.17 the FS_IOC_GETLBMD_CAP ioctl tells an application what metadata format the device uses. Every other bio is handled automatically. blk_mq_submit_bio() calls bio_integrity_prep(), which has lived in block/bio-integrity-auto.c since 6.15. The call comes immediately after the bio has been split to the queue limits, so each fragment gets metadata sized to its own data. The function skips bios that already carry a payload, have no data, or are neither reads nor writes. It allocates a bio_integrity_data from a mempool. That structure holds the payload, a single metadata bvec, a saved copy of the data iterator, and a work item. It seeds the reference tags from the bio's start sector and sets the check flags from csum_type and BLK_INTEGRITY_REF_TAG. For a write it then calls blk_integrity_generate() to compute the tuples before the bio is queued.

For a read the expensive work happens on completion. bio_endio() calls bio_integrity_endio() first. If the read succeeded and its payload asks for checking, the bio is not ended yet. Instead, a work item is queued on kintegrityd, a high-priority, CPU-intensive workqueue with WQ_MEM_RECLAIM so it can make progress under memory pressure. Recomputing a CRC over a megabyte of data is not something to do in an interrupt handler. The worker runs blk_integrity_verify_iter() over the saved iterator. On a mismatch it logs the failing sector and sets BLK_STS_PROTECTION, the status section 7 listed. The worker then frees the payload and calls bio_endio() again, and this time the bio completes.

Next to format, the directory /sys/block/<dev>/integrity/ holds protection_interval_bytes, tag_size, device_is_integrity_capable, and two writable switches, write_generate and read_verify. Clearing a switch sets BLK_INTEGRITY_NOGENERATE or BLK_INTEGRITY_NOVERIFY, and the effect depends on the format. Clearing write_generate or read_verify on a device whose metadata is exactly a PI tuple makes the block layer skip it entirely, so the controller generates or checks the PI itself. A device with extra metadata bytes beyond the tuple still needs a buffer, so the block layer allocates it without check flags and zeroes it for writes so that uninitialized kernel memory is never written to the disk. Zeroing is also applied whenever the metadata is larger than the PI tuple.

One step remains: remapping reference tags. The tags hold the sector the submitter saw, the seed in bip_iter.bi_sector. A bio may have been moved after the tags were written, for example by a partition offset or a device-mapper target that cloned the bio together with its payload. When a write starts, blk_mq_start_request() calls blk_integrity_prepare(), which rewrites every tag that still holds its virtual value to the physical LBA, then marks the payload BIP_MAPPED_INTEGRITY so the remap is done only once. When a read completes, blk_integrity_complete() runs the remap in reverse before the bios end, so verification above compares against the seed it expects. Both hooks run only for formats with BLK_INTEGRITY_REF_TAG.

Inline Encryption with blk-crypto

Encrypting a disk in software, as dm-crypt does, makes the CPU process every byte twice, once to encrypt it and once to copy it. Many storage controllers, especially the UFS and eMMC hosts on ARM64 phone and automotive SoCs, have an inline encryption engine between their DMA engine and the bus. Qualcomm calls it ICE, and Samsung's version is FMP. The engine holds a small number of keys in keyslots and encrypts or decrypts each data unit as it passes through, using a key and a data-unit number (DUN) that the command supplies. The data in memory stays plaintext, and the CPU does no cryptography. blk-crypto exposes such engines to the kernel, and its main user is fscrypt. When an ext4 or f2fs filesystem is mounted with inlinecrypt, file contents are encrypted by the storage hardware instead of by the CPU. This is how Android implements file-based encryption. 

struct blk_crypto_config {              /* include/linux/blk-crypto.h */
        enum blk_crypto_mode_num crypto_mode;   /* AES_256_XTS, AES_128_CBC_ESSIV,
                                                   ADIANTUM, SM4_XTS */
        unsigned int data_unit_size;    /* bytes encrypted with one IV */
        unsigned int dun_bytes;         /* how wide the DUN may grow */
        enum blk_crypto_key_type key_type;      /* RAW or HW_WRAPPED */
};

struct blk_crypto_key {
        struct blk_crypto_config crypto_cfg;
        unsigned int data_unit_size_bits;
        unsigned int size;
        u8 bytes[BLK_CRYPTO_MAX_ANY_KEY_SIZE];
};

struct bio_crypt_ctx {                  /* hung off bio->bi_crypt_context */
        const struct blk_crypto_key *bc_key;
        u64 bc_dun[BLK_CRYPTO_DUN_ARRAY_SIZE];  /* DUN of the first data unit */
};

A user prepares a key with blk_crypto_init_key(), announces it to a device with blk_crypto_start_using_key(), and attaches it to each bio with bio_crypt_set_ctx(bio, key, dun, gfp). The DUN is usually the file-relative block number, so each data unit gets a different IV. The block layer advances the DUN when it splits a bio (bio_crypt_advance()). It merges two bios only when they use the same key and their DUNs are contiguous (bio_crypt_ctx_mergeable()). Keys must be removed with blk_crypto_evict_key() before they are freed, which clears them from every keyslot that holds them.

On the request path the work is keyslot management. After blk_mq_bio_to_request() copies the context into rq->crypt_ctx, blk_crypto_rq_get_keyslot() looks the key up in the profile's hash table of programmed slots. If the key is not present, it takes the least recently used idle slot and calls the driver's keyslot_program(). A slot is reference-counted while requests use it, and if every slot is busy the submitter waits for one to become idle. The driver needs only two numbers per command. For UFS these are the slot index (blk_crypto_keyslot_index(rq->crypt_keyslot)) and the DUN, which go into the transfer descriptor. On completion the slot reference is dropped before the bios end, because the key's owner may evict it as soon as its last bio has completed. Table 14-13 lists what the driver provides in return.

The in-tree implementations are mainly ARM64 SoC drivers. They include the UFS core (drivers/ufs/core/ufshcd-crypto.c) with the Qualcomm and Exynos variants, and the eMMC command-queue engine (cqhci, with sdhci-msm). All of the Qualcomm drivers share the ICE driver in drivers/soc/qcom/ice.c. Device-mapper targets that only remap sectors pass a profile through from the devices below them. Hardware-wrapped keys, added in 6.15, address a remaining exposure: a raw key in kernel memory can be read by an attacker who compromises the kernel. A wrapped key is encrypted by the hardware and unwrapped only inside the engine. User space creates and converts such keys with the BLKCRYPTOIMPORTKEY, BLKCRYPTOGENERATEKEY, and BLKCRYPTOPREPAREKEY ioctls, and fscrypt asks the hardware to derive a separate software secret for the uses it cannot offload.

A filesystem should not have to know whether the device has an engine, so blk-crypto has a software fallback (block/blk-crypto-fallback.c, CONFIG_BLK_INLINE_ENCRYPTION_FALLBACK). It implements the same interface with kernel crypto API transforms and its own keyslot profile (100 slots by default, the num_keyslots module parameter). Linux 7.0 moved the fallback decision above the block layer. Filesystems and iomap now submit encrypted bios through blk_crypto_submit_bio(), which passes the bio straight to submit_bio() when it has no context or the device supports its configuration natively, and otherwise hands it to the fallback. submit_bio_noacct() fails any crypt bio the device cannot handle natively with BLK_STS_NOTSUPP, so the request path deals only with hardware keyslots. For a write, the fallback encrypts into bounce pages from a mempool and submits new bios, holding back the original until they complete. For a read, it replaces the bio's bi_end_io and decrypts the data in place on a workqueue after the device returns it.

The two features exclude each other. blk_crypto_register() refuses a profile for a queue that supports integrity and warns that hardware inline encryption is being disabled. queue_limits_commit_update() rejects integrity limits that define PI tags (a nonzero tag_size) on a queue that already has a crypto profile. bio_integrity_prep() warns if a bio somehow carries both. A given device therefore uses at most one of them, as Figure 14-10 notes, and in practice they serve different markets. PI is used mostly by enterprise SAS and NVMe storage, and inline encryption by mobile and embedded flash.

Mapping Requests for DMA

Whatever a request carries, the driver must eventually give the device bus addresses for it. The classic two-step method remains: blk_rq_map_sg() fills a scatterlist from the request's bvecs, merging physically contiguous pieces within the segment limits, and dma_map_sg() maps it. blk_rq_map_integrity_sg() does the same for the metadata. In 6.16 all of these helpers moved into block/blk-mq-dma.c and were rebuilt on a single iterator (struct blk_map_iter). The iterator walks either the data bvecs or the integrity bvecs and returns physically contiguous ranges.

6.17 added a way to skip the scatterlist. The interface is in include/linux/blk-mq-dma.h: 

struct blk_dma_iter iter;               /* addr, len, status, p2pdma state */
struct dma_iova_state *state = &cmd->dma_state;  /* kept until unmap */

if (!blk_rq_dma_map_iter_start(rq, dma_dev, state, &iter))
        return iter.status;             /* nothing mapped */
if (blk_rq_dma_map_coalesce(state)) {
        /* the IOMMU made the whole request one range: iter.addr, iter.len */
        add_one_segment(cmd, iter.addr, iter.len);
} else {
        do {
                add_one_segment(cmd, iter.addr, iter.len);  /* PRP/SGL entry */
        } while (blk_rq_dma_map_iter_next(rq, dma_dev, &iter));
        if (iter.status != BLK_STS_OK)
                goto unmap;
}
/* completion: blk_rq_dma_unmap(rq, dma_dev, state, len, iter.p2pdma.map) */

blk_rq_dma_map_iter_start() first examines the first physical range to see whether it is PCI peer-to-peer memory, such as another device's BAR. If the peer can be reached directly, it is mapped to a bus address with no IOMMU involvement. Otherwise, when the device is behind an IOMMU (Intel VT-d or AMD-Vi on x86_64, an SMMU on ARM64) and the request's segment gaps line up with the IOMMU page size, the function allocates one IOVA range for the whole request and links every physical range into it with dma_iova_link(). The result is a single DMA address and length, and blk_rq_dma_map_coalesce() returns true. If neither path applies, each range is mapped individually with dma_map_phys(), and _next returns them one at a time. Unmapping is equally cheap. blk_rq_dma_unmap() destroys the IOVA range in one call and returns true. It returns false only when the per-segment mappings must be undone one by one, which is why NVMe records its segments only in that case. blk_rq_integrity_dma_map_iter_start() and _next do the same for the metadata buffer. The NVMe PCIe driver uses both, and it is currently the only in-tree user. Most drivers still use scatterlists, which remain fully supported.

Writing a Block Driver

With the machinery in place, a block driver is surprisingly small. This section shows the skeleton of a request-based blk-mq driver and then points at the real drivers that follow the same shape, so the pattern is recognizable in the source tree. The theme throughout is that the driver supplies only the two things the block layer cannot know—the device's queue geometry and how to turn a request into a hardware command—and inherits everything else.

A Minimal blk-mq Driver

A request-based driver needs three things: an blk_mq_ops table with a queue_rq, a blk_mq_tag_set describing its queue geometry, and a gendisk created against that tag set. The following sketch assembles them. Every function in it has appeared earlier in the chapter. 

/* .complete callback: runs on the submitting CPU (softirq) after the
 * device signals done; ends the request and frees its tag. */
static void my_complete(struct request *rq)
{
        struct my_cmd *cmd = blk_mq_rq_to_pdu(rq);   /* per-request scratch */

        blk_mq_end_request(rq, cmd->status);          /* end bios, free tag */
}

/* Device interrupt handler: find the finished request from the tag the
 * device echoed back and hand it to blk-mq for completion. */
static irqreturn_t my_irq(int irq, void *data)
{
        struct my_dev *dev = data;
        struct request *rq;
        struct my_cmd *cmd;
        u16 tag = my_read_completed_tag(dev);        /* device-specific */

        rq  = blk_mq_tag_to_rq(dev->tag_set.tags[0], tag);
        if (!rq)
                return IRQ_NONE;
        cmd = blk_mq_rq_to_pdu(rq);
        cmd->status = my_hw_status(dev) ? BLK_STS_IOERR : BLK_STS_OK;

        blk_mq_complete_request(rq);                 /* steers to .complete */
        return IRQ_HANDLED;
}

static blk_status_t my_queue_rq(struct blk_mq_hw_ctx *hctx,
                                const struct blk_mq_queue_data *bd)
{
        struct request *rq = bd->rq;
        struct my_dev *dev = rq->q->queuedata;

        blk_mq_start_request(rq);               /* mark in flight, set deadline */

        /* Walk the request's segments and program the hardware (DMA). */
        /* ... build device command from rq's bios, kick the device ... */

        return BLK_STS_OK;                      /* accepted; completes later */
}

static const struct blk_mq_ops my_mq_ops = {
        .queue_rq = my_queue_rq,
        .complete = my_complete,                /* finish the rq after IRQ */
};

static int my_probe(struct my_dev *dev)
{
        /* The device's geometry and capabilities, declared once. */
        struct queue_limits lim = {
                .logical_block_size  = 512,
                .max_hw_sectors      = 2048,     /* 1 MB per command */
                .max_segments        = 64,
                .features            = BLK_FEAT_WRITE_CACHE | BLK_FEAT_FUA,
        };
        struct gendisk *disk;
        int ret;

        /* 1. Configure the queue geometry. */
        memset(&dev->tag_set, 0, sizeof(dev->tag_set));
        dev->tag_set.ops           = &my_mq_ops;
        dev->tag_set.nr_hw_queues  = 1;
        dev->tag_set.queue_depth   = 128;
        dev->tag_set.numa_node     = NUMA_NO_NODE;
        dev->tag_set.cmd_size      = sizeof(struct my_cmd);
        ret = blk_mq_alloc_tag_set(&dev->tag_set);
        if (ret)
                return ret;

        /* 2. Allocate the disk + queue together. */
        disk = blk_mq_alloc_disk(&dev->tag_set, &lim, dev);
        if (IS_ERR(disk)) {
                blk_mq_free_tag_set(&dev->tag_set);
                return PTR_ERR(disk);
        }
        dev->disk         = disk;
        disk->fops        = &my_bdops;           /* block_device_operations */
        disk->private_data = dev;
        snprintf(disk->disk_name, DISK_NAME_LEN, "mydisk");
        set_capacity(disk, dev->nr_sectors);

        /* 3. Go live. */
        ret = add_disk(disk);
        if (ret) {
                put_disk(disk);
                blk_mq_free_tag_set(&dev->tag_set);
        }
        return ret;
}

When the device completes an I/O—signaled by its interrupt—the driver's handler finds the request (from the tag the device returned) and calls blk_mq_complete_request(rq), which eventually reaches blk_mq_end_request(rq, status) to finish the bios and free the tag. That is the entire data path: queue_rq to submit, a completion call to finish. Everything else—merging, scheduling, queue mapping, tag management, partition remapping, statistics—is supplied by the block layer. The contrast with 2.6-era request_fn drivers, which had to walk the queue, manage the queue lock, and drive the elevator by hand, is stark.

A few obligations in that small path are easy to get wrong. The first call inside queue_rq must be blk_mq_start_request(): it transitions the request to the in-flight state and records the deadline the queue's timeout worker will check, and until it runs the request may not be completed. The order matters—a driver that programs the hardware before calling it risks the device completing the request before blk-mq considers it started. Second, queue_rq runs in a context that may not sleep by default; a driver that must block (to allocate, to wait on a mutex) has to set BLK_MQ_F_BLOCKING in its tag-set flags, which tells blk-mq to call queue_rq from a sleepable context at some cost. Third, the per-request scratch declared by cmd_size is reached with blk_mq_rq_to_pdu(rq)—that is where a driver builds and keeps its hardware command descriptor, next to the request and with no extra allocation. Finally, if queue_rq cannot even begin (a bad request, a dead device) it should complete the request with an error via blk_mq_end_request() and return BLK_STS_OK, reserving the BLK_STS_RESOURCE/BLK_STS_DEV_RESOURCE returns for the temporary-busy case that should be retried.

Teardown mirrors probe in reverse and must observe the same ordering discipline. When the device goes away the driver calls del_gendisk() to stop new opens and I/O and remove the partitions, then put_disk() to drop the disk reference, and finally blk_mq_free_tag_set() to release the shared tag set and its preallocated requests—in that order, because the tag set outlives the disk and must not be freed while the queue built from it still exists. Because del_gendisk() waits for the queue's usage counter to drain (the reference taken on every submit_bio()), it also guarantees that no request is still in flight by the time the tag set is freed.

The queue_limits passed to blk_mq_alloc_disk() is the whole of what the driver tells the block layer about its device, and it is worth dwelling on because this is the part that changed most since Linux 2.6. A 2.6-era driver called a series of setters—blk_queue_max_hw_sectors(), blk_queue_logical_block_size(), blk_queue_write_cache() and a dozen more—each mutating a live queue, so the queue passed through a succession of half-configured states and no single place validated the result. Today the driver fills in one stack-allocated structure and hands it over; blk_validate_limits() checks the whole thing for consistency once, and rejects the disk if it does not add up. A limit that must change later—a loop device pointed at a new backing file, a disk whose capacity grew—is changed through the queue_limits_start_update()/queue_limits_commit_update() pair, which applies the change atomically against a frozen queue rather than in pieces.

The features field inside that structure carries the device's boolean properties, and it is where a driver declares things that the layer above will change its behavior for. BLK_FEAT_WRITE_CACHE says the device has a volatile cache, which is what makes the flush machinery of section 7 run at all; BLK_FEAT_FUA adds that the device can write a single command through to stable media, which lets the block layer skip the trailing flush. BLK_FEAT_ROTATIONAL is the one that decides whether /sys/block/<dev>/queue/rotational reads 1, and with it much of the default I/O scheduling and readahead policy. Others announce optional capabilities: BLK_FEAT_POLL for the polled completions of section 7, BLK_FEAT_NOWAIT for REQ_NOWAIT support, BLK_FEAT_STABLE_WRITES when data must not be modified while writeback is in flight (checksumming and integrity devices), BLK_FEAT_DAX for direct-access persistent memory, and BLK_FEAT_ATOMIC_WRITES and BLK_FEAT_PCI_P2PDMA for the newer hardware features. Where a 2.6 driver set queue flags, a modern one declares features—and the difference is not cosmetic, because the stacking code of section 2 can then propagate them correctly through a device-mapper or RAID stack.

The sketch leaves one thing to the comments that a real driver must confront: turning the request's segments into something the hardware can read. The classic answer is blk_rq_map_sg(), which walks the request's bios and fills a scatterlist the driver then hands to dma_map_sg()—which is why max_segments in the limits above must match the size of the scatterlist the driver allocates. Newer drivers can avoid the intermediate array entirely: blk_rq_dma_map_iter_start() and blk_rq_dma_map_iter_next() (include/linux/blk-mq-dma.h) walk and map in a single pass, yielding one DMA address and length at a time, and blk_rq_dma_map_coalesce() reports the happy case in which an IOMMU folded the entire request into one contiguous range. The NVMe PCIe driver uses this path, and section 8 describes how it works. One further detail concerns drivers with more than one hardware queue: the skeleton finds its request with blk_mq_tag_to_rq(tag_set.tags[0], tag) because it has exactly one, but a tag is only unique within a hardware queue, so a real multi-queue driver must either look the tag up in the right queue's tags or use blk_mq_unique_tag(), which packs the queue number and the tag into one value.

Teardown has one more case than the orderly one above. When a device is pulled out rather than unbound—a hot-unplugged NVMe drive, a disconnected USB disk—the driver should call blk_mark_disk_dead() first, which fails all outstanding and future I/O immediately instead of letting del_gendisk() wait for requests that can never complete. Two related tools are worth knowing for the reconfiguration cases in between: blk_mq_quiesce_queue() stops the layer from calling queue_rq and waits for the calls already running to return, and freezing the queue with blk_mq_freeze_queue() additionally drains everything in flight. A driver that needs to change its queue count, resize its tag set, or reset its controller does so between a freeze and an unfreeze, which is the same mechanism del_gendisk() relies on.

Real Drivers: NVMe and virtio-blk

The skeleton is exactly the shape of the production drivers. virtio-blk (drivers/block/virtio_blk.c) is the paravirtualized disk used by virtual machines and the easiest full example to read: its virtio_queue_rq() calls blk_mq_start_request(), translates the request into a virtio descriptor chain, and pushes it onto a virtqueue; its probe allocates a tag set (mapping one hardware queue per virtqueue) and a disk exactly as above. null_blk (drivers/block/null_blk/) is a memory-backed test driver that is even smaller and is the canonical reference for the blk-mq API.

NVMe (drivers/nvme/) is where the multi-queue design pays off fully. An NVMe SSD exposes many independent hardware submission and completion queues, so its driver sets nr_hw_queues to the CPU count and maps one hardware queue per CPU; a CPU submits a command to its own queue and is interrupted by that same queue's completion, with no cross-CPU synchronization anywhere on the fast path. This is precisely the scaling blk-mq was built to enable, and it is why a modern NVMe drive can be driven at millions of IOPS from a single system—something the 2.6-era single-queue block layer could never have done.

Two larger families fit the same mold. The SCSI subsystem (drivers/scsi/) puts a blk-mq front end on every SCSI, SAS, and SATA disk: its shared mid-layer (drivers/scsi/scsi_lib.c) implements queue_rq once, turning each request into a SCSI command, so the hundreds of individual host-adapter drivers never touch blk-mq directly. This is why a SATA SSD on an AHCI controller and a fibre-channel array present the identical /dev/sd* interface upward. Simpler special devices follow the skeleton almost literally: loop (drivers/block/loop.c) backs a block device with a file, and the MMC/SD stack drives embedded flash the same way. These share one property—each is the bottom of the stack, owning real (or file-backed) storage, and therefore takes the request-based path.

A few members of the family are worth knowing because they stretch the pattern in instructive directions. loop is the standard example of a driver whose queue_rq genuinely cannot avoid sleeping, since it has to perform file I/O on the backing file, so it sets BLK_MQ_F_BLOCKING and is called from a context where that is allowed. ublk (drivers/block/ublk_drv.c) goes further still: it is a generic framework for implementing the entire device in userspace, forwarding each request to a userspace daemon over io_uring and taking the completion back the same way. The appeal is that a qcow2 or network-backed device can then be written in any language, debugged with ordinary tools, updated independently of the kernel, and crash without taking the machine down. From the block layer's point of view it is an unremarkable blk-mq driver; the interesting part is entirely in where queue_rq sends the work. And for readers interested in the kernel's Rust support, drivers/block/rnull/rnull.rs implements the same null_blk interface through Rust abstractions, which is a compact way to see what a safe wrapper over blk_mq_ops looks like.

NVMe is also worth a second look, because "the NVMe driver" is really a shared core plus several transports. drivers/nvme/host/core.c holds the command set, namespace handling, and multipath logic, while drivers/nvme/host/pci.c, nvme-tcp, nvme-rdma and nvme-fc supply only the transport underneath—so an NVMe namespace reached across a network presents the same /dev/nvme* device and the same blk-mq behavior as one on the PCIe bus. Its multipath support is where GENHD_FL_HIDDEN from section 4 is used: the per-path disks exist but are not shown, and only the multipath disk is visible. virtio-blk illustrates a different refinement: it implements queue_rqs as well as queue_rq, so a plug flush becomes one batch of descriptors and a single notification to the host rather than one per request—the batching path of section 5 used exactly as intended.

It is worth closing the loop with the other kind of driver from section 1. Stacking drivers—device-mapper (drivers/md/dm.c: LVM, dm-crypt, dm-thin) and MD RAID (drivers/md/md.c)—do not follow this skeleton at all. They allocate their disk with blk_alloc_disk() and supply a submit_bio method instead of a tag set and queue_rq, because they consume bios, remap or replicate them, and resubmit them to the real drivers below. So the two shapes in this chapter correspond to the two positions in the stack: request-based drivers at the bottom talking to hardware, and bio-based drivers in the middle transforming I/O on its way down. Recognizing which shape a given driver in the source tree uses—does it have a blk_mq_ops with queue_rq, or a block_device_operations with submit_bio?—is the fastest way to place it in the architecture.

Opening a Block Device File

The chapter closes where a user program touches a block device: opening a device file such as /dev/sda1. This mirrors the character-device open path of Chapter 13 but resolves to the block subsystem instead of a cdev. As with a character device, the VFS gives a block-device inode a stub operations table at creation time—init_special_inode() points its i_fop at def_blk_fops (block/fops.c)—and the real work happens at open. 

/* block/fops.c */
const struct file_operations def_blk_fops = {
        .open           = blkdev_open,
        .release        = blkdev_release,
        .llseek         = blkdev_llseek,
        .read_iter      = blkdev_read_iter,
        .write_iter     = blkdev_write_iter,
        .iopoll         = iocb_bio_iopoll,      /* polled completions */
        .mmap_prepare   = blkdev_mmap_prepare,
        .fsync          = blkdev_fsync,
        .unlocked_ioctl = blkdev_ioctl,
        .splice_read    = filemap_splice_read,
        .splice_write   = iter_file_splice_write,
        .fallocate      = blkdev_fallocate,     /* discard / write-zeroes */
        .uring_cmd      = blkdev_uring_cmd,     /* async discard via io_uring */
        ...
};

Opening runs blkdev_open(). It first translates the file's flags into a block-layer mode and, for an exclusive open, records the struct file itself as the holder, then checks permission with bdev_permission(). Only after that does it turn the inode's device number into a block_device: blkdev_get_no_open() looks up the cached block_device for inode->i_rdev in the internal block-device filesystem (each block_device has a backing inode there) and takes a reference. It then calls bdev_open(), which resolves whether this is a whole disk or a partition (blkdev_get_whole() vs blkdev_get_part()), invokes the driver's block_device_operations.open, and increments bd_openers. Along the way it records on the struct file what the device can do that an ordinary file cannot: FMODE_CAN_ATOMIC_WRITE if the hardware supports the atomic writes of section 3, and FMODE_HAS_METADATA if the disk carries integrity data. From that point the open struct file can be read and written—those go through blkdev_read_iter/blkdev_write_iter and, via the page cache, ultimately become the bios this chapter has followed.

The open mode is translated on the way in. The VFS O_RDONLY, O_WRONLY/O_RDWR, O_EXCL, and O_NDELAY flags become the block layer's own blk_mode_t bits—BLK_OPEN_READ, BLK_OPEN_WRITE, BLK_OPEN_EXCL, BLK_OPEN_NDELAY—and this mode is what bdev_open() passes down to the driver's block_device_operations.open, so a driver learns whether it is being opened for writing or exclusively. BLK_OPEN_NDELAY is the block-layer reading of O_NDELAY: it lets a management tool open a removable or not-yet-ready device (an empty card reader, an unspun-up disk) without blocking, which is why utilities that only need to issue an ioctl can open a device that has no media.

Reads and writes on the open descriptor take one of two paths, both ending in the bios of this chapter. A buffered access goes through the block device's own page cache—the block_device has an address_space just as a file does—so a read() is served from cached pages or triggers readahead, and a write() dirties pages that writeback later turns into REQ_OP_WRITE bios; this path is implemented with the iomap framework (blkdev_iomap_begin()). An access opened with O_DIRECT instead takes the direct path (blkdev_direct_IO()), which pins the user pages and builds bios straight from them, bypassing the page cache entirely—the mode databases and mkfs use to control their own caching. The direct path is not free of rules: the file offset and the transfer length must be multiples of the logical block size, and the user buffer must satisfy the device's DMA alignment, or the read fails with -EINVAL rather than being silently fixed up. Either way the result is the same kind of bio submitted through submit_bio(), so opening a raw block device and reading from it exercises exactly the stack the rest of the chapter built.

Reading and writing is only half of what a block device file is for; the other half is ioctl. blkdev_ioctl() (block/ioctl.c) handles a sizeable vocabulary of commands before ever consulting the driver, and they fall into two groups. The first simply reads back the geometry of section 2: BLKGETSIZE64 for the capacity in bytes, BLKSSZGET and BLKPBSZGET for the logical and physical block sizes, BLKIOMIN, BLKIOOPT and BLKALIGNOFF for the minimum and optimal I/O sizes and the alignment offset, BLKROTATIONAL for whether the device seeks, and BLKGETDISKSEQ for the sequence number that distinguishes one disk from a later one that reused its name. This is how blockdev, lsblk, and every mkfs learn what they are writing to. The second group acts: BLKDISCARD, BLKSECDISCARD and BLKZEROOUT issue the corresponding operations from Table 14-4 directly, BLKFLSBUF flushes and invalidates the device's page cache, BLKROSET marks the device read-only, and BLKRRPART and BLKPG reread or edit the partition table. Zoned devices add their own set (BLKREPORTZONE, BLKRESETZONE and the open, close and finish commands), and BLKTRACESETUP starts the tracing described in section 7. Only a command the block layer does not recognize is passed down to the driver's own block_device_operations.ioctl, which is why so few drivers implement one.

Two entries in that table are worth calling out because they are newer than most of the surrounding material. blkdev_fallocate() makes fallocate() meaningful on a block device: FALLOC_FL_PUNCH_HOLE becomes a discard, and FALLOC_FL_ZERO_RANGE and FALLOC_FL_WRITE_ZEROES become a write-zeroes, so the same system call that punches a hole in a file trims a range of an SSD. blkdev_uring_cmd() exposes the same idea asynchronously: BLOCK_URING_CMD_DISCARD lets an io_uring submission queue a discard without blocking a thread, which matters for a program trimming a large device. And iopoll, pointing at iocb_bio_iopoll(), is the file-layer end of the polled completion path of section 7—the route by which an iocb marked IOCB_HIPRI reaches bio_poll().

One block-specific concern is exclusive access. A filesystem mounting a device, or a tool like mkfs formatting it, must not have another writer scribbling underneath. The open path supports this through the holder mechanism: an exclusive open (BLK_OPEN_EXCL) records a holder in bd_holder via bd_prepare_to_claim(), and a conflicting exclusive open fails with -EBUSY. This is how the kernel prevents two filesystems from mounting the same partition at once, and why you cannot format a mounted disk. A newer refinement, BLK_OPEN_RESTRICT_WRITES, lets a holder (such as a mounted filesystem) permit shared readers while blocking any writer that is not the holder, closing the older hole where a raw write to a mounted block device could corrupt the filesystem.

Not every opener is a user process. A filesystem being mounted, a device-mapper target stacking on a lower device, or the swap subsystem all need the same machinery from inside the kernel, and they get it through bdev_file_open_by_dev() and bdev_file_open_by_path(). These return a struct file rather than a bare block_device, so an in-kernel opener holds exactly the same kind of reference a user process does and drops it the same way, with bdev_fput(). The holder is passed as an argument, and along with it a bd_holder_ops table—the callbacks of section 4 by which the block layer tells the holder that the device has been removed, or asks it to sync, freeze, or thaw. That is the path by which pulling a USB disk out of a mounted filesystem reaches the filesystem rather than merely producing I/O errors.

The object model underneath is worth noting because it changed since Linux 2.6. Each block_device is now backed by its own inode in a dedicated pseudo-filesystem, the bdev superblock (blockdev_superblock, block/bdev.c), rather than being embedded in the device-file inode as it was in 2.6. This gives every disk and partition a single canonical block_device and backing inode—shared by all openers and owning the page cache used for writeback—reached by dev_t through blkdev_get_no_open(). Opening /dev/sda1 from ten processes therefore yields ten open struct file objects but one block_device, one address_space, and one bd_openers count, which is exactly what lets the last-close release and the exclusive-holder logic work correctly. The user-visible semantics—open a node, reach a driver, honor exclusivity—are unchanged from Linux 2.6 even though the implementation was rebuilt around this separate object.

Closing runs the same steps in reverse. blkdev_release() calls bdev_release(), which syncs the device if it looks like the last opener is going away, gives back any write access reserved under BLK_OPEN_RESTRICT_WRITES, releases the exclusive claim if this file held one, and flushes a DISK_EVENT_MEDIA_CHANGE event so that a removal commanded from userspace by eject is noticed. Only then does it decrement bd_openers and, on the last close, call the driver's block_device_operations.release. A driver therefore sees a balanced pair of open and release calls per device—not per file—and can use them to spin a disk up and down or to take and drop a firmware reference, exactly as a character driver does with its own open and release.

Summary

This chapter followed a block I/O from a filesystem down to the disk. The block layer exists because storage is slow, randomly addressable, and mediated by the page cache, so most accesses never reach the device and the ones that do must be merged and ordered for efficiency. It counts in four units at once—the fixed 512-byte kernel sector, the device's logical and physical blocks, the filesystem's block, and the memory segment—and much of its work is converting between them within the bounds a device declares. The transfer in flight is a bio—a start sector plus a list of memory segments described by bio_vec entries and walked by a bvec_iter—which the layer splits against device limits and chains for single completion. A gendisk represents the whole device and a block_device each openable partition, with control operations in block_device_operations and the data path flowing through the request queue. Drivers come in two shapes: request-based ones at the bottom of the stack, which implement queue_rq, and bio-based ones—device-mapper, MD RAID, and the purely in-memory devices—which implement submit_bio and transform bios on their way down. A user program reaches all of this by opening a device file, which resolves the dev_t to that block_device, honors exclusive holders, and turns reads and writes into the same bios—buffered through the page cache or direct with O_DIRECT—while its ioctls and fallocate reach the device's own commands.

The heart of the modern block layer is blk-mq. Per-CPU software staging queues feed a smaller set of per-hardware-queue dispatch queues, requests are identified by tags drawn from a bounded pool that doubles as flow control, and a driver implements just queue_rq plus a completion call against a blk_mq_tag_set. An optional scheduler— mq-deadline, kyber, bfq, or none—reorders requests between the software and hardware queues, while plugging and merging coalesce adjacent I/O. Each bio's I/O priority, taken from the submitting task or set with ioprio_set() and rewritten by io.prio.class, steers those schedulers and, on some devices, the hardware itself. The lifecycle ties these together and is thoroughly asynchronous: a submit_bio() becomes a tagged request dispatched to the driver's queue_rq, the submitting thread returns, and the device's interrupt later drives completion back through blk_mq_complete_request() to end the bios and free the tag—or, when the device never answers, the queue's single timeout worker notices the expired deadline and lets the driver abort. Two optional services ride along the same path. Integrity metadata is generated for a write after the bio is split and verified for a read on kintegrityd. Inline encryption programs the bio's key into a controller keyslot per request, and since 7.0 the software fallback for devices without an engine is chosen before the bio enters the block layer.

Three themes cut across those mechanisms and are worth carrying away separately. The first is that the driver now declares rather than configures. A device's geometry and capabilities arrive as one validated queue_limits—block sizes, maximum transfer, segment count, topology, and a BLK_FEAT_* features mask—handed over when the disk is allocated, instead of a succession of setters mutating a live queue, and a later change is an explicit transaction against a frozen queue. The second is that shaping the I/O stream is no longer the elevator's job alone: the rq_qos chain and cgroup throttling act on a bio before it becomes a request, which is what makes fairness available on an NVMe drive running no scheduler at all. The third is that correctness constraints the layer cannot express as a limit are pushed to the edges—flush and FUA decomposed into a shared, coalesced sequence, zoned writes ordered by per-zone plugging, atomic writes and zone appends refused for splitting rather than quietly divided.

The through-line is scalability. The 2.6 block layer was built when a disk did hundreds of I/O operations per second and a machine had a handful of CPUs; its single queue and single lock were adequate. Modern storage does millions of operations per second across many hardware queues, and modern machines have many CPUs all issuing I/O at once. The multi-queue redesign—per-CPU submission, tag-based tracking, and a driver interface of a single queue_rq—is what lets Linux extract that performance while keeping drivers small.
