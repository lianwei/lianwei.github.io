The previous chapters described the kernel's generic filesystem machinery: the VFS abstraction, the page cache, the block layer, and the reclaim of cached pages. None of that actually decides how bytes are arranged on a disk—that is the job of a concrete filesystem. This chapter looks at one in detail—the native Linux filesystem, in near-universal use, and a clean illustration of how a real filesystem implements the VFS methods. In Linux 2.6 that filesystem was ext2 and its journaling successor ext3; this chapter studies their descendant, ext4, which absorbed both of them—the ext3 driver has been removed from the kernel entirely, and ext2 and ext3 volumes are now mounted by the ext4 code. Ext4 is no longer the only reasonable choice—xfs and btrfs have taken the default slot on several distributions—but it remains the most widely deployed Linux filesystem and the one whose lineage runs directly back to the ext2 of 1993, which is exactly what makes it the useful one to study here.

Ext4 is worth studying precisely because it is not a clean-slate design but an evolution. It keeps the block-group structure, the inode, and the directory format of ext2, so that an old ext2 volume is still a valid ext4 volume, while adding the features that a modern filesystem needs: extents in place of the old indirect-block map, 64-bit block numbers, metadata checksums, nanosecond timestamps, a smarter multi-block allocator, delayed allocation, and journaling through the jbd2 layer with an optional fast-commit path. The result is a filesystem whose on-disk skeleton a 2.6-era reader would recognize but whose behavior is substantially new. This chapter follows the classic arc—characteristics, on-disk structures, extents, in-memory structures, the VFS methods, allocation, and journaling—noting at each step what carried over and what changed, and then adds a final section for the capabilities that have no 2.6-era counterpart at all: fast commit, inline data, transparent encryption, and fs-verity. A theme worth watching as it goes is how little of the modern ext4 is ext4-only code. Extended attributes, case-insensitive lookup, encryption, verity, direct I/O through iomap, and the journal itself are all generic kernel services that ext4 opts into, so a good deal of this chapter is about how a filesystem is assembled rather than written.

From Ext2 to Ext4

Before the structures, it helps to place ext4 in its family and understand the compatibility scheme that lets one driver serve three filesystems. This section sketches the lineage, the feature-flag mechanism that negotiates what a given volume supports, and the block-group layout that organizes the whole disk.

The reason to study one filesystem in depth, rather than survey many, is that the generic VFS layer defines what operations a filesystem must provide, but only a concrete implementation shows how those operations become bytes on a disk—how an inode number becomes a location, how free space is found, how a crash is survived. Ext4 is the right choice for this because its on-disk format is documented and stable, because it is still the filesystem a reader is most likely to be standing on, and because its evolution from ext2 makes the design tradeoffs unusually visible: nearly every ext4 feature exists to fix a specific, nameable limitation of the ext2 structure. Reading ext4 is therefore also a way of re-reading ext2 with thirty years of hindsight.

A Family of Filesystems

Linux's first native filesystem was actually the MINIX filesystem it borrowed from the operating system Linux was first developed under; its 14-character names and 64 MiB size limit were quickly outgrown. The Extended Filesystem (ext, 1992) lifted those limits, and a year later the Second Extended Filesystem (ext2, 1993) replaced it with the robust, long-lived design that became the canonical Linux filesystem. Ext2 introduced the block-group structure, the inode layout, and the directory format that—remarkably—ext4 still uses today. Its one serious weakness was crash recovery: because a filesystem operation touches several on-disk structures that cannot all be written atomically, an unclean shutdown could leave the volume inconsistent, and the only remedy was to scan the entire volume with e2fsck, which on a large disk could take many minutes or longer.

Ext3 (2001) fixed exactly that, and nothing else: it added a journal—a log of pending changes that can be replayed after a crash—while keeping ext2's on-disk layout byte-for-byte identical, so an ext2 volume could be upgraded to ext3 in place simply by creating a journal file, and an ext3 volume could be mounted as ext2 by ignoring the journal. That strict compatibility is why the two are usually named together. Ext4 (2008) was the first to break on-disk compatibility, and it did so only where a new capability demanded it—extents, 64-bit block numbers, larger inodes, checksums—guarding each change behind a feature flag so that a volume using none of them is still a valid ext3 (and ext2) volume. The lineage is thus a chain of careful, mostly-compatible extensions rather than a series of rewrites, which is precisely what lets one driver handle the whole family.

That last point is the practical reality in Linux 7.0.12, and it is worth stating precisely. The standalone ext3 driver was removed from the kernel entirely; the fs/ext4/ code registers to handle the ext3 filesystem name and mounts those volumes itself. A separate fs/ext2/ driver does still exist, but in Linux 7.0.12 its Kconfig entry is labelled deprecated and tells users to switch to ext4, for a concrete reason: the old driver cannot represent timestamps past 03:14:07 UTC on 19 January 2038, because it never learned to read the extra timestamp bits that only a larger inode has room for. Its stated purpose now is to be kept "as a simple reference for filesystem developers." When it is not built—which is the default, since CONFIG_EXT4_USE_FOR_EXT2 is enabled whenever CONFIG_EXT2_FS is off—the ext4 code registers the ext2 name too. The driver therefore installs three file_system_type structures, ext2, ext3, and ext4, all pointing at the same mount function. This works because ext2 and ext3 are, in feature terms, strict subsets of ext4: an ext3 volume is just an ext4 volume that uses no ext4-only features, and the driver simply finds none of those features set. The journaling itself lives in a separate layer, jbd2 (fs/jbd2/), a general journaling service that ext4 drives but that is not specific to it—other subsystems could use it too. This chapter's split between "the filesystem" (ext4) and "the journal" (jbd2) mirrors that code structure and the Linux 2.6 split between ext3 and JBD.

It is worth being concrete about what happens when the ext4 driver mounts an old volume, because "one driver serves three filesystems" can sound like a compatibility shim and it is not one. There is no translation layer and no ext2 emulation mode. The driver reads the superblock, finds that HAS_JOURNAL is clear, and therefore never starts jbd2; it finds that EXTENTS is clear on every inode, and therefore maps blocks through the indirect-block code in fs/ext4/indirect.c that ext4 still carries for exactly this purpose; it finds no METADATA_CSUM, and therefore neither verifies nor writes checksums. Each ext4 feature is independently conditional on its flag, so an ext2 volume is simply the case where every condition is false. The corollary matters for the rest of the chapter: the ext2 code paths of Linux 2.6 have not been retired, they are the fallback branches that run whenever a flag is absent, and a few of them—the indirect-block mapper above all—are still reachable on volumes created today if the administrator asks for them.

Features and Feature Flags

Because one driver must serve volumes formatted anywhere from 1997 to today, ext4 needs a precise way to know what a given volume supports. That is the job of three feature-flag fields in the superblock, and their three-way split is the key to backward and forward compatibility. A flag in s_feature_compat marks a feature the driver may ignore if it does not understand it—the volume is still safe to mount and use. A flag in s_feature_incompat marks a feature that changes the format so fundamentally that a driver not understanding it must refuse to mount the volume at all. And a flag in s_feature_ro_compat is the middle case: a driver that does not understand it may still mount the volume read-only, because reading is safe but writing would corrupt the unknown structures. The magic number s_magic is 0xEF53 for all three filesystems—the same value ext2 has always used—so the feature flags, not the magic, are what distinguish an ext4 volume from an ext2 one. This design has proven durable: new capabilities are added as flags, old drivers degrade safely, and a volume carries an exact record of what it needs, checked at mount time by ext4_check_feature_compatibility().

The check itself is simpler than the contract it enforces, and reading it is the fastest way to believe the contract. The driver declares what it knows how to handle as three constant masks, EXT4_FEATURE_COMPAT_SUPP, EXT4_FEATURE_INCOMPAT_SUPP, and EXT4_FEATURE_RO_COMPAT_SUPP, and ext4_feature_set_ok() does little more than test the volume's flag words against them. If the volume has an incompat bit outside the mask the mount fails outright, naming the offending bits. If the caller asked for a read-only mount the function returns success immediately, without consulting the ro_compat word at all—which is the read-only rule stated as code rather than as policy. Only for a read-write mount is the ro_compat word tested, and an unknown bit there downgrades nothing; it simply refuses, leaving the administrator to mount read-only explicitly. The compat mask, tellingly, is barely used: nothing needs to be checked, because not understanding a compat feature is by definition harmless.

Two wrinkles in that function are worth knowing because they break the neat story. The first is that the answer does not depend on the volume alone. Casefolding is refused unless the kernel was built with CONFIG_UNICODE, and a volume with QUOTA or PROJECT cannot be mounted read-write by a kernel built without quota support—so the same disk can be fully mountable on one machine and read-only on another running the identical kernel version, differently configured. The flags describe the volume's requirements; whether they are met is a property of the kernel binary. The second is that ro_compat has an escape hatch of its own: the READONLY flag is a feature the driver does understand, and understanding it means quietly setting SB_RDONLY and mounting anyway, which is how an image can be marked permanently read-only rather than merely unsupported.

One flag in Table 18-1 is not a capability at all, and it repays a second look because it shows the mechanism being used for something else entirely. RECOVER means "this journal has not been replayed." Ext4 sets it when it mounts a volume read-write and clears it on a clean unmount, so finding it set at mount time is exactly how a crash is detected—the volume itself carries the dirty bit. What makes it instructive is that it is an incompat flag despite describing state rather than format. The reason is the three-way contract working as designed: a driver that did not understand RECOVER would mount a crashed volume, ignore the pending journal, and write over metadata whose committed updates had never been applied—so refusing the mount outright is precisely the right behavior, and the existing mechanism expresses it with no new machinery.

The three-way split is more than a convenience; it is a small, precise contract between disk formats and drivers spanning decades. The distinction turns entirely on what a driver that does not understand the feature would do wrong. A compat feature adds structures that an ignorant driver can simply not touch—a HTree directory index (DIR_INDEX) is disguised as empty directory entries, so a driver that ignores it still reads the directory correctly as a flat list, which is why it is only compat. A ro_compat feature changes the meaning of data such that reading is safe but any write by an ignorant driver would corrupt it: metadata checksums (METADATA_CSUM) are the classic case, since a driver that does not maintain the checksums would leave them stale on every write, so such a driver is allowed to mount read-only but not read-write. An incompat feature changes the on-disk format so fundamentally that even reading is unsafe—extents (EXTENTS) reinterpret the inode's block array entirely, so a driver that reads it as indirect pointers would follow garbage, which is why it must refuse the volume outright.

This is also how the family relationship is defined in practice rather than by version number. When mkfs.ext4 creates a volume it sets the ext4-only flags (extents, 64-bit, flex_bg, checksums); mkfs.ext3 sets only HAS_JOURNAL; mkfs.ext2 sets none of them. The driver does not ask "is this ext2, ext3, or ext4?"—it reads the flags and does exactly what they require, mounting read-write if it supports every incompat and ro_compat flag present, read-only if it supports the incompat ones but not all ro_compat ones, and refusing otherwise. A volume can even be upgraded in place by setting new flags with tune2fs. The feature-flag mechanism is therefore the real subject of this subsection: it is what makes "ext2, ext3, ext4" a spectrum of capability on one format rather than three separate filesystems.

Block Groups and Flexible Block Groups

An ext4 volume is divided, as ext2 was, into a sequence of block groups—equal-sized spans of blocks, each a self-contained mini-filesystem with its own bitmaps, a slice of the inode table, and data blocks. Grouping serves locality: keeping an inode, its directory entry, and its data in the same block group keeps the disk head (or, on flash, the access pattern) local. Each group holds s_blocks_per_group blocks and s_inodes_per_group inodes, and its layout is the classic one: a copy of the superblock and group descriptors (in some groups), then the block bitmap, the inode bitmap, the inode table, and finally the data blocks. Table 18-2 lists a block group's components.

Before the first block group, at the very start of the volume, comes the boot block—the first 1024 bytes, which ext4 deliberately does not use. This reservation is a holdover from when a filesystem might occupy a whole disk and its opening sector had to hold a boot loader or a master boot record and partition table; leaving it untouched lets a filesystem coexist with the boot code that a BIOS would run at power-on. It is also why the superblock sits at offset 1024 rather than 0: the boot block occupies the space before it. On a modern system the volume is almost always inside a partition and the boot block goes unused, but the format keeps the gap so that a filesystem written directly to a raw device remains bootable and so that the on-disk layout has not changed since ext2. The boot block belongs to no block group and contains no filesystem metadata—it is simply reserved space the filesystem steps over.

Two aspects of the classic layout are worth drawing out because they explain its dimensions. First, a group's size is not arbitrary: the block bitmap must fit in a single block, and with b bytes per block a bitmap covers 8b blocks, so a 4 KiB block gives 32,768 blocks per group—128 MiB. The whole filesystem is thus tiled into 128 MiB groups by this one constraint. That arithmetic is worth carrying forward, because it sets so many other ext4 quantities, and it is also the one place the classic layout has been loosened: with the BIGALLOC feature a bitmap bit represents not a block but a cluster of blocks (s_log_cluster_size), so a group still holds 32,768 bits but covers many times more space—a 16-block cluster gives 2 GiB groups. Bigalloc trades allocation granularity for a shorter bitmap and fewer groups, which is why it is aimed at volumes full of large files, and why the driver refuses to mount it read-write on a volume without extents.

Second, the superblock and group-descriptor copies are not in every group. Storing a backup in all of them would waste space and, worse, mean every group's first blocks are metadata; the sparse_super feature instead keeps backups only in groups 0 and 1 and in those whose number is a power of 3, 5, or 7. The test is ext4_bg_has_super() in fs/ext4/balloc.c, and it is three lines worth reading, because it rejects every even-numbered group before it bothers testing the powers at all. The backups thin out geometrically, so a volume of any size carries a manageable handful rather than thousands—and sparse_super2 reduces it further still, keeping just two, whose group numbers are recorded explicitly in s_backup_bgs rather than derived from a rule. This matters more than it sounds on a very large volume, because a backup is not one block but the superblock and the whole group-descriptor table, which on a petabyte-scale filesystem is itself substantial.

The descriptor table raises a scaling problem of its own that the classic layout did not have to face. Every group's descriptor lives in one table at the front of the volume, so the table grows with the volume, and it must also be able to grow in place if the filesystem is ever resized. Ext4 answers that twice. The RESIZE_INODE feature reserves a run of blocks after the descriptor table, owned by inode 7, purely so that the table has room to expand when groups are appended, which is what makes online growth possible without relocating user data. And for volumes too large for one contiguous table, META_BG abandons the single table altogether, splitting the descriptors into metagroups—each one block of descriptors, stored alongside the groups it describes—from s_first_meta_bg onward, so the table's size stops being a limit on the volume's. These are ext2-era bones being extended rather than replaced—the same pattern as everywhere else in this chapter.

The change ext4 makes here is flexible block groups (flex_bg). Rather than each group storing its own bitmaps and inode table interspersed with its data, flex_bg gathers the metadata of several consecutive groups—2^s_log_groups_per_flex of them, typically 16—into the first group of the set, leaving the others as contiguous runs of pure data blocks. This packs the small, frequently-accessed metadata together (good for caching and for reading it in bulk at mount) and leaves long uninterrupted stretches for file data, which the extent-based allocator can fill with large contiguous extents. The benefit compounds with extents and delayed allocation: because a flex group offers a data run many times larger than a single classic group's, a large file can be laid down as one enormous extent rather than being chopped at every group boundary by interposed bitmaps—so the three ext4 features reinforce one another. Two further optimizations ride along: an uninitialized group (marked EXT4_BG_BLOCK_UNINIT or EXT4_BG_INODE_UNINIT in its descriptor) has never been used and so needs no on-disk bitmap or zeroed inode table at all, which lets mkfs format a multi-terabyte volume in seconds and lets e2fsck skip empty groups. On-Disk Layout

With the volume divided into block groups, this section walks the concrete structures those groups contain: the superblock that describes the whole volume, the group descriptors and bitmaps that track free space, the inode that describes each file, the directory entries that name files, the extended attributes that hang extra information off an inode, and the checksums that guard all of it. These are the ext2 on-disk data structures, extended for ext4.

The Superblock

The superblock (struct ext4_super_block, fs/ext4/ext4.h) describes the volume as a whole: how many inodes and blocks it has, how big a block is, how many blocks and inodes each group holds, the feature flags of the previous section, the volume UUID, and much more. It lives at a fixed offset (1024 bytes) from the start of the volume so it can be found before anything else is known, and backup copies are kept in some block groups so a damaged primary can be recovered. Several of these fields did not exist in ext2 and mark ext4's growth. The _hi halves of the block counts implement 64-bit addressing, lifting the volume-size ceiling from 16 TiB to 1 EiB. s_inode_size records that ext4 inodes are no longer a fixed 128 bytes but a larger, configurable size (commonly 256), leaving room for nanosecond timestamps and extended attributes. And s_checksum_seed together with s_checksum anchor the metadata-checksum scheme described at the end of this section. The kernel reads the on-disk superblock once at mount into a buffer, then works from an in-memory descriptor (§4) that points back at it.

The superblock's fixed location is what bootstraps the whole mount. Nothing about a volume can be read until its geometry is known—block size, group size, where the inode tables are—and all of that lives in the superblock, so it must sit at an address the kernel can compute without any prior knowledge. That address is byte offset 1024, chosen so the first 1024 bytes are left for a boot sector or partition label, and it is the same on every ext filesystem from ext2 onward. From the superblock the kernel derives everything else: the number of block groups (total blocks divided by s_blocks_per_group), the size and location of the group descriptor table that follows it, and so on. Because a corrupt primary superblock would render the volume unmountable, the sparse-super backups of the previous section are the safety net—e2fsck -b can point the kernel at a backup copy to recover.

The volume also reserves the first several inode numbers for fixed purposes, another ext2 convention ext4 keeps. Inode 1 (EXT4_BAD_INO) collects known bad blocks; inode 2 (EXT4_ROOT_INO) is the root directory /, the anchor from which all path resolution begins; inode 7 (EXT4_RESIZE_INO) reserves descriptor blocks for online resizing; and inode 8 (EXT4_JOURNAL_INO) is the internal journal file that s_journal_inum normally points at. The first inode available for ordinary files is 11 (EXT4_GOOD_OLD_FIRST_INO). This fixed numbering means the root directory is always findable—the kernel reads inode 2 to begin any lookup—without storing its location anywhere. The numbering is itself negotiable: s_first_ino records where ordinary files actually begin, so the reserved range can be extended on a volume that needs more of them, and the constant 11 is only the value a classic volume carries. Not all of the superblock describes the volume's shape. A surprising fraction of it is a logbook, and this part has grown considerably since ext2. s_state is the cleanliness bit: EXT4_VALID_FS means the volume was unmounted cleanly, and the driver clears it on a read-write mount and sets it again at unmount, so s_state and the RECOVER flag of §1.2 between them tell a recovering system both that the volume was in use and whether a journal is waiting. Two further bits record that errors have been seen (EXT4_ERROR_FS) and that orphan inodes are being cleaned up (EXT4_ORPHAN_FS). Alongside it, s_errors records what the administrator wants done the next time something goes wrong—continue, remount read-only, or panic—which is why that policy survives a reboot without being in /etc/fstab.

The error bookkeeping goes further than a flag, and it is worth knowing about because it is the first thing to look at on a volume that has misbehaved. The superblock keeps a running s_error_count and, for both the first and the most recent error, the time it happened, the inode and block involved, and—remarkably—the name of the kernel function and the line number that reported it, in s_first_error_func and s_first_error_line. A filesystem that has been silently corrupting itself therefore carries a small, persistent incident report that dumpe2fs will print long after the messages have rolled out of the kernel log. Two other counters live here for different reasons: s_kbytes_written accumulates the lifetime kilobytes written to the volume, which is how a flash device's wear can be estimated from the filesystem side, and s_mnt_count against s_max_mnt_count, with s_lastcheck, is the old mechanism that forces a periodic e2fsck after enough mounts or enough time—usually disabled now, but still the reason an occasional boot used to pause to check the disk.

One superblock field points at a structure that solves a problem none of the others address: s_mmp_block, the block used by multi-mount protection. On shared storage—a SAN volume, or an image visible to two virtual machines—nothing in the filesystem prevents two kernels from mounting the same device read-write at once, which destroys it almost immediately, and there is no lock manager available to arbitrate. Ext4's answer is to use the only thing both nodes can see: the disk. A mounted volume runs a kmmpd kernel thread that rewrites a sequence number into the MMP block every s_mmp_update_interval seconds, along with the node's hostname and device name. A node that wants to mount reads the block, sleeps for roughly twice the interval, and reads it again; if the sequence changed in the meantime, somebody else is alive on this volume and the mount is refused, naming the machine responsible. Two sequence values are reserved rather than counted—one meaning cleanly unmounted and one meaning e2fsck is running—so the same block also keeps a repair tool and a mount from colliding. It is a deliberately crude mechanism, a heartbeat with a timeout instead of a lock, but it needs no infrastructure beyond the block device itself, which is exactly the constraint.

Group Descriptors and Bitmaps

Immediately after the superblock comes the group descriptor table, one struct ext4_group_desc per block group, recording where that group's metadata lives and how much of it is free. Each descriptor holds the block numbers of the group's block bitmap, inode bitmap, and inode table, plus running counts of free blocks, free inodes, and directories, and a set of flags. As with the superblock, ext4 widened it: the block pointers and counts gained _hi halves for 64-bit volumes, so the descriptor grew from 32 to 64 bytes (recorded in s_desc_size). Table 18-4 lists the key fields.

Finding a group's descriptor is the same kind of arithmetic that finds an inode, and just as cheap. The descriptors are packed in order, so ext4_get_group_desc() in fs/ext4/balloc.c divides the group number by the number of descriptors per block to pick the table block and takes the remainder as the offset within it; because both quantities are powers of two, the two operations are a shift and a mask. The table blocks themselves are pinned in memory for the life of the mount in the s_group_desc array of buffer heads, so the lookup touches no disk at all. That is why the descriptor table is worth keeping small: on a volume with tens of thousands of groups it is read once and then consulted constantly, and every byte added to a descriptor is a byte multiplied by the group count and held resident. It is also why the widening to 64 bytes was made conditional on 64BIT rather than unconditional.

The two bitmaps a descriptor points at are the heart of free-space tracking, unchanged in concept from ext2: the block bitmap is one bit per block in the group (set if the block is in use), and the inode bitmap is one bit per inode. Each fits in a single block, which bounds a group's size—with 4 KiB blocks a bitmap covers 32,768 blocks, so a group spans 128 MiB. What ext4 adds is the uninitialized optimization already mentioned: a group that has never allocated anything sets BLOCK_UNINIT/INODE_UNINIT in its descriptor and stores no bitmap at all—the kernel treats it as all-free—and bg_itable_unused lets tools skip the never-touched tail of the inode table. This laziness is also what makes mkfs.ext4 finish in a second on a multi-terabyte volume: rather than write zeroed inode tables across the whole disk up front, it marks the groups uninitialized and leaves the zeroing to the kernel. A per-filesystem ext4_lazyinit thread, started at mount and staggered by a random delay of up to five seconds so that several volumes do not all begin at once, walks the uninitialized groups in the background and zeroes their inode tables, throttling itself to keep the work far below the foreground I/O rate: it times how long the first group takes and then waits ten times that long between groups, so a slow disk is left alone proportionally more than a fast one. The multiplier is the init_itable= mount option. Until that thread reaches a group, an allocation into it simply initializes the group on the spot. Together these turn formatting and checking a huge, mostly-empty volume from an operation proportional to its size into one proportional to its use. Figure 18-3 shows how a descriptor ties a group together.

The free-space counts in the descriptor exist to spare the allocator from consulting the bitmaps for every decision. When ext4 needs to place a new inode or a run of blocks, it can scan the compact descriptor table—one small record per 128 MiB group—to find a group with enough room before ever reading a bitmap, and only then examines that group's bitmap for the exact bits. The bg_used_dirs_count serves the Orlov directory allocator of §6, which balances directories across groups by preferring those holding fewer of them. Keeping these counts accurate is part of what a journaled operation must do: allocating a block decrements bg_free_blocks_count and flips a bitmap bit, and both changes are journaled together so a crash cannot leave the count and the bitmap disagreeing.

There is a subtlety in how the two bitmaps differ in consequence. A wrong bit in the block bitmap wastes or double-allocates disk space; a wrong bit in the inode bitmap can orphan or double-use a file. Both are guarded in ext4 by their own checksums (the bitmap-csum fields in the descriptor), so corruption is caught on read rather than acted upon. And the block bitmap, unlike the inode bitmap, must account for the group's own metadata: the blocks holding the superblock copy, descriptors, bitmaps, and inode table are marked in-use in the bitmap from the start, so the allocator never hands them out as data. This is why a freshly-formatted group is not entirely free even before any file is written—its overhead blocks are already reserved—and why the uninitialized optimization is expressed as a descriptor flag rather than an all-zero bitmap: the flag lets the kernel compute the overhead on the fly instead of storing a bitmap that merely records it.

The Inode

Every file, directory, symlink, and device node is described by an inode (struct ext4_inode), stored in its group's inode table. The inode holds the file's mode and permissions, owner and group, size, timestamps, link count, block count, flags, and—most importantly—the information needed to find its data. The classic fields are all still there, carried over from ext2, but ext4 has extended the inode in three visible ways: it is physically larger, its timestamps carry nanosecond precision and a wider epoch, and its 60-byte i_block area is reinterpreted. The i_block array is the crux of the ext2-to-ext4 change and deserves emphasis. In ext2 those 15 words were always block pointers: 12 direct, then one single-, one double-, and one triple-indirect pointer. Ext4 keeps that meaning for inodes without the EXTENTS_FL flag (so old files still work), but for a modern file the same 60 bytes instead hold the root of an extent tree—a small header plus a handful of extent records—which §3 examines. And for a very small file or directory, the INLINE_DATA_FL flag repurposes the area yet again to hold the file's actual contents inline, avoiding a data block entirely. One field, three meanings, selected by inode flags: it is a compact example of how ext4 layered new capability onto ext2's skeleton without breaking it. The extra inode space beyond the original 128 bytes, sized by i_extra_isize, is where the nanosecond timestamp halves, the creation time, and inline extended attributes live. The larger inode is what makes several ext4 features possible at once, and it is worth seeing why the size is negotiated rather than fixed. The classic ext2 inode was exactly 128 bytes (EXT4_GOOD_OLD_INODE_SIZE). Ext4 records a possibly larger size in the superblock's s_inode_size, and within each inode i_extra_isize says how much of the space past the first 128 bytes is actually used—so the format can grow over time without every field moving. That extra room is dense with additions: the high halves of the timestamps (the i_*time_extra fields, which encode nanoseconds in the upper 30 bits and two epoch bits in the lower two, which are added to the high bits of the seconds count and so push the range past the year-2038 32-bit limit to roughly the year 2446), the creation time i_crtime that ext2 never had, the inode checksum, the project id for project quotas, and space for small extended attributes stored inline. A single 128-byte inode could hold none of these; the negotiated larger inode holds them all, which is why the size is a superblock parameter rather than a constant.

The negotiation has a second half that is easy to miss, because i_extra_isize is a property of each inode, not of the volume. An inode written by an older kernel may declare a smaller extra area than the current one would like to use, so the superblock carries s_want_extra_isize—how much space new inodes should reserve—and the driver grows an existing inode's extra area when it can, which sometimes means evicting an inline extended attribute to an external block to make room. This is the mechanism behind an otherwise puzzling behavior: a volume created with 128-byte inodes can never gain nanosecond timestamps or a creation time, no matter how new the kernel, because there is physically nowhere to put them and the inode size cannot be changed after mkfs. It is also the reason the deprecated ext2 driver's 2038 problem is not simply a bug to be fixed—the fields it would need may not exist on the volume at all.

Two small fields repay a second look because they mean less than they seem. i_links_count is the hard-link count—the number of directory entries pointing at this inode—and when it reaches zero (no name refers to the file) and no process has it open, the inode and its data are freed; this is the mechanism behind unlink(). i_blocks_lo counts the blocks the file occupies, but historically in 512-byte units rather than filesystem blocks, and with the HUGE_FILE feature it can switch to counting in filesystem-block units to represent very large files—a quirk that trips up naïve size calculations. The i_generation field, not in the table, is a version number bumped each time an inode number is reused, so that a stale reference (such as an NFS file handle) to an inode that has since been recycled for a different file can be detected rather than silently following the wrong file.

It is worth spelling out how an inode number becomes a disk address, since that translation is the reason the whole block-group scheme exists and it is pure arithmetic with nothing stored. Inode numbers start at 1, so ext4 subtracts one and divides: the group is the reduced number divided by s_inodes_per_group, and the index within that group's inode table is the remainder. The group's descriptor gives the first block of its inode table; the index divided by the number of inodes per block selects the block within it, and the remainder times s_inode_size gives the byte offset inside that block. This is __ext4_get_inode_loc() in fs/ext4/inode.c, and the whole of it is two divisions and two modulos. Three consequences follow. Locating any inode costs exactly one block read once the descriptor table is cached, with no index to traverse. The total inode count is fixed when the volume is created, because moving the tables would change every inode's address—which is why mkfs asks how many inodes you want and why a filesystem can run out of them with free space remaining. And an inode number is meaningless outside its volume, since it encodes a position in this geometry.

Directory Entries and HTree

A directory is an ordinary file whose data blocks hold a sequence of directory entries, each a struct ext4_dir_entry_2 mapping a name to an inode number. An entry stores the inode number, a record length that lets entries be variable-sized and deletions be handled by merging free space, the name length, a file-type byte (so the kernel can answer readdir type queries without reading each inode), and the name itself. This format is essentially unchanged from ext2. Reading a directory linearly is fine for a handful of entries, but a directory with thousands of files would make every lookup an O(n) scan.

The record-length field is the clever part of the linear format and worth understanding before the index is layered on. Each entry's rec_len is the distance to the next entry, which is normally just big enough for this entry's name but can be larger. Deleting a file does not shift the blocks that follow; instead the deleted entry's space is absorbed by extending the previous entry's rec_len to skip over it, so the gap becomes reusable free space that a later create can subdivide. This is why a directory that once held many files stays physically large after they are deleted—the record lengths carry the empty space rather than reclaiming it. The file-type byte, meanwhile, is a pure optimization: it lets readdir report whether each name is a file, directory, or symlink without opening every inode, which for a large directory saves an enormous number of inode reads. (That same byte position is overloaded, in the checksum tail entry, as the sentinel value 0xDE.)

Ext4's answer, inherited from late ext3 and enabled by the DIR_INDEX feature, is the HTree—a hashed B-tree index layered invisibly on top of the linear directory. The trick is that the index blocks masquerade as empty directory entries (a large entry with inode 0), so a filesystem that does not understand HTree still reads the directory correctly as a flat list, while one that does can hash a target name and descend the tree to the one block that could contain it, turning lookup in a million-entry directory from a linear scan into a couple of block reads. The index root and nodes (struct dx_root, struct dx_node in fs/ext4/namei.c) are keyed by a hash of the filename. This backward-compatible layering is characteristic of the whole ext family.

Two design choices make the HTree practical. First, it is deliberately shallow—one or two levels by default—rather than a deep tree: a two-level index over 4 KiB blocks already addresses millions of entries, so a lookup is at most a few block reads, and the code never has to balance a tall tree. Second, the hash is seeded per-directory (the seed lives in the index root, defaulting to a volume-wide value) and a specific half-MD4 or TEA hash is used rather than a general-purpose one, chosen to spread real filenames evenly across buckets so that no single leaf block overflows under a natural naming scheme like file0001…file9999. When a leaf does fill, it is split and the index updated, but because entries are found by hash rather than by position, the underlying linear directory can still be read in its physical order by any tool that ignores the index—so fsck and old drivers see a valid flat directory while modern lookups get logarithmic speed. It is one of the most elegant examples of ext4's habit of hiding new structure inside old.

The two-level ceiling is not absolute. It is a real limit—with 4 KiB blocks it caps a single directory at a few tens of millions of entries and, because the index is addressed by logical block number in a 32-bit field, at 4 GiB of directory data—and workloads that keep one enormous directory used to hit it. The largedir incompatible feature raises the depth to three levels, which is what ext4_dir_htree_level() in fs/ext4/ext4.h returns when the flag is set; without it the code uses the compatible limit of two. The third level costs one more block read on every lookup, which is why it is opt-in rather than the default: most directories never need it, and paying for it everywhere would be a poor trade.

One later addition bends the directory format further without changing it. The casefold feature lets a directory be marked case-insensitive, so that README and readme name the same file—behavior Windows and Android applications expect and that user space previously had to emulate. Ext4 implements it by mounting the volume with a Unicode encoding (s_encoding) and then deferring to the generic case-insensitive dentry operations in fs/libfs.c, which compare names with utf8_strncasecmp; for a directory carrying the flag, ext4 hashes the casefolded form of the name rather than the bytes on disk. Hashing the folded form is the load-bearing detail: it is what allows the HTree index to keep working, because every spelling of a name lands in the same bucket. The names themselves are still stored exactly as they were created, so the directory remains readable by a driver that knows nothing of casefolding—the same compatibility-through-concealment the index itself relies on.

Combining casefolding with encryption breaks the scheme, and the way ext4 repairs it is instructive. On an encrypted directory the names stored on disk are ciphertext, so a lookup performed without the key cannot fold them—and even with the key, hashing the folded plaintext would mean decrypting every name just to find one. Ext4 sidesteps this by storing the hash in the directory entry itself for directories that are both casefolded and encrypted, the case ext4_hash_in_dirent() tests, and by switching those directories to a keyed SipHash (DX_HASH_SIPHASH) instead of the half-MD4 or TEA hash used elsewhere. A keyed hash is necessary because the stored value would otherwise leak information about the plaintext name to anyone who can read the directory block without the key. The hash version is recorded per directory in the index root, so the three schemes—legacy, half-MD4 or TEA, and SipHash—coexist on one volume, and a directory carries the rule for reading itself.

Extended Attributes

Not everything a modern system wants to record about a file fits in the inode's fixed fields. An access control list, a SELinux label, a capability set, an encryption policy—all of these are name/value pairs attached to a file rather than properties the filesystem itself understands. The VFS calls them extended attributes, and ext4 implements them in fs/ext4/xattr.c, one of the larger files in the driver. They deserve their own look because three features described later in this chapter are built on them: POSIX ACLs are stored as extended attributes, fscrypt keeps each directory's encryption policy in one, and inline data (§8.2) spills the tail of a small file into one.

Ext4 stores an inode's attributes in two places, and tries the cheap one first. The in-inode area is the space between the end of the 128-byte classic inode body plus i_extra_isize and the end of the inode slot—the same negotiated tail that holds the extra timestamp halves and the inode checksum. It begins with a four-byte ext4_xattr_ibody_header whose magic is 0xEA020000, followed by attribute entries. With the 256-byte inodes mkfs.ext4 creates by default there are roughly a hundred bytes free here, which is enough for a SELinux label or an encryption context, and an attribute stored there costs no extra disk access at all: it is already in the buffer the inode was read from. Only when the in-inode area overflows does ext4 allocate a separate block, recording its number in the inode field still named i_file_acl—a fossil from the days when an ACL was the only thing anyone stored out of line. That block starts with a fuller ext4_xattr_header carrying a magic, a reference count, a hash over all the attributes it holds, and its own crc32c checksum.

The entry format is compact and slightly counter-intuitive, because names and values grow toward each other. Each ext4_xattr_entry stores a name length, a one-byte name index, the offset and size of the value, and a hash, followed by the name characters themselves—entries are packed from the start of the area forward, while their values are packed from the end backward, so the free space is the gap in the middle and neither side needs to be relocated when the other grows. The name index is a small dictionary-compression trick: rather than store the string security.selinux, the entry stores index 6 for the security. prefix and only the suffix selinux. The defined indexes cover user. (1), the access and default POSIX ACLs (2 and 3), trusted. (4), security. (6), and system. (7), with further numbers reserved for richacls, encryption, and the Hurd. It is a small saving per attribute, but extended attributes are set on every file on a system running SELinux, so it is a saving repeated hundreds of thousands of times.

Two refinements show how much pressure this path takes on such a system. Because a labelled tree tends to give thousands of files the identical attribute set, an external attribute block is shareable: its h_refcount lets up to 1024 inodes point at the same block, and ext4 finds candidates through an mb_cache keyed by the header's hash, so labelling a whole directory tree can cost one block rather than one block per file. Setting an attribute on a shared block does not modify it—the block is cloned first and the old reference dropped, copy-on-write in miniature. The second refinement lifts the size ceiling. An out-of-line value must otherwise fit within a single block, so with 4 KiB blocks no attribute could exceed a few kilobytes; the ea_inode feature lets an entry set e_value_inum instead of an offset, storing the value in a whole hidden inode of its own. The value then has a file's addressing behind it rather than a block's, which is what allows the 64 KiB maximum the VFS advertises.

Metadata Checksums

The most pervasive ext4 addition invisible in the layout is metadata checksumming. With the metadata_csum feature, nearly every metadata structure carries a crc32c checksum: the superblock (s_checksum), each group descriptor and its bitmaps, each inode (i_checksum_lo/_hi), each extent-tree block, and each directory block (in a trailing checksum entry). The purpose is early, reliable detection of corruption—a bad disk sector, a misdirected write, or memory error that flips a metadata bit is caught when the structure is read, rather than silently propagating into worse damage. Ext2 had no such protection; a corrupted block pointer would simply be followed.

The checksums are seeded so they are both position-dependent and stable. Each is computed with crc32c over the structure's contents together with a per-volume seed and usually the object's own identity (its inode number, block group, or block number), so an otherwise-identical block copied to the wrong location fails its checksum. The seed is normally derived from the volume UUID, but the CSUM_SEED feature stores an explicit s_checksum_seed in the superblock instead, so that the UUID can be changed on a live filesystem without having to recompute every checksum on the volume. This checksum layer, spanning every structure this section described, is one of the clearest expressions of ext4's priority on integrity for the large, long-lived volumes it is built to hold.

Two mechanical details recur throughout the implementation and explain some otherwise-odd field placements. The first is that a structure cannot include its own checksum in the checksummed range, so ext4 checksums the bytes up to the field, then feeds in a zero of the field's width, then continues past it—visible in ext4_group_desc_csum() and ext4_inode_csum() as a dummy_csum variable. The second is that the original 128-byte inode had no spare 32-bit field to hold a checksum, so the inode's is split: the low half lives in i_checksum_lo inside the classic area and the high half in i_checksum_hi out in the extra area, which means an inode on a volume formatted with 128-byte inodes is protected by only sixteen bits. The inode checksum is seeded per inode with the volume seed mixed with the inode number and its generation count, so a stale inode image from a previous incarnation of the same number fails, and it covers the whole inode including the inline extended attributes of §2.5. The group descriptor's checksum is similarly seeded with its group number, and is sixteen bits rather than thirty-two because the field predates metadata_csum: the earlier uninit_bg feature introduced bg_checksum as a crc16 purely to make the uninitialized-group flags safe to trust, and metadata_csum reused the field by truncating a crc32c into it rather than growing the descriptor.

The algorithm is nominally negotiable and in practice is not. s_checksum_type names the function, but crc32c is the only value the driver accepts, and a volume declaring anything else is refused at mount. This is a deliberate narrowing: crc32c has hardware acceleration on both x86_64 and ARM64, so the checksums cost close to nothing, and supporting a second algorithm would mean carrying a slow path for no benefit. What happens when a check fails is governed by the s_errors policy of §2.1: the driver reports the failure through ext4_error(), which records the incident in the superblock and then continues, remounts read-only, or panics. A checksum detects corruption; it does not correct it, and ext4 stores no redundancy from which a bad structure could be rebuilt—the backup superblocks and descriptors of §1.3 are the only copies of anything, and they are stale by design.

It is important to be clear about what these checksums do and do not protect, because the boundary is a deliberate design decision. Metadata checksums cover only metadata—the structures that describe where data lives and how the filesystem is organized. They do not cover file data: ext4 has no per-block data checksum, so a bit-flip in a file's contents is not detected by the filesystem (that is the domain of fs-verity for read-only files, or of a checksumming filesystem like btrfs or ZFS). The reasoning is that metadata corruption is catastrophic—a bad block pointer can lose or cross-link entire files—while a data-block error damages only the one file that owns it, and checksumming every data block on every read and write would be a heavy, always-on cost. Ext4 spends its integrity budget where the leverage is highest.

The checksum scheme also extends into the journal, which matters for crash consistency. Jbd2 can checksum its own transactions (the CSUM_V3 journal feature), so that recovery after a crash can verify a transaction was written completely before replaying it—a torn write that left a transaction half on disk is detected and discarded rather than replayed into corruption. Combined with the per-structure checksums, this means both the steady-state filesystem and its recovery log are guarded, so the two ways a volume can be damaged—bad media under normal operation, and an interrupted write during a crash—are both caught. This end-to-end integrity story, absent entirely from the ext2/ext3 of Linux 2.6, is a large part of why ext4 remains trusted for volumes measured in terabytes and lifetimes measured in years.

Extents

The single largest change from ext2 to ext4, and the reason the i_block array is reinterpreted, is the move from indirect block maps to extents. This section explains why the old scheme did not scale, how the extent tree replaces it, and the unwritten extents that support preallocation.

Why Extents Replaced Indirect Blocks

Ext2 mapped a file's logical blocks to physical ones with an indirect block scheme: the first 12 blocks are named by direct pointers in the inode, and larger files use a single indirect block (a block full of pointers), then a double indirect block (a block of pointers to blocks of pointers), then a triple. It works, but it has two costs that grew unbearable as files and disks grew. First, it is pointer-dense: every single block of a large file needs its own 4-byte pointer somewhere, so a 1 GiB file needs about a megabyte of indirect blocks, and reading or truncating the file means walking all of them. Second, it records no notion of contiguity: even if a file's blocks are perfectly sequential on disk, the map still stores one pointer per block, so the common case—large contiguous files—is represented as wastefully as the worst case.

An extent captures contiguity directly. A single struct ext4_extent records "logical block L for len blocks lives at physical block P," so one extent can map up to 32,768 contiguous blocks—128 MiB with 4 KiB blocks—in a single 12-byte record. A large file laid out contiguously might need just one extent where ext2 needed thousands of pointers. This shrinks the metadata, makes sequential access and truncation cheap, and aligns the on-disk map with how the multi-block allocator (§6) actually places files—in large contiguous runs. The 12-byte record earns its efficiency from a careful field split, and the numbers are worth seeing because they set the format's limits. The physical start is 48 bits—ee_start_lo (32 bits) plus ee_start_hi (16 bits)—which is what makes ext4 a 48-bit-block filesystem, addressing up to 2^48 blocks (1 EiB at 4 KiB). The logical start ee_block is 32 bits, so a file's block index fits in 4 billion blocks (16 TiB), which is the practical single-file ceiling. And the length ee_len is 16 bits, capping one extent at 65,536 blocks—but ext4 reserves the top bit as the "unwritten" flag, so a written extent maxes out at 32,768 blocks. These three limits—1 EiB volume, 16 TiB file, 128 MiB per extent—all fall directly out of how the 12 bytes are divided.

Extents are not a free win in every case, and it is honest to say where they help less. Their advantage is proportional to contiguity: a file the allocator could lay down in a few large runs needs only a few extents, but a badly fragmented file—one written in tiny scattered pieces on a nearly-full disk—may need almost as many extents as it has blocks, at which point the extent map is no smaller than the old indirect map and the tree grows deeper. This is why extents work hand in hand with the multi-block allocator and delayed allocation of §6, whose whole purpose is to produce contiguity so that extents stay compact. Extents give the format the ability to represent a contiguous file cheaply; the allocator's job is to make files contiguous in the first place. The two features are designed together, which is why ext4 introduced them together.

Which map a file uses is a property of the file, not only of the volume. The EXT4_EXTENTS_FL inode flag selects between the two, and it is set when the inode is created on a volume carrying the extents feature; a file created earlier keeps its indirect map forever unless something converts it. Something can: EXT4_IOC_MIGRATE, reachable through chattr +e, runs ext4_ext_migrate() to build an extent tree describing the same blocks and swap it in, and ext4_ind_migrate() goes the other way for a file whose map is simple enough to express with indirect blocks. Neither moves any data—they rewrite only the map—so migration does not defragment a file, and a fragmented file converted to extents simply gets a large extent tree. This is also why fs/ext4/indirect.c is still nearly 1,500 lines in 7.0.12: the indirect implementation is not legacy code kept for sentiment but the live path for every ext2 and ext3 volume the driver mounts, and for extent-less files on ext4 volumes.

The Extent Tree

Extents are organized as a shallow B-tree rooted in the inode. The 60-byte i_block area holds an ext4_extent_header followed by up to four entries. For a small or contiguous file, those entries are leaf extents and the whole map fits in the inode—no external metadata blocks at all. For a larger or more fragmented file, the in-inode entries become index nodes (ext4_extent_idx) pointing at external extent blocks, each of which holds its own header and a larger array of extents or further index nodes. The header's eh_depth gives the tree's height and eh_magic (0xF30A) identifies a valid node. To map a logical block, the kernel starts at the inode root and, at each level, binary-searches the sorted entries (ext4_ext_binsearch) to find the child or extent covering the target, descending until it reaches a leaf—so even a badly fragmented large file is mapped in a handful of steps rather than a long pointer walk. Figure 18-5 contrasts the two schemes.

The tree stays remarkably shallow, and it is worth doing the arithmetic to see why. The inode root holds at most 4 entries, but a full 4 KiB extent block holds a 12-byte header plus 340 twelve-byte records. So a depth-1 tree (index entries in the inode pointing at leaf blocks) maps up to 4 × 340 ≈ 1,360 extents, and a depth-2 tree maps 4 × 340 × 340 ≈ 462,000 extents; since each extent can cover 128 MiB, even a depth-1 tree describes enormous files when they are reasonably contiguous. In practice the extent tree is almost never deeper than one or two levels, so mapping a block is one binary search in the inode plus at most one or two block reads—far cheaper than the up-to-three indirect-block reads ext2 needed, and cheaper still when the whole map fits in the inode. The eh_max field in each header records how many entries the node can hold, so the code knows when a node is full and must be split.

The shallowness is not merely typical, it is enforced. Depth is capped at EXT4_MAX_EXTENT_DEPTH, which is 5, and a node claiming more is rejected as EFSCORRUPTED before it is walked. The cap serves two purposes beyond sanity: it bounds the recursion and the stack of ext4_ext_path entries the walker carries, and it lets ext4 compute an upper bound on the journal credits a tree modification can need, since a split can in the worst case dirty one block per level plus the inode. A structure with unbounded depth would make that estimate impossible, and jbd2 requires the estimate up front. It is also worth being precise about a word used loosely above: the extent tree is B-tree-shaped rather than a B-tree. Nodes split when they fill and the tree grows a level when the root fills, but there is no rebalancing and no merging of underfull nodes—deleting extents can leave a node nearly empty and it stays that way until truncation removes it. The asymmetry is deliberate: keeping a strict balance invariant would mean touching sibling nodes on ordinary writes, and every such touch is another block the transaction must carry.

Every node is self-describing and self-verifying, which matters for a structure the kernel walks on every file access. The ext4_extent_header at the start of each node carries eh_magic = 0xF30A to catch a block that has been corrupted into looking like an extent node, eh_entries and eh_max to bound the search, and eh_depth so the walker knows whether the entries that follow are index nodes or leaf extents without tracking depth separately. With metadata_csum, each external extent block also ends in an ext4_extent_tail holding a checksum over the block, so corruption of the map itself is caught on read rather than followed to the wrong physical blocks. This is the same integrity discipline the on-disk-layout section described, applied to the one structure whose corruption would most directly lose a file's data.

The checking goes considerably beyond the magic number, and it happens on every node the walker touches rather than only at fsck time. ext4_valid_extent_entries() in fs/ext4/extents.c verifies that eh_entries does not exceed what a node of this size and depth can hold, that no extent has zero length or a logical range that wraps around, that the entries are sorted by logical block and do not overlap one another, and that the first entry of a leaf begins at exactly the logical block the index entry above it claimed—so an index and its child cannot disagree. Every physical block number, in both leaves and index nodes, is passed through ext4_inode_block_valid(), which rejects a range that would overlap the filesystem's own metadata. That last check is the one that matters most: an extent pointing into the inode table or a group descriptor is the difference between a corrupted file and a corrupted filesystem, and refusing to follow it confines the damage to the file that owns the bad map.

One further operation is worth naming because it inverts the usual access pattern. ext4_ext_precache() walks a file's entire extent tree in one pass and loads every extent into the in-memory extent-status tree of §4.3, rather than fetching mappings a lookup at a time. Ext4 does this when the whole map is about to be needed anyway—the FIEMAP ioctl is the obvious case—because reading the tree in one sweep is far kinder to the disk than the repeated root-to-leaf descents a per-block lookup performs, and it leaves the mapping cached for whatever follows.

Unwritten Extents

Extents also solve a problem indirect blocks could not express cleanly: preallocation. An application that knows it will write a large file can ask the kernel (through fallocate()) to reserve the space now, so the blocks are allocated contiguously and the later writes cannot fail for lack of space. But those blocks must not be readable as the stale data that happens to be on disk, which would be an information leak. An extent solves this with an unwritten flag: the high bit of ee_len marks an extent whose blocks are allocated but not yet written, and a read of an unwritten extent returns zeros without touching the disk. When the application finally writes part of the range, ext4 splits the extent, converting the written portion to a normal extent and leaving the rest unwritten. This is why an unwritten extent's maximum length (32,767 blocks) is one less than a written one's—the top bit of the length field is spent on the flag. Preallocation with unwritten extents gives databases and virtual-machine images contiguous, guaranteed space without the cost or the security risk of pre-zeroing it.

Converting an unwritten extent as data arrives is a small but instructive piece of bookkeeping. A write into the middle of an unwritten extent must split it into up to three pieces—an unwritten head, a now-written middle, and an unwritten tail—because the record can hold only one state for its whole range; ext4 does this in ext4_ext_map_blocks when a write lands on unwritten blocks. As adjacent pieces become written, the reverse happens: ext4 merges neighboring extents that are contiguous in both logical and physical space back into one, so a file that is filled in sequentially collapses back toward a single extent rather than accumulating fragments. This split-and-merge churn is why the number of extents a file has can rise and fall over its life, and why the extent-status tree of the next section caches the current mapping in memory to avoid re-walking the on-disk tree through all this change.

Splitting is not always the cheapest answer, and ext4 will sometimes take the opposite one. A split turns one extent record into two or three and costs a tree entry permanently; physically writing zeros over the leftover unwritten blocks costs one small I/O once and leaves a single written extent behind. So when the unwritten remainder is small, the driver zeroes it instead. The threshold is s_extent_max_zeroout_kb, 32 KiB by default and exposed for tuning as /sys/fs/ext4/<device>/extent_max_zeroout_kb, and the substitution is permitted only when the extent lies wholly inside the file's current size—zeroing past end-of-file would write blocks the file does not yet logically own. ext4_ext_convert_to_initialized() therefore weighs five outcomes: split into three, split into two with the head zeroed, split into two with the tail zeroed, split into two with no zeroing, or no split at all with the edges zeroed. The trade being made is fragmentation against I/O, and it is resolved in favor of keeping the tree small, because a tree entry is paid for on every future lookup while the zeroing is paid for once.

There is also a timing constraint that shapes the write path, and it explains a piece of machinery that would otherwise look gratuitous. Converting an extent modifies the tree, which requires a jbd2 transaction, and a transaction cannot be started from bio-completion context. Buffered writeback into unwritten extents therefore cannot finish the conversion where the I/O finishes: ext4_end_bio() hands the completed ext4_io_end_t to ext4_put_io_end_defer(), which queues it on the inode's i_rsv_conversion_list and wakes the filesystem's rsv_conversion_wq workqueue, where ext4_end_io_rsv_work() runs in process context and can open a handle. Direct I/O has no such problem, because iomap calls ext4_dio_write_end_io() in a context where a transaction is allowed, and it converts the range immediately. The consequence of the deferral is that a file's on-disk extent map can lag its on-disk data by the length of a workqueue delay, which is why operations that must see an accurate map—truncate and fallocate() among them—call inode_dio_wait() and flush the pending conversions before they begin.

Extents also represent a file's holes naturally, which indirect blocks did only awkwardly. A sparse file—one with unwritten gaps, common for database files and memory dumps—simply has no extent covering the hole's logical range; a read there returns zeros and consumes no disk space, and the file's allocated block count reflects only the extents that exist. Because an extent names an explicit logical start (ee_block), a gap between one extent's end and the next extent's start is the hole, needing no marker of its own. This makes sparse files cheap to represent and fast to traverse (the SEEK_HOLE and SEEK_DATA operations of lseek() walk the extent tree directly, through the iomap reporting loop of Chapter 15), a neat side benefit of a map built around ranges rather than per-block pointers.

The same range-oriented map is what lets fallocate() offer operations that would be impractical on a per-block pointer map. Beyond plain preallocation, ext4 implements FALLOC_FL_PUNCH_HOLE (remove a range, freeing its blocks and leaving a gap), FALLOC_FL_ZERO_RANGE (make a range read as zeros while keeping the space allocated, which is preallocation's unwritten trick applied to a range that already had data), and the two shifting operations FALLOC_FL_COLLAPSE_RANGE and FALLOC_FL_INSERT_RANGE, which remove or insert a range and slide the rest of the file over it without moving any data. That last pair is only feasible because the logical block numbers live in the extent records: ext4_ext_shift_extents() rewrites the ee_block field of the affected extents and the index entries above them, so collapsing a gigabyte out of the middle of a file is a metadata edit proportional to the number of extents rather than a copy proportional to the file. An indirect map, where a block's logical position is its position in the pointer array, could not do this at all.

One recent addition inverts the unwritten-extent bargain. FALLOC_FL_WRITE_ZEROES asks for a range that is allocated and reads as zeros, like ZERO_RANGE, but backed by written extents: ext4 allocates the blocks and issues a write-zeroes command to the device, so nothing is left to convert later. It is accepted only when the block device advertises an unmapping write-zeroes operation (bdev_write_zeroes_unmap_sectors()), because the point is that the hardware can record the range as zeroed without actually transferring zeros. The motivation is latency predictability rather than allocation speed: an unwritten extent makes the first write to each range more expensive, since it must split and convert, which is exactly the cost a database or a virtual-machine image wanted to avoid by preallocating in the first place. Where the device can do the zeroing, paying once at fallocate() time buys a file with no conversion work anywhere in its future.

In-Memory Structures

The on-disk structures are compact and byte-exact; the in-memory ones are richer, holding cached copies, computed state, and links into the VFS. This section describes ext4's in-core superblock and inode and the extent-status cache that is new to ext4; the method tables through which the VFS calls into it get a section of their own next.

The distinction between the two worlds matters because they have different lifetimes and different owners. The on-disk structures belong to the volume: they persist across mounts, are shared by every kernel that has ever touched the filesystem, and must never change format without a feature flag to warn older kernels. The in-memory structures belong to this mount on this boot: they are built up when the volume is mounted or an inode is first accessed, they hold pointers and locks and per-CPU state that make no sense on disk, and they are torn down at unmount. A large part of what the ext4 driver does is keep the two consistent—reading disk structures into memory on demand, caching them, and writing dirty in-memory state back through the journal so a crash leaves the on-disk copy recoverable.

The In-Core Superblock

When a volume is mounted, ext4 attaches a struct ext4_sb_info (fs/ext4/ext4.h) to the VFS super_block through its s_fs_info pointer, reachable with the EXT4_SB() accessor. This in-core superblock is the mount's control block. It caches a pointer to the on-disk superblock buffer (s_es), the array of group descriptors (s_group_desc), the volume geometry (groups count, blocks and inodes per group, inode size), the parsed mount options, and—crucially for the later sections—a pointer to the journal (s_journal), the multi-block allocator's state (the s_mb_* fields), and the s_flex_groups array for flex_bg. It also holds the free-space counters, and here ext4 makes a scalability change worth noting: the free block, free inode, and directory counts are per-CPU counters (s_freeclusters_counter, s_freeinodes_counter, s_dirs_counter) rather than single fields, so that allocations on many CPUs do not contend on one global count—an approximate total is kept per CPU and reconciled only when an exact value is needed, such as for statfs.

Several other members of ext4_sb_info reveal how a mount is actually wired together. The raw superblock is not free-floating memory: it lives in a buffer head, s_sbh, that pins the disk block holding the superblock in the page cache, and s_es simply points into that buffer—so marking s_sbh dirty and letting it be written back is how a superblock change reaches disk. The group descriptors are reached through s_group_desc, an array of buffer-head pointers that is RCU-protected (struct buffer_head * __rcu *) so that online resizing can grow the descriptor table while readers walk it without taking a lock. The mount also owns the machinery for the features the rest of this chapter covers: the orphan list (s_orphan, the in-memory head of the on-disk list of unlinked-but-open inodes that recovery must clean up), the multi-block allocator's buddy caches and tunables (the many s_mb_* fields), the per-CPU locality groups used for small-file allocation (s_locality_groups), and the cluster geometry (s_cluster_ratio and s_cluster_bits) that bigalloc filesystems use to allocate in multi-block clusters. Finally, each mount registers its own extent-status shrinker (s_es_shrinker) so that the caches described below can be reclaimed under memory pressure—an instance of exactly the shrinker mechanism Chapter 17 described.

A mount is also a visible object, and the in-core superblock is what makes it one. ext4_sb_info carries a s_kobj that publishes the mount at /sys/fs/ext4/<device>/, where most of the tunables named in this chapter appear as plain files—extent_max_zeroout_kb from §3.3 among them—along with counters the driver keeps for its own diagnosis. Beside it, s_proc creates /proc/fs/ext4/<device>/ holding options, which prints the mount options actually in effect rather than the ones requested, and es_shrinker_info, which reports how the extent-status caches of §4.3 are behaving. The point is that none of this state exists on disk and none of it is reconstructible after unmount, so exposing it is the only way to observe it; a filesystem this heavily tuned would otherwise be opaque. Two further fields are worth separating because their names invite confusion: s_mount_state is the cached copy of the on-disk s_state from §2.1, while s_mount_flags is purely in-memory—it records conditions such as the filesystem having been aborted after an error, which must not survive the unmount that follows.

The In-Core Inode

Similarly, each active inode is represented by a struct ext4_inode_info that embeds the VFS struct inode (as its vfs_inode member) and surrounds it with ext4-specific state, reachable with EXT4_I(). Embedding rather than pointing means one allocation holds both the generic and the filesystem-specific inode, and EXT4_I() is just pointer arithmetic. The ext4 part caches an in-core copy of the i_block array (i_data), the inode flags, the true on-disk size (i_disksize, which can differ from the VFS size during delayed allocation), the jbd2 inode used for ordered-mode data (jinode), the count of blocks reserved but not yet allocated (i_reserved_data_blocks), the inline-data offset and size, and the transaction ids needed to implement fsync correctly (i_sync_tid, i_datasync_tid). Several of these fields—the extent-status tree, the reserved-block count, the jbd2 inode—did not exist in ext2 and exist to support features covered below. Figure 18-6 shows how the in-memory and on-disk structures relate.

Because the VFS inode is embedded rather than pointed at, ext4 supplies the allocation and freeing hooks the VFS uses for it: ext4_alloc_inode carves a combined ext4_inode_info out of a dedicated slab cache and returns the address of its vfs_inode member, and the matching destructor frees the whole object once the VFS is done. The consequence is that the ext4 state lives exactly as long as the VFS keeps the inode cached, which is what lets the fields below be treated as always present. The surrounding ext4 fields also carry the per-inode synchronization that the on-disk inode has no room for. The most important is i_data_sem, a read/write semaphore that serializes changes to the block map—it is held for writing while ext4_map_blocks allocates or splits extents and for reading while a lookup walks them, so a truncate cannot race a page fault that is mapping the same file. A separate xattr_sem guards the in-inode extended-attribute area, and i_es_lock protects the extent-status tree discussed next.

Two more links deserve mention because they connect the inode to features elsewhere in the chapter. When a file is unlinked while still open, or is mid-truncate, its inode is threaded onto the mount's orphan list through i_orphan (or, with the newer orphan-file feature, recorded by i_orphan_idx), so that journal recovery can finish the deletion if the system crashes before the last close. And each inode carries its own preallocation state—i_prealloc_node, a red-black tree of block ranges reserved for this file, protected by i_prealloc_lock—so that the multi-block allocator can hand a growing file a contiguous run and keep the rest of that run in reserve for the file's next write. On an encrypted filesystem the same structure also anchors the per-inode key state through i_crypt_info. None of these fields exist on disk; they are pure in-memory bookkeeping that the ext4 driver rebuilds each time an inode is brought into core.

One distinction inside the in-core inode is worth drawing explicitly, because two different things are both called flags. The i_flags copied from the on-disk inode are the file's persistent properties—immutable, append-only, extent-mapped, encrypted, casefolded—and they survive unmount because they are written back. Separately, i_state_flags holds the EXT4_STATE_* bits, which describe what is true of this inode right now in this kernel and are discarded when the inode leaves the cache. They are a running commentary on the driver's own work: EXT4_STATE_NEW marks an inode whose disk image is not yet initialized, EXT4_STATE_XATTR that the inode has in-inode extended attributes, EXT4_STATE_NO_EXPAND that the extra area of §2.3 could not be grown, EXT4_STATE_EXT_PRECACHED that the extent-tree sweep of §3.2 has already run, EXT4_STATE_ORPHAN_FILE that this inode is recorded in the orphan file rather than the orphan list, and EXT4_STATE_VERITY_IN_PROGRESS that an fs-verity Merkle tree is being built and the file's apparent size is therefore temporarily untrustworthy. Reading the list is a good way to see which operations in ext4 are multi-step enough to need a marker saying they are half done.

The inode also carries the per-file half of fast commit, the feature §8.1 describes. An inode modified since the last commit is threaded onto the journal's list through i_fc_list, and while it sits there ext4 accumulates the logical block range it has touched in i_fc_lblk_start and i_fc_lblk_len, coalescing separate writes into one span rather than recording each. That range is what a fast commit replays, which is why the tracking has to live in the inode: the journal records that this inode changed, and the inode remembers where. Because a commit must see a consistent range, the inode has an i_fc_wait queue and an EXT4_STATE_FC_COMMITTING bit so that a writer arriving mid-commit waits rather than extending a range that is already being written out.

The Extent Status Tree

A structure with no on-disk counterpart, and one of ext4's important performance additions, is the extent status tree (fs/ext4/extents_status.c). Walking the on-disk extent tree requires reading extent blocks from disk, which is wasteful if the same mappings are needed repeatedly. So each inode keeps an in-memory red-black tree (i_es_tree) of struct extent_status records, each caching the mapping of a logical-block range: where it lives physically and its status—written, unwritten, delayed, or hole. ext4_map_blocks() consults this cache first (ext4_es_lookup_extent) and only falls back to the on-disk tree on a miss, so repeated access to a file maps its blocks from memory.

The status tree does more than cache, though: it is what makes delayed allocation possible. A block that has been written into the page cache but not yet given a physical location is recorded in the status tree as a delayed extent—a mapping that exists logically but has no block behind it yet. This lets the kernel answer "is this block mapped?" for a not-yet-allocated block without inventing a fake physical address, and lets it account for the space reserved against the eventual allocation. The hole status similarly caches the knowledge that a range is unmapped, so a sparse file's holes are not re-looked-up on the disk each time. This in-memory tree, cross-referenced with the reserved-block count in the inode, is the machinery behind the delayed-allocation behavior described in §6.

Two smaller pieces complete the structure. The tree is fronted by a one-entry cache: struct ext4_es_tree holds not just the red-black root but a cache_es pointer to the most recently accessed record, checked before the tree is searched at all. Sequential access to a file—the overwhelmingly common pattern—therefore usually hits that single pointer, and the red-black walk is reserved for the cases that actually jump around. Separately, an inode on a bigalloc volume carries an i_pending_tree, a second red-black tree holding nothing but cluster numbers. It exists because bigalloc reserves space in clusters while delayed allocation accounts for it in blocks: when two blocks in the same cluster are both dirtied, only one cluster needs to be reserved, and the pending tree is how ext4 remembers which clusters already have a reservation outstanding so it does not charge the file twice for the same space.

The record itself is deliberately tiny. A struct extent_status holds only its red-black node, the first logical block it covers (es_lblk), the length (es_len), and the physical block (es_pblk)—there is no separate status field. Instead the four states are packed into the top five bits of es_pblk (the ES_TYPE_MASK bits, plus one more discussed below), which is safe because physical block numbers are only 48 bits wide while es_pblk is 64, leaving 59 bits for the block number and eleven to spare. Packing the status into the pointer keeps each cached mapping small, which matters because a busy inode can accumulate thousands of them. That density is also why the tree needs to be reclaimable: the entries are a cache, and under memory pressure the kernel must be able to drop the ones it can rebuild. This is where the mount's extent-status shrinker comes in. Entries that merely cache a written or unwritten mapping can be re-read from the on-disk extent tree, so the shrinker (via __es_shrink, walking a per-mount LRU of inodes with reclaimable entries) is free to evict them when the reclaim path of Chapter 17 asks it to, and so is it for hole entries, which merely record the absence of an extent and can be rediscovered the same way. Only delayed entries are pinned. ext4_es_must_keep() returns true for exactly that one case, because a delayed entry is not a cache at all: it is the sole record that a block has been written into the page cache and reserved but not yet allocated, and dropping it would lose the reservation. The distinction is visible in the accounting—every entry counts toward the inode's i_es_all_nr, but only reclaimable ones count toward i_es_shk_nr, and an inode joins the mount's shrinker list only when that second count becomes nonzero.

The shrinker's policy is more careful than a simple sweep, and it borrows two ideas from page reclaim. The fifth bit packed into es_pblk is not a state at all but EXTENT_STATUS_REFERENCED, set when an entry is used and treated by es_do_reclaim_extents() as a second chance: an entry found with the bit set has it cleared and is skipped, so only an entry that survives a pass without being touched again is evicted. That is the clock algorithm of Chapter 17, applied to a filesystem cache. The second idea is that the scan resumes rather than restarts: each inode remembers i_es_shrink_lblk, the logical block where the last scan stopped, so successive shrink passes walk forward through a large tree instead of repeatedly punishing its beginning. The mount keeps the whole apparatus in s_es_list, a list of inodes with reclaimable entries, and records hit and miss counts in per-CPU counters whose totals appear in the es_shrinker_info file of §4.1.

Ext4 Methods

The VFS never calls the filesystem directly: it calls through tables of function pointers—super_operations, inode_operations, file_operations, and address_space_operations—that each filesystem fills in with its own implementations. Ext4 installs the superblock table once at mount time and chooses the inode, file, and address-space tables per object when ext4_iget reads an inode from disk, so a single driver can present the right behavior for a directory, a regular file, a device node, or a symlink. The subsections below walk the three classic families—super, inode, and file operations—brought up to date for ext4; Table 18-7 lists the main tables and representative methods.

The count of tables is itself informative. Ext2 needed few; ext4 has accumulated enough that choosing among them is a real step in bringing an inode into core. There are three inode_operations tables for the ordinary file types and three more for the three kinds of symlink, two file_operations tables—because a directory is opened and read like a file but does almost nothing a file does—and four address_space_operations tables reflecting the four ways ext4 can move data between a file and the disk. Each of these forks exists because some feature changed behavior enough that a conditional inside a shared method would have been worse than a separate table, and the set is a fair map of where ext4 has grown since Linux 2.6.

Superblock Operations

The super_operations table, ext4_sops (fs/ext4/super.c), holds the methods the VFS invokes on the volume as a whole and on inode lifecycle. The inode-lifecycle hooks are the busiest: ext4_alloc_inode carves a combined ext4_inode_info from the ext4 slab cache (the embedding described in §4.2) and ext4_free_in_core_inode / ext4_destroy_inode return it, while ext4_write_inode and ext4_dirty_inode copy the in-core inode's fields back into its on-disk ext4_inode under a journal handle so the update is recoverable. ext4_evict_inode runs when the last reference to an inode goes away: if the inode was unlinked it truncates the file, frees its blocks and the inode itself, and removes it from the orphan list; otherwise it simply writes back any dirty state.

The volume-wide methods are fewer but important. ext4_statfs answers df by reading the per-CPU free-block and free-inode counters of §4.1 rather than rescanning bitmaps. ext4_sync_fs forces the current journal transaction to commit, which is how sync reaches ext4, and ext4_freeze / ext4_unfreeze quiesce the filesystem—flushing and then barring new transactions—so a consistent snapshot can be taken of the block device underneath. ext4_put_super tears the mount down at unmount, and ext4_show_options renders the active mount options back into /proc/mounts. When built with quota support the table also routes the kernel's quota reads and writes through ext4_quota_read and ext4_quota_write so quota files are updated inside the journal like any other metadata.

Two of the lifecycle hooks are easy to misread as redundant, and the distinction is a useful one. destroy_inode and free_inode are both teardown methods, but ext4_destroy_inode runs immediately when the VFS is finished with the inode and is where ext4 does its checking—it complains loudly if the inode is being freed while still carrying dirty state or an orphan-list membership—whereas ext4_free_in_core_inode returns the memory to the slab cache and is deferred by RCU. The split exists because lockless readers may still be walking the inode by pointer when it is evicted; the memory cannot be reused until an RCU grace period has passed, but the consistency checks want to run at eviction time while the context is still meaningful. A third hook, ext4_drop_inode, is consulted earlier still: it decides whether an inode with no remaining links should be evicted at once or kept in cache, which is how an unlinked but still open file stays alive until its last descriptor closes.

Inode Operations

Ext4 supplies several inode_operations tables and selects one per inode by type, because the VFS dispatches namespace and attribute operations on the inode. In ext4_iget the driver switches on the file mode: a regular file gets ext4_file_inode_operations, a directory ext4_dir_inode_operations, and a device, FIFO, or socket the minimal ext4_special_inode_operations. Symlinks fan out three ways—an encrypted symlink, a fast symlink whose target is stored inline in the inode's i_block area, and a slow symlink whose target lives in a data block each get their own table, because reading the link differs in each case. This per-type selection is why one on-disk inode format can back files, directories, and every special type: the mode field chooses which set of methods the VFS will call.

The richest table is ext4_dir_inode_operations, which carries the namespace methods that create and destroy names in a directory: ext4_create, ext4_lookup, ext4_link, ext4_unlink, ext4_symlink, ext4_mkdir, ext4_rmdir, ext4_mknod, ext4_tmpfile, and ext4_rename2. Each of these mutates several metadata blocks at once—a rename may touch two directory blocks, two inodes, and the group descriptors—so every one runs inside a single jbd2 handle (§7.3), which is what makes a rename or an unlink atomic across a crash. Alongside the namespace methods sit the attribute methods that directories share with files: ext4_setattr and ext4_getattr for stat/chmod/truncate, ext4_listxattr for extended attributes, ext4_get_acl/ext4_set_acl for POSIX ACLs, ext4_fiemap for reporting a file's extent map to userspace, and the fileattr hooks that back chattr/lsattr. The ext4_file_inode_operations table used for regular files holds exactly this attribute set and none of the namespace methods, because a regular file has no directory entries of its own.

File and Address-Space Operations

The file_operations table, ext4_file_operations (fs/ext4/file.c), governs what an open file descriptor can do. ext4_file_read_iter and ext4_file_write_iter carry the read and write paths, ext4_sync_file implements fsync and fdatasync by committing the file's data and metadata, and ext4_llseek implements SEEK_HOLE and SEEK_DATA so a program can find the allocated and sparse regions of a file. The table also routes fallocate to ext4_fallocate (which preallocates unwritten extents without writing data), ioctl to ext4_ioctl (the interface behind chattr, online defrag, and the fast-commit and encryption controls), and open and release to hooks that manage per-file state.

A directory is opened and read through the same system calls as a file, so it too needs a file_operations table, and ext4_dir_operations (fs/ext4/dir.c) is a good illustration of how little the two have in common. Its read method is generic_read_dir, which does nothing but return EISDIR; the real work is iterate_shared, pointing at ext4_readdir. The shared in that name matters—several threads may read one directory concurrently, holding the inode only for reading—which is why the per-open state that readdir needs lives in the file's private_data rather than in the inode.

That per-open state exists because HTree directories break an assumption readdir was built on. The telldir and seekdir interface requires a single scalar position that can be saved and returned to later, and for a linear directory the byte offset of an entry serves perfectly. An indexed directory has no such order: its entries are reachable by hash, and a leaf split can move an entry to a different block at any time, so a byte offset saved before the split names the wrong entry after it. Ext4's answer is to make the position be the hash. For an indexed directory, f_pos holds the major and minor hash of the entry rather than an offset, and ext4_dir_llseek reports an artificial end-of-file (ext4_get_htree_eof()) at the top of the hash space rather than at the directory's size, since the hash and the file length have nothing to do with one another.

Reading then proceeds a hash range at a time. ext4_dx_readdir fills a per-file red-black tree of struct fname records from one span of the hash space, sorted so that entries come back in a stable order, and emits them before fetching the next span. Names whose hashes collide are chained together off a single tree node, which is why the fname record has a next pointer at all. The whole cache is tagged with a cookie taken from the directory's version counter, so that if the directory is modified between two getdents calls the stale tree is discarded and rebuilt rather than returning entries that no longer exist. The result satisfies POSIX—a readdir that runs to completion sees every entry that was present throughout—without requiring the directory to have a stable physical order, which is the price of indexing it.

The most interesting tables for ext4's modern behavior are the address_space_operations, and there are four of them. ext4_aops is the classic path; ext4_da_aops differs only in its write_begin/write_end methods, which reserve delayed blocks instead of allocating them, and which one an inode uses determines whether its writes allocate immediately or defer to writeback—the subject of §6.3. ext4_journalled_aops serves data=journal inodes and differs more widely, replacing write_end, dirty_folio, and invalidate_folio because every data block must be attached to a transaction, and using buffer_migrate_folio_norefs so that a folio pinned by the journal is not migrated out from under it. ext4_dax_aops is the outlier: it has no read_folio, no readahead, and no write_begin at all, because DAX bypasses the page cache entirely and the methods that manage cached pages have nothing to manage.

ext4_set_aops() chooses among them in a strict priority that encodes which features override which. Journaled data wins outright—an inode in data=journal mode gets ext4_journalled_aops and the function returns before DAX or delayed allocation is even considered, because putting data through the log is incompatible with both. DAX is tested next, then the delalloc mount option, and ext4_aops is what remains. Reading the function is the quickest way to understand which of ext4's data paths are mutually exclusive and in what order they win. Reads and direct I/O, meanwhile, increasingly go through the iomap framework of Chapter 16 rather than the older buffer-head path: ext4_file_read_iter uses iomap_dio_rw with ext4_iomap_ops for direct I/O and the DAX path for persistent memory, and llseek's SEEK_HOLE/SEEK_DATA uses iomap's reporting ops. This is ext4 adopting the generic, extent-friendly I/O interface the previous chapters built, in place of the block-at-a-time mechanisms of Linux 2.6.

The same modernization reaches the page cache itself. Ext4 in Linux 7.0.12 asks for large folios, calling mapping_set_folio_order_range on each inode so that the cache allocates file pages in multi-page units instead of one page at a time, which suits a filesystem whose extents already describe long contiguous runs. The minimum order comes from the block size—get_order(blocksize)—which is how ext4 supports a volume whose blocks are larger than the machine's pages at all: a single block must never be split across two folios. The maximum is capped at 2048 blocks, and the reason is a nice illustration of how the layers constrain each other, because the cap exists to bound the journal credits a single writeback of one folio might have to reserve. For the same reason a volume mounted data=journal clamps the maximum down to the minimum and gets no large folios at all: when every data block passes through the log, a large folio's worth of blocks is more than one transaction should be asked to promise.

The file table also declares capabilities rather than only supplying functions, which is a newer style than the 2.6-era tables. Its fop_flags field advertises that ext4 supports asynchronous buffered readahead, that a memory-mapped write can be synced, that uncached buffered I/O (RWF_DONTCACHE) works, and—most consequentially—FOP_DIO_PARALLEL_WRITE, meaning direct-I/O writes that do not extend the file may proceed with the inode held only for reading, so several writers can be in flight at once. The generic layer consults these flags before it calls ext4 at all, which lets the VFS take fast paths without each filesystem having to reimplement them.

The newest capability in this area is atomic writes, exposed to applications as the RWF_ATOMIC flag on pwritev2. The guarantee is that the write either lands entirely or not at all, even across a power loss—which is precisely what a database needs to stop doing its own double-write journaling of pages. Ext4 does not implement this in software; it passes the guarantee through from hardware that offers it, and the conditions are correspondingly narrow. ext4_atomic_write_init() gives up immediately unless the block device advertises atomic writes and the volume has the extents feature, and it then computes a permitted size range: s_awu_min is the larger of the filesystem block size and the device's minimum unit, while s_awu_max is the smaller of the device's maximum and the cluster size—the block size on an ordinary volume, the bigalloc cluster on one using it. The upper bound is a filesystem constraint rather than a hardware one: ext4 can only promise atomicity for a range it knows the allocator will keep in one piece. Support is per file and direct-I/O only; ext4_file_open sets FMODE_CAN_ATOMIC_WRITE on a regular, extent-mapped file when the mount found hardware support, and the mount announces the negotiated range in the kernel log, still labelled experimental in 7.0.12.

Allocating Space

Disk-space management—how the filesystem finds free blocks and inodes and assigns them to files—is the area ext4 reworked more than almost any other, replacing ext2's one-block-at-a-time allocation with a multi-block allocator, adding delayed allocation, and mapping through extents. This section covers how a logical block becomes a physical one, how the allocator chooses blocks, how delayed allocation improves layout, and how inodes are allocated.

Mapping Logical to Physical Blocks

Every access to a file's data begins by turning a logical block number (an offset within the file) into a physical one (a location on disk). The central function is ext4_map_blocks() (fs/ext4/inode.c), and it embodies the ext2-to-ext4 duality. It first checks the in-memory extent status tree; on a miss, it dispatches by the inode's format—an inode with EXTENTS_FL goes to ext4_ext_map_blocks() to walk the extent tree, while an old-format inode goes to ext4_ind_map_blocks() to walk the indirect blocks. The same function serves both reads (just map) and writes (map, creating blocks if needed): a flags argument with EXT4_GET_BLOCKS_CREATE tells it whether a missing mapping should trigger allocation. This single entry point, hiding the extent/indirect distinction behind one interface, is why the rest of ext4 rarely needs to care which layout a file uses.

Two further details make ext4_map_blocks the hinge of the whole read/write path. First, its return value is the number of blocks it actually mapped, which may be fewer than requested—a single call maps one contiguous run, and a caller that wants more loops, advancing by the returned length. Second, whatever it learns it writes back into the extent status tree before returning, so the expensive on-disk walk happens once and every later access to those blocks is answered from memory. The flags argument carries more than just CREATE: variants tell the allocator to convert an unwritten extent to written after the data lands (EXT4_GET_BLOCKS_CONVERT), to allocate but leave the extent unwritten for fallocate (EXT4_GET_BLOCKS_UNWRIT_EXT), or to only query without allocating. This one function therefore serves buffered reads and writes, direct I/O through iomap, fallocate, and the writeback path of delayed allocation—each passing the flags that describe what it needs. Figure 18-7 traces this dispatch.

The locking follows from that split between querying and creating, and it is a pattern worth recognizing because it recurs throughout the kernel. A lookup that only needs to read the map takes i_data_sem for reading, so any number of threads can map blocks of the same file at once. An allocating call begins the same way—it first tries the read-only query, on the assumption that the blocks may already exist—and only if that fails does it drop the read lock, take the semaphore for writing, and re-run the lookup before allocating. The second lookup is not redundant: between dropping the read lock and taking the write lock another thread may have allocated exactly the blocks this one wanted, and without the recheck both would allocate. Ext4 also validates every mapping it returns through check_block_validity(), which applies the same test that refused a bad extent in §3.2, so a corrupt map is caught at the point of use rather than followed into the metadata.

The Multi-Block Allocator

When a write does need new blocks, ext4 calls its multi-block allocator (mballoc, fs/ext4/mballoc.c), a substantial improvement over ext2's scheme of scanning a bitmap for one free block at a time. Mballoc is built to allocate runs of contiguous blocks at once, matching the extent-based layout, and to keep a file's blocks together. It maintains, per block group, an in-memory buddy bitmap—derived from the on-disk block bitmap—that records free space grouped by power-of-two size, so the allocator can quickly find a free run of the size it wants rather than scanning bit by bit. The entry point ext4_mb_new_blocks() takes an allocation request describing the desired length and a goal block (usually near the file's existing blocks), and ext4_mb_regular_allocator() searches for a suitable run.

Before the search begins, mballoc normalizes the request (ext4_mb_normalize_request): rather than ask for exactly the number of blocks the write needs, it rounds the goal length up to a predictable bucket sized from the file. The table is a fixed ladder—16, 32, 64, 128, 256, and 512 KiB, then 1 MiB—so that a file which keeps growing is handed room to extend into and repeated small writes coalesce into one run. Above a megabyte the rule changes in a way worth noticing: the request is rounded not just in size but in position, to a 2, 4, or 8 MiB window aligned on its own size within the file's logical space. Aligning the windows means that two files growing side by side ask for disjoint windows rather than overlapping ones, so their allocations interleave far less than they would if each simply asked for "8 MiB starting wherever I am now". The request also records whether it should be served from a per-inode preallocation or, for small streaming writes below MB_DEFAULT_STREAM_THRESHOLD (16 blocks), from the shared locality group.

The search is staged by increasingly relaxed criteria so the common, cheap cases are tried first. There are five, from CR_POWER2_ALIGNED—which looks for a large, power-of-two-aligned free run ideal for big files—through CR_GOAL_LEN_FAST and CR_BEST_AVAIL_LEN, which accept progressively less ideal runs near the goal, then CR_GOAL_LEN_SLOW, which scans more thoroughly, and finally CR_ANY_FREE, the last resort that takes whatever is free. The costly step in the older allocator was finding a group likely to satisfy the current criterion, and here the optional mb_optimize_scan mode helps: mballoc keeps two auxiliary indexes—one ordering groups by their largest free run (s_mb_largest_free_orders) and one by average fragment size (s_mb_avg_fragment_size)—so it can jump straight to a promising group instead of scanning every group linearly. It can also prefetch a run of block bitmaps ahead of the search (s_mb_prefetch) so the disk reads overlap.

Two forms of preallocation keep files contiguous even when they grow in small steps. A per-inode preallocation reserves a window of blocks around a growing file so its next writes extend it contiguously rather than landing wherever there is room; the reserved window lives on the inode's i_prealloc_node tree until it is used or discarded. A per-locality-group preallocation, shared by small files written together, packs them into a common area (default 512 blocks, s_mb_group_prealloc) so that many tiny files do not scatter across the disk. When a file is closed or space runs low, its unused preallocation is handed back so the reservation does not leak free space. Figure 18-8 sketches the buddy and preallocation scheme.

An allocator also has to answer the question of whether there is room, and ext4's answer has more structure than a single comparison. ext4_has_free_clusters() in fs/ext4/balloc.c subtracts three different reserves before it will say yes to an ordinary caller: the classic root reserve from the superblock's s_r_blocks_count, the clusters already promised to delayed allocation in s_dirtyclusters_counter, and a pool called s_resv_clusters. It also reads the per-CPU counters of §4.1 approximately first and only pays for an exact summation when the approximate answer lands near the watermark—the cheap check is wrong only in the direction of being conservative. A process running as the reserve owner or holding CAP_SYS_RESOURCE may spend the root reserve, which is the familiar behavior that keeps a full disk usable by an administrator, but even root cannot touch s_resv_clusters. Only a caller that passes EXT4_MB_USE_RESERVED may.

That last pool exists for a reason that inverts the usual intuition, and it is one of the more elegant details in the allocator: on an extent-based filesystem, freeing space can require allocating it. Punching a hole in the middle of an extent splits it in two, and if the leaf node is full the split needs a new extent-tree block; converting an unwritten extent as delayed allocation resolves can do the same. A filesystem that had genuinely run out of blocks would then be unable to complete the very operations that would give blocks back—wedged full. So ext4_set_resv_clusters() holds back 2 percent of the volume or 4,096 clusters, whichever is smaller, purely for these metadata allocations. The cap matters: the reserve is sized for a handful of tree blocks at a time, not proportionally to the disk, because the operations it protects need one or two blocks each. And it is set up only on volumes with the extents feature, since an indirect-mapped filesystem computes its space needs exactly and never has to split anything to free it.

One more rule shapes what an allocation failure means. Blocks released by a delete or a truncate do not become available the moment the file goes away; they are recorded as pending and only returned to the buddy bitmaps when the transaction that freed them commits, through the journal's commit callback into ext4_process_freed_data(). Until then they are counted in s_mb_free_pending but cannot be handed out, because a crash before the commit must leave them still belonging to the old file. The consequence is that ENOSPC from ext4 is often transient, and the allocator treats it that way: ext4_should_retry_alloc() checks whether a commit might be about to release something, forces one with jbd2_journal_force_commit_nested(), and tells the caller to try again, up to three times. If nothing is pending it instead flushes the outstanding discard work, since a volume mounted discard may be holding blocks that are queued for trimming. Only after those avenues are exhausted does the error reach the application.

Delayed Allocation

Ext4's other major allocation change is delayed allocation, or allocate-on-flush. When a program writes to a file through the buffered path, ext2 would allocate the data block immediately, during the write() call. Ext4 instead defers the allocation: ext4_da_write_begin() merely copies the data into the page cache, marks the range dirty, records a delayed extent in the status tree, and reserves space against the free count with ext4_da_reserve_space()—but assigns no physical block. The actual allocation happens later, at writeback time, when ext4_writepages() maps a whole run of dirty pages to physical blocks at once through mpage_map_and_submit_extent().

Deferring pays off two ways. Because the allocator sees the file's dirty pages together rather than one at a time, it can place them as one large contiguous extent instead of scattered single blocks—exactly the layout extents represent efficiently, produced naturally by allocating at flush. And because many short-lived files are written and deleted before writeback ever runs, their blocks are never allocated at all: a temporary file that lives for a fraction of a second costs no allocation work and touches no bitmap. The reserved-block accounting (the i_reserved_data_blocks count and the delayed extents in the status tree) is what lets the filesystem promise the space without committing a location, so statfs still reports it as used and a later allocation cannot fail unexpectedly. Reserving without allocating raises a hazard the design has to close: the kernel could promise space it cannot later deliver, producing an ENOSPC error at writeback where no application is prepared for it. Ext4 avoids this by reserving clusters against a global per-CPU count (s_dirtyclusters_counter) at write time and refusing the reservation up front if the free space is not there—so a buffered write that would overcommit the disk fails during write(), not silently at flush. The reservation is deliberately pessimistic, counting the metadata blocks (extent-tree blocks) the eventual mapping might need as well as the data, and it is released and reconciled against the true free count once the real allocation runs. This accounting, together with the writeback dirty-page throttling of Chapter 15, is what lets delayed allocation defer work safely rather than merely optimistically.

Deciding how much to reserve is less obvious than it sounds, and ext4_insert_delayed_blocks() is where the subtlety lives. On an ordinary filesystem the answer is the block count, but under bigalloc the unit of allocation is a cluster of many blocks, and a write may fall into a cluster that some other extent of the same file already owns or has already reserved. Charging for it again would make the filesystem report itself full long before it was. So ext4 first asks ext4_clu_alloc_state() what the cluster at each end of the new range is already doing: if it is shared with a delayed, written, or unwritten extent, that cluster is not charged. A cluster shared with a written or unwritten extent instead gets an entry in the i_pending_tree of §4.3, which records that the file has a claim on part of a cluster it did not pay for—the bookkeeping that lets the space be released correctly when the file is eventually truncated. And because written and unwritten status entries are exactly the ones the shrinker may have evicted, a miss in the status tree is not taken as an answer: the code falls through to the on-disk extent tree rather than over-reserving on the strength of a cache miss.

Allocating Inodes

Allocating an inode for a new file is handled by ext4_new_inode() (fs/ext4/ialloc.c), and the interesting decision is which block group to place it in, because that choice shapes the disk's long-term locality. Ext4 uses two strategies depending on what is being created. For a new directory, it uses the Orlov allocator (find_group_orlov), which deliberately spreads top-level directories across many block groups with room to grow, on the reasoning that a directory's files will later want space near it—scattering directories leaves each one contiguous expansion room. For a new file, it uses find_group_other, which instead tries to place the inode in the same group as its parent directory, keeping a directory and its files together so listing and reading them stays local. Once a group is chosen, the inode bitmap is scanned for a free inode, honoring the uninitialized-group optimization so an untouched group need not have its bitmap read.

Both strategies are flex_bg-aware: because a flex group clusters several block groups' bitmaps and inode tables together, the allocator first reduces the target to a flex group (shifting the parent group right by s_log_groups_per_flex) so a directory and its inode table land in the same tightly packed region. If the chosen group has no free inode, find_group_other does not fall back to a dumb linear scan of every following group; it probes with a quadratic hash step that spreads the retries out, converging on a free group without clustering new inodes right behind the full one. The Orlov allocator for directories adds a further test: it only spreads a new top-level directory into a group whose free-inode and free-block counts are above the filesystem average (and whose directory "debt" is not too high), so directories are scattered for growth room but not into groups that are already crowded. Once an inode number is claimed, if its group was never initialized the inode table is zeroed lazily, so formatting a huge filesystem does not require writing every inode table up front.

Two details of the directory case are worth drawing out. The first is what counts as "top level." Ext4 spreads a new directory only when its parent is the filesystem root or carries the EXT4_INODE_TOPDIR flag, which an administrator can set with chattr +T. That flag is the user-visible control over this policy: marking a directory as a top-level one tells ext4 to treat each of its children as the start of a new region rather than packing them beside their parent, which is what one wants for a directory holding many independent subtrees—home directories, or per-project checkouts—and not what one wants inside a single source tree.

The second is that the spreading is not random, or not quite. When a name is available, Orlov picks the starting group by hashing the new directory's name and taking the result modulo the group count, falling back to a random group only when no name was supplied. Hashing makes the choice deterministic and evenly distributed at the same time: creating the same directory on two identical filesystems places it in the same group, so layout is reproducible, while unrelated names still scatter. From that starting point the allocator scans all groups and keeps the candidate holding the fewest directories among those whose free inodes and free clusters both exceed the filesystem average—so it is looking for the least crowded above-average group, not merely the first acceptable one. If no group passes every test, it falls back to a simpler search for a group with more free inodes than average, starting at the parent's.

Journaling with JBD2

Journaling, introduced with ext3, is where the largest modern change lies, so it gets a full section. Ext4 journals through jbd2 (Journaling Block Device 2, fs/jbd2/), a general-purpose journaling service that ext4 drives. This section covers why journaling exists, the three modes ext4 offers, the transaction and handle abstractions, how a transaction commits and is later checkpointed, and how recovery replays the log after a crash.

Two things about jbd2 are worth stating up front. First, it is a self-contained layer under fs/jbd2/, not part of ext4: it knows nothing about inodes or directories, only about buffers of bytes that must be made durable atomically, which is why other filesystems (such as ocfs2) can use it too. Ext4 is simply its principal client. Second, the journal is itself stored on the volume—normally as a hidden internal file occupying reserved inode 8, sized by mke2fs to a few tens or hundreds of megabytes, though it can instead live on a separate device for filesystems that want the log's writes off the main spindle. Either way it is a fixed-size circular log: transactions are appended at its head and retired from its tail, and the whole mechanism below is about filling and draining that ring safely. Figure 18-10 sketches the overall architecture.

Why Journaling

A filesystem operation as simple as creating a file changes several independent on-disk structures: it allocates an inode (updating the inode bitmap and group descriptor), writes the inode, and adds a directory entry. If the machine crashes partway through, the disk is left inconsistent—an inode marked used but not in any directory, or a directory entry pointing at an uninitialized inode. Ext2's answer was to scan and repair the entire volume with e2fsck after any unclean shutdown, which on a large disk is unacceptably slow. Journaling replaces that whole-volume scan with a log. Before the real structures are modified, the changes are written to a journal—a reserved area of the disk—and marked complete with a commit record. After a crash, recovery need only replay the committed transactions from the journal, a bounded operation proportional to the journal size rather than the volume size. A change that had not yet committed is simply discarded, leaving the filesystem as if the operation never began.

The technique is write-ahead logging: the log is written ahead of the real update, so the disk always holds either the old consistent state or a durable record of how to reach the new one. Jbd2's log is physical and redo-only—it stores complete post-modification copies of the affected metadata blocks, not a logical description like "increment the free count." That choice makes replay trivially idempotent: writing a journaled block to its home location produces the same result whether it runs once or ten times, so recovery can be interrupted by a second crash and simply restarted from the beginning with no harm. The price is that every journaled metadata block is written twice—once to the log, once to its home—which is why ext4 journals only metadata by default and batches aggressively to amortize the cost. Journaling also does not make e2fsck obsolete: replaying the log restores consistency after an ordinary crash in seconds, but a full check is still the backstop against bugs or media errors that corrupt the filesystem in ways no log can describe, and the kernel still forces one periodically or when it detects damage.

The Three Journaling Modes

Journaling metadata guarantees the filesystem's structure is consistent after a crash, but says nothing about file data. Ext4 offers three modes, selected by the data= mount option, that trade data safety against speed. Table 18-11 compares them.

The default, ordered mode, is the important one to understand because it gives most of the safety of full data journaling at a fraction of the cost. It journals only metadata, but imposes an ordering rule: before a transaction that references newly-allocated data blocks may commit, those data blocks must already be on disk. This closes the dangerous window that writeback mode leaves open—where the metadata (say, a file's new size and block pointers) reaches disk but the data does not, so after a crash the file appears to contain whatever stale bytes were previously in those blocks. Ordered mode guarantees a committed metadata change never exposes uninitialized data, without paying to journal the data itself. Ext4 implements the ordering through the jbd2 inode (jinode) attached to each file, which the commit path uses to flush the file's data before writing the commit record.

The other two modes sit on either side of ordered. Full data journaling (data=journal) writes every data block through the log as well, so after a crash a file's contents are recovered to their last committed state exactly like its metadata; the cost is that all data is written twice and the mode disables the delayed-allocation and direct-I/O paths, so it is usually reserved for workloads that value data atomicity over throughput, though it can paradoxically help fsync-heavy random-write loads by turning scattered home writes into sequential log writes. Writeback mode (data=writeback) drops the ordering rule entirely: metadata still commits atomically, but data may reach disk before or after it, so a file extended just before a crash can come back with the correct size but garbage contents. It is the fastest and the least safe, and is rarely chosen now that ordered mode's overhead is low. The mode is a property of the whole mount, set at mount time, not a per-file choice—so a filesystem runs entirely in one of the three. Ordered remains the default because it makes the guarantee users actually expect (no file ever exposes a previous owner's data) at close to writeback speed.

Transactions and Handles

Jbd2 organizes journaled changes at two levels. A handle (handle_t) represents one atomic filesystem operation—the metadata changes of a single create, unlink, or write—that must either all reach the journal or none do. A transaction (transaction_t) batches many handles together into one unit that is committed to the journal at once. Batching is the key to journaling performance: instead of writing the journal for every operation, jbd2 accumulates a few seconds' or a few thousand operations' worth of handles into one running transaction and commits them together, so the fixed cost of a commit is amortized over many operations. The workflow ext4 follows for any metadata change is a strict bracket. It starts a handle with ext4_journal_start(), declaring in advance how many metadata blocks it might modify—its credits—so jbd2 can guarantee the running transaction has room in the journal for them. For each metadata buffer it is about to change, it calls ext4_journal_get_write_access() to enroll the buffer in the transaction, makes its modification, and calls ext4_handle_dirty_metadata() to mark the buffer for journaling. Finally it stops the handle with ext4_journal_stop(). All the handles active in a window join the same running transaction; the credit reservations ensure the transaction never overflows the journal. This credit discipline—estimating the worst-case metadata footprint of every operation up front—is a distinctive and demanding part of writing filesystem code against jbd2.

A running transaction does not commit immediately. It stays open, collecting handles, until one of three things happens: the commit timer expires (the commit= mount option, five seconds by default), the transaction grows large enough to risk filling the journal, or an fsync or sync forces it out. Only then does jbd2 close it and hand it to the commit path. This window is exactly why data written to a fresh file can be lost in a crash a few seconds later even though the filesystem stays consistent—the transaction holding that operation had not yet committed. A handle that would outlive its worst-case credit estimate is not simply failed: jbd2 lets a long operation call jbd2_journal_extend to ask for more credits in the current transaction, or jbd2_journal_restart to close its handle, let the transaction commit, and reopen a fresh handle—so a truncate that frees millions of blocks proceeds in bounded journal-sized steps rather than demanding one impossibly large transaction.

Two finer points round out the model. Handles nest: if code already inside a handle starts another, jbd2 simply increments a reference count (h_ref) and joins the existing handle rather than opening a second one, so helper functions can each bracket their own work without creating separate transactions. And a block whose old contents matter—chiefly a bitmap block, where a bit being cleared must not be undone by replay of a stale copy—is enrolled with jbd2_journal_get_undo_access instead of the ordinary write-access call, telling jbd2 to preserve the committed version correctly. As it moves through commit a transaction advances through a fixed sequence of nine states recorded in t_state: T_RUNNING while it accepts handles, T_LOCKED once closed to new ones, a brief T_SWITCH while the journal hands over to the next running transaction, T_FLUSH and T_COMMIT as the data and journal writes go out, then T_COMMIT_DFLUSH and T_COMMIT_JFLUSH as each of those two waits completes, T_COMMIT_CALLBACK while the filesystem's commit callback runs—this is where ext4 releases the blocks of §6.2 that were freed by the transaction—and finally T_FINISHED. The states are this finely divided because different waiters care about different points: a process in fsync can be released once the journal writes are durable, well before the callback and cleanup finish.

A hard limit underlies the credit discipline. No single transaction may reserve more than j_max_transaction_buffers blocks, computed as a third of the usable journal, so even a transaction that has the log entirely to itself cannot fill it—the remainder must stay available for the committing transaction and for the checkpointing that will drain it. That bound is what makes jbd2_journal_restart necessary rather than merely convenient: an operation whose worst-case footprint exceeds a third of the journal cannot be expressed as one transaction at all, no matter how large the log, so it must be split. When a handle asks for credits the running transaction cannot supply, its caller blocks in __jbd2_log_wait_for_space(), which wakes the commit thread and waits for a checkpoint to retire enough of the tail. This is the back-pressure that keeps a small journal correct rather than catastrophic under a heavy metadata load: the filesystem slows to the rate at which the log can be drained.

Commit and Checkpoint

A running transaction becomes durable through commit, driven by the per-journal kjournald2 kernel thread. When a transaction is committed (because enough time passed, it grew large, or an fsync forced it), jbd2_journal_commit_transaction() (fs/jbd2/commit.c) runs a sequence of phases: it closes the transaction to new handles, in ordered mode flushes the associated file data to disk, writes the modified metadata blocks to the journal preceded by a descriptor block that lists their real destinations, and finally writes a commit block. The commit block is the atomic switch—until it is on disk the whole transaction is void; once it is, the transaction is durable. To make that switch reliable on real hardware, jbd2 issues the commit with a cache flush and the FUA (force-unit-access) flag (REQ_PREFLUSH | REQ_FUA) so the disk cannot reorder the commit block ahead of the data it depends on. A committed transaction still lives in the journal, not yet at its real on-disk location—the descriptor block records where each metadata block eventually belongs. Checkpointing (jbd2_log_do_checkpoint(), fs/jbd2/checkpoint.c) is the background process that copies journaled metadata from the log to its permanent home and then frees that portion of the journal for reuse. The journal is thus a circular buffer: commit appends transactions at the head, checkpoint retires them from the tail. Only when a transaction has been checkpointed is its journal space reclaimable; until then it is retained so recovery can replay it. This two-stage durability—fast commit to the log, lazy checkpoint to the final location—is what lets journaling add safety without forcing every metadata change to seek to its scattered real location synchronously.

A few details of the on-disk log make the commit trustworthy. Every journal block begins with the magic number 0xc03b3998 and a type tag—descriptor, commit, or revoke—so recovery can parse the ring without external metadata. The descriptor block carries a tag per journaled block giving its real home address and flags; one flag, escape, handles the awkward case of a metadata block whose own first bytes happen to equal the journal magic, which jbd2 rewrites so it cannot be mistaken for a journal record and restores on replay. With the metadata-checksum feature the commit block also carries a checksum over the whole transaction (the version-3 journal format), so a commit block that was only partially written—torn across the crash—is detected and the transaction treated as incomplete, rather than trusting a commit that never fully reached the platter. After a transaction is checkpointed and its space reclaimed, jbd2 advances the journal superblock's start pointer and sequence number so the tail moves forward and the freed blocks can be overwritten by future commits.

The batching that makes journaling affordable has a counterintuitive twist in the fsync path. When a synchronous handle stops, jbd2 does not immediately force a commit; it deliberately sleeps first, in jbd2_journal_stop(), to give other processes calling fsync at the same moment a chance to join the same transaction and share its cost. How long it sleeps is self-tuning: the journal measures its own j_average_commit_time and waits about that long, clamped between j_min_batch_time (zero by default) and j_max_batch_time (15 milliseconds), both exposed under /sys/fs/ext4/<device>/. So the system waits proportionally to what a commit actually costs on this hardware rather than a fixed guess. One refinement keeps the heuristic honest: if the same process was the last synchronous writer, the wait is skipped entirely, since a single thread calling fsync in a loop has no one to batch with and would only be slowed down. The effect is that a server with many concurrent committing clients pays roughly one commit for all of them, while a lone writer pays no added latency.

The preflush-and-FUA commit can also be relaxed deliberately. The journal_async_commit mount option enables the JBD2_FEATURE_INCOMPAT_ASYNC_COMMIT journal feature, which issues the commit block without waiting for the preceding journal writes to be acknowledged. The ordering guarantee is then recovered a different way: because the commit block carries a checksum over the transaction's contents, recovery can detect a commit block that reached the platter ahead of the blocks it describes and reject the transaction, rather than relying on the drive to have honored a barrier. It trades a stricter write ordering for a verifiable one, which on hardware with a slow cache flush can be a significant win—and it is why the option implies journal checksumming and cannot be used without it.

Recovery and Revoke

After a crash, mounting the volume triggers recovery (jbd2_journal_recover(), fs/jbd2/recovery.c), which replays the journal in three passes. The scan pass reads forward from the journal's tail to find the end of the last fully-committed transaction. The revoke pass collects all revoke records (explained below). The replay pass then walks the committed transactions again and writes each journaled metadata block to its real destination—unless it was revoked—bringing the filesystem to the consistent state it had at the last commit. Anything after the last commit block is ignored, so an interrupted transaction simply vanishes. Figure 18-12 shows the on-disk journal and the recovery passes.

The revoke record deserves explanation because it guards against a subtle replay hazard. Suppose a metadata block—say a directory block—is journaled, then later the file is deleted and that same physical block is reused as ordinary file data. If a crash occurs and recovery blindly replayed the journal, it would write the old journaled directory contents over the block that now holds user data, corrupting the file. A revoke record (fs/jbd2/revoke.c) prevents this: when a journaled metadata block is freed and may be reused as data, jbd2 logs a revoke for it, and the revoke pass of recovery collects these so the replay pass knows to skip any block that was later revoked. It is a small mechanism that makes the log safe to replay even though blocks change roles over time—one of the details that makes journaling correct rather than merely plausible.

Recovery inherits the same idempotence that makes replay safe. Because it only copies journaled block images to their homes and skips revoked ones, running it twice leaves the same result as running it once, so a crash during recovery is harmless—the next mount simply replays from the tail again. The journal superblock records where the tail is; when a filesystem is unmounted cleanly the log is checkpointed empty and its start pointer set to zero, which is how the next mount knows there is nothing to recover and skips the passes entirely. Revoke records carry the transaction sequence number at which the block was revoked, so the replay pass cancels only journaled copies older than the revoke and still applies a legitimate re-journaling of the same block in a later transaction—the mechanism is precise, not a blanket ban on the address. When replay finishes, the filesystem matches its state at the last commit; ext4 then clears the journal and, if anything looked wrong or a periodic check is due, may still run e2fsck as the deeper backstop the previous subsection described.

Not every failure is a crash, and jbd2 has a distinct response for the case where it can no longer trust itself: jbd2_journal_abort(). An I/O error writing the log, or an ext4 error handler running under errors=remount-ro, shuts the journal down for the life of the mount. The semantics are deliberately specific. Every attempt to start a new handle returns -EROFS, and a handle already open discovers the abort when it calls jbd2_journal_stop, which returns -EIO—so an operation in flight learns that it failed rather than silently appearing to succeed. Atomicity is gone, and jbd2 says so plainly: dirty journaled buffers are written back to their home locations without passing through the journal, on the reasoning that leaving as much correct data as possible on disk gives e2fsck more to work with than abandoning it. The error number is recorded in the journal superblock, which is the part that outlives the crash: the next mount reads it, knows the volume was abandoned in an untrusted state, and insists on a full check before the filesystem may be used again. Abort is the point at which the log stops being a repair mechanism and becomes a warning to the next mount.

Fast Commit and Modern Features

Ext4 has continued to gain capabilities well beyond what ext3 offered, and a few are worth describing because they change how the filesystem behaves in ways the 2.6-era ext3 could not. This section covers fast commit, inline data, and the encryption and integrity features.

What these have in common is how they were added. Each is an optional capability layered onto the same on-disk framework through a feature flag (§1.2), not a redesign: a volume gains fast commit, inline data, encryption, or verity only when the corresponding flag is set at format or enable time, and a kernel or e2fsprogs that predates a feature either ignores it safely or refuses the mount according to which flag class it falls in. That is how ext4 has absorbed a decade of new capabilities while remaining the same filesystem that still mounts an ext2 image—the mechanism §1.2 described, seen here doing real work.

Fast Commit

Ordinary jbd2 commit is coarse: an fsync() on a single file forces the entire running transaction—everyone's batched metadata changes—out to the journal, and each committed metadata block is written in full. For a workload that calls fsync frequently on small files (a database, a mail server), that is far more I/O than the one file's change actually required. Fast commit (fs/ext4/fast_commit.c, the FAST_COMMIT feature) is a finer-grained journaling path that addresses this. Instead of committing the whole transaction, it logs a compact, logical description of just the changes the fsync'd inode needs—records for the inode itself, its directory entries, and the specific block ranges added—into a dedicated fast-commit area, and makes them durable with a single small write. ext4_fc_commit() writes these tagged records (TLVs) terminated by a tail record that makes the batch atomic.

Fast commit does not replace the normal journal; it sits alongside it as an optimization for the common fsync case. A fast commit records the minimal logical delta and can be replayed on top of the last full commit, so recovery first replays the regular journal and then the fast-commit records. When a change is too complex to express as a simple fast-commit record, the inode is marked ineligible and falls back to a full transaction commit. The result is that a durability-sensitive application doing many small synchronous writes pays far less journal I/O per fsync, which is a substantial throughput gain for exactly the workloads that stress a journal hardest—and a capability with no analogue in the 2.6-era ext3.

The records themselves are a small vocabulary of nine tagged types. An EXT4_FC_TAG_INODE record carries the updated inode; EXT4_FC_TAG_ADD_RANGE and EXT4_FC_TAG_DEL_RANGE record extents added to or removed from it; and EXT4_FC_TAG_CREAT, EXT4_FC_TAG_LINK, and EXT4_FC_TAG_UNLINK capture the directory-entry changes that name or unname the file. Three more frame the batch rather than describe a change: EXT4_FC_TAG_HEAD opens a run and records the transaction id the records are relative to, EXT4_FC_TAG_PAD fills the remainder of a block so that records never straddle a block boundary, and EXT4_FC_TAG_TAIL, carrying a checksum, closes each batch and is what makes it atomic on replay. These fast-commit blocks live in a region of the journal reserved for them (jbd2 advertises the fast-commit feature), so recovery finds them in the same log it already scans. Not every change can be reduced to these records, though: an extended-attribute update, a cross-directory rename, a directory rename, a fallocate on a range, a resize, or a file switched to data-journaling marks the filesystem ineligible, not merely the inode that caused it. This is worth stating precisely, because it is the feature's sharpest limitation: ext4_fc_mark_ineligible() takes a superblock, sets EXT4_MF_FC_INELIGIBLE on the whole mount, and records the transaction id at which it happened, after which every commit on the volume falls back to a full jbd2 commit until that transaction has committed normally. A single setxattr anywhere on the filesystem therefore switches off the fast path for every other file until the next full commit catches up. Fast commit is thus a fast path for the common case—an fsync that added or extended one file on a volume where nothing unusual is happening—with the full journal always behind it as the general mechanism. It was added in Linux 5.10.

Ext4 enumerates thirteen distinct ineligibility reasons and counts each one separately, which turns the limitation into something an administrator can actually diagnose. Beyond the operations just listed, the set includes a MOVE_EXT from the defragmenter, an extent-to-indirect migration, enabling verity, swapping the boot loader inode, an encrypted filename, and even a memory-allocation failure—NOMEM is an ineligibility reason because the fast-commit path has nowhere to retry, so it must degrade rather than fail. The per-reason counters, alongside the number of fast commits and the count that fell back, are published at /proc/fs/ext4/<device>/fc_info, in the per-mount procfs directory of §4.1. If fast commit is enabled but a workload sees no benefit, that file names the operation responsible. Internally the inodes awaiting a fast commit sit on two lists, FC_Q_MAIN and FC_Q_STAGING, so that changes made while a fast commit is already in flight accumulate on the staging list rather than racing the one being written—the same running-versus-committing split that jbd2 uses for full transactions, applied one level down.

Inline Data

The INLINE_DATA feature, mentioned when the inode was introduced, addresses the opposite end of the size spectrum: very small files and directories. A file of a few dozen bytes would normally consume a whole data block (typically 4 KiB) to hold its contents plus an extent to point at it—mostly waste. With inline data, such a file's contents are stored directly in the inode's i_block area and its extended-attribute space instead, so the file occupies no data block at all and needs no extent. Since a directory is just a small file of directory entries, a small directory can be inlined the same way. On filesystems with many tiny files—configuration trees, source repositories, mail spools—this both saves space and speeds access, because the data is read along with the inode in a single I/O rather than requiring a second block read. It is the modern realization of ext2's "block fragmentation" feature, which was planned but never implemented.

The mechanism reuses the two spare areas of the inode. The first 60 bytes go in the i_block array—the same space that would otherwise hold the extent root—and anything beyond that spills into a system extended attribute named system.data kept in the inode's extra area. The 60 bytes are not a tunable number but simply the size of i_block, fifteen 32-bit entries, which is why EXT4_MIN_INLINE_DATA_SIZE is defined from that array rather than written down as a constant. How much the extra area contributes is more variable than it first appears: ext4_get_max_inline_size() computes it as whatever is left in the inode after i_extra_isize, the attribute header, and the entry describing system.data itself, which on a 256-byte inode with the usual 32-byte extra header works out to about 130 bytes in total. The important consequence is that inline capacity is not a function of inode size alone—the extra area is the same space ordinary extended attributes live in, so a file carrying an ACL or a SELinux label has correspondingly less room to inline, and may be pushed out to a real block at a size where an otherwise identical file would still fit. An inline inode is flagged with EXT4_INLINE_DATA_FL and has neither an extent tree nor any allocated block—the two representations are mutually exclusive. When such a file or directory grows past what the inode can hold, ext4 transparently converts it: it allocates a real data block, copies the inline bytes out, builds an extent to point at them, and clears the inline flag, after which the file behaves like any other. For a directory the inlined form even keeps the . and .. entries in their expected places so the conversion to a real directory block is seamless.

Encryption and Verity

Finally, ext4 integrates two security features through generic VFS frameworks. fscrypt provides transparent per-directory encryption: a directory can be marked so that the file contents and names beneath it are encrypted on disk with a key supplied by the user, and decrypted only in the page cache for a process holding the key—so the raw device reveals nothing without it. fs-verity provides the complementary integrity guarantee: a file can be made read-only and sealed under a Merkle tree of hashes, so that any later corruption or tampering of its contents is detected on read, which is used to protect critical read-only files such as installed application packages. Both are implemented as shared VFS services (used by f2fs and others too) that ext4 opts into rather than ext4-specific code—the same pattern of leaning on generic infrastructure that this chapter has seen with iomap and jbd2. Together with metadata checksums, they extend ext4's integrity and confidentiality guarantees well past anything the ext2/ext3 of Linux 2.6 provided.

Two properties of these frameworks are worth making explicit. fscrypt encrypts file contents and names but deliberately not the metadata—sizes, timestamps, owners, and the directory tree's shape stay in cleartext—so it protects the data at rest without hiding that a filesystem exists or how it is structured; the key is a master key held in a keyring and referenced by a per-directory policy, and contents and names are enciphered with modes such as AES-256-XTS and AES-256-CTS (with hardware inline-crypto offload where the block device supports it). This is also why an encrypted filename is one of the conditions that makes a fast commit ineligible: the logical directory-entry record it would log cannot expose the plaintext name. fs-verity, by contrast, targets authenticity: sealing a file builds a Merkle tree of hashes over its data, stored past the file's end, and makes the file permanently read-only; every read verifies the pages against the tree, whose single root hash can be signed so the kernel can attest that a file matches a trusted measurement—the basis for verified boot of application images. Neither feature is ext4-specific, which is the recurring lesson of this chapter: the modern ext4 is as much an assembly of shared kernel services—iomap, jbd2, fscrypt, fs-verity, the page cache—as it is a body of ext4-only code.

A few practical details finish the picture. AES-256-XTS and AES-256-CTS are the usual choices but not the only ones: fscrypt also offers Adiantum, a mode built for devices whose processors lack AES acceleration and which would otherwise pay an unacceptable penalty for encrypting at rest, along with SM4 and HCTR2 variants. The choice is recorded in the directory's policy, so it is fixed when encryption is enabled rather than at mount time. On the verity side, ext4 stores the Merkle tree and its descriptor past the file's i_size, in blocks that belong to the inode but lie beyond the visible end of the file, and locates the descriptor by way of the last allocated block —which is why enabling verity first truncates away any other blocks past EOF that might confuse the search. Enabling it is itself a transactional operation: the inode is put on the orphan list first, so a crash midway through leaves a file the next mount will clean up rather than one stuck half-sealed. Verity and DAX are mutually exclusive, since a mapping handed straight to userspace cannot be verified page by page on the way out.

Summary

This chapter followed a single concrete filesystem, ext4, from its on-disk bytes to its crash-recovery log. The lesson of ext4 is evolution under compatibility: it keeps ext2's block groups, inode, and directory format—so old volumes remain valid and one driver serves all three filesystems—while layering on the features a modern filesystem needs, negotiated through the superblock's three families of feature flags. Its on-disk structures are the ext2 ones widened for 64-bit volumes, larger inodes, nanosecond timestamps, and, pervasively, crc32c metadata checksums that ext2 lacked entirely. The larger inode also bought room for extended attributes to live in line with the inode they describe, which matters more than it sounds: the ACLs, security labels, and encryption policies that a modern system attaches to every file are read for free out of the block the inode already came from.

The deepest change is how files map to disk. Extents replaced ext2's indirect-block map, recording contiguous runs in a shallow tree rooted in the inode, which shrinks metadata and matches the way the multi-block allocator places files in large contiguous runs. Delayed allocation defers block assignment to writeback so whole extents are allocated at once and short-lived files cost nothing, backed by an in-memory extent status tree with no on-disk counterpart. In memory, a mount is anchored by an ext4_sb_info and each open file by an ext4_inode_info that embeds the VFS inode, and ext4 presents itself to the VFS through the standard super, inode, file, and address-space operation tables—now routing data through iomap rather than through buffer heads as in Linux 2.6. And crash consistency, the reason ext3 existed, is provided by the general jbd2 layer: handles batch into transactions, transactions commit atomically to a circular journal and are later checkpointed to their real locations, and after a crash a three-pass recovery replays the committed log, with revoke records keeping replay safe as blocks change roles. Fast commit, inline data, case-insensitive lookup, large folios in the page cache, atomic direct writes, and the fscrypt and fs-verity frameworks round out a filesystem that is recognizably ext2's descendant yet substantially new. Two themes cut across the sections and are worth separating out, because neither is visible from any single structure. The first is that space accounting has become as intricate as space allocation. Free blocks are no longer simply counted: delayed allocation promises them before they have locations, freed blocks stay unavailable until the transaction that released them commits, and a small pool is held back permanently because on an extent filesystem the act of freeing space can itself require allocating a metadata block. An ENOSPC from ext4 is therefore a statement about several overlapping reservations rather than a simple shortage, which is why the allocator forces a journal commit and retries before believing it. The second is that almost every mechanism in the chapter is a negotiated trade rather than a fixed rule, and the negotiation is usually exposed: how large a run the allocator rounds a request up to, how long a commit waits for other fsync callers to join it, how much of an unwritten extent is zeroed instead of split, how many extent-status entries the shrinker may reclaim. Ext4's tunables under /sys/fs/ext4/ are not incidental—they are the places where the filesystem declines to guess, and reading them is a fast way to see which of its decisions are genuinely heuristic.
