Chapter 21

Power Management

How Linux 7.0 saves energy at every scale — putting the whole system to sleep and waking it again, hibernating to disk, powering individual devices and whole power domains down while the system runs, and choosing each CPU’s frequency and idle state, all steered by the Energy Model and held in check by thermal management, on ACPI-based x86_64 machines and PSCI- and SCMI-based ARM64 ones.

Linux 2.6.11 had the pieces of power management only in embryo—APM and ACPI suspend-to-RAM, the swsusp hibernation code, a cpufreq core with an on-demand governor, and ACPI processor C-states entered from the idle loop—but they were peripheral features of a kernel that still woke every idle CPU with a periodic tick and could not power a single device down while the system ran. Two decades later the balance has reversed. A phone, a laptop, and a rack of servers all spend most of their lives partly asleep, and the kernel decides, thousands of times a second, how deeply each CPU should idle, how fast each core should clock, which devices may lose power, and when the whole machine can stop. Power management has become one of the kernel's core resource-allocation problems, on the same footing as scheduling and memory management, and it reaches into almost every subsystem this book has described.

This chapter and the next complete Part III with two subsystems that 2.6.11 already had. Much of today's power-management code is younger than that kernel: tickless idle arrived in 2.6.21, the cpuidle framework in 2.6.24, the device-callback table struct dev_pm_ops in 2.6.29, the runtime PM core in 2.6.32, generic PM domains in 3.1, and the Energy Model, which ties power numbers to performance states for the scheduler, in 5.0. Earlier chapters met it at the edges. Chapter 3 introduced the TASK_FROZEN state the freezer uses (§3.3); Chapter 4 the IRQF_NO_SUSPEND flag and enable_irq_wake() (§8.2); Chapter 6 tickless idle, tick broadcasting, and how timekeeping survives suspend (§4.6, §6.6, §7.3); Chapter 7 energy-aware scheduling and the scheduler's cpufreq hook (§10, §11.4); and Chapter 13 the PM fields of the device model and the consumer side of SoC power domains (§3.4, §8.4). This chapter is where the power-management subsystems themselves are taken apart.

It works from the largest scale to the smallest. §1 explains why power is a kernel problem, which firmware interfaces the kernel works through, and how power and residency are measured. §2 covers the system sleep states user space can request through /sys/power/state and the platform hooks behind them, and ends with power-off and reboot, the transitions that do not come back. §3 follows the ordered suspend sequence that freezes tasks, suspends every device in dependency order, and hands the last CPU to firmware, together with the freezer, wakeup sources, and the tools for diagnosing a suspend that fails. §4 describes hibernation, which saves memory to disk and survives a full power cycle. §5 and §6 turn to devices that sleep while the system runs: runtime power management for individual devices and PM domains for groups that share a power rail. §7 and §8 cover the CPU itself, with cpufreq choosing operating frequencies, cpuidle choosing idle states, and PM QoS bounding both, and §9 the Energy Model, thermal framework, and powercap, which feed physical reality—watts and degrees—back into those decisions. Throughout, the chapter treats both of the book's architectures explicitly: x86_64 machines described by ACPI, with Intel and AMD performance-state hardware, and ARM64 systems that talk to firmware through PSCI and SCMI and describe their power topology in the device tree. It leaves out the ACPI interpreter (ACPICA and AML), the power-supply and battery class, and the clock and regulator provider frameworks whose consumer side is in Chapter 13, §8; devfreq, which applies the cpufreq ideas to buses and GPUs, gets only a sketch.

Why Power Management Is a Kernel Problem

It is tempting to think of power management as a driver concern: each device knows how to turn itself off, so let it. The reason Linux needs a large shared framework instead is that power decisions have global consequences. A device cannot lose power while a child device still depends on it; a CPU cannot enter a deep idle state if a driver needs an interrupt serviced within twenty microseconds; the whole system cannot suspend while a wakeup event is still being processed.

The decisions also run on very different timescales, which is the other reason they belong in the kernel. An idle state is chosen every time a CPU runs out of work, often thousands of times a second, and the choice must cost no more than a few microseconds. Frequency is re-evaluated every few milliseconds from the scheduler's own utilization updates. A runtime-suspended device usually waits an autosuspend delay of tens of milliseconds to seconds, and system sleep lasts minutes or hours. The fastest of these could only ever be made in kernel code, and all of them depend on information that only the kernel has: the scheduler's view of load, the device model's dependency graph, the next timer expiry, and the count of wakeup events still in flight. The division of labour follows from that. User space sets policy: systemd-logind decides when to suspend, power-profiles-daemon or tuned picks a performance bias, and Android's power HAL holds and releases wakeup sources. The kernel supplies the mechanisms, and makes the decisions that must be taken at once within the limits user space has set.

Where the Power Goes

The power drawn by CMOS logic has two components. Dynamic power is spent switching transistors and grows roughly as C·V2·f—capacitance times the square of the supply voltage times the clock frequency. Because a higher frequency needs a higher voltage to switch reliably, the two rise together, and dynamic power grows much faster than linearly with clock speed. A voltage and frequency pair that the silicon is qualified to run at is called an operating performance point (OPP), and lowering the OPP is the lever cpufreq pulls (§7). Static power, or leakage, flows whenever a block is powered at all, whether it is switching or not, and on modern processes it is a large fraction of the total. Leakage can only be removed by cutting the supply to a block, which is what idle states (§8), runtime PM (§5), and PM domains (§6) do at progressively larger scales.

These two components pull in different directions. Running slowly saves dynamic power but keeps the chip leaking for longer; running fast and then powering down completely—race to idle—can win when leakage dominates. Neither rule is universally right, and the kernel does not hard-code one. Instead it asks platform code to describe the cost of each performance state (the Energy Model, §9.1), the cost of entering and leaving each idle state (the target residency and exit latency of §8), and the latency each workload can tolerate (PM QoS, §8.4), and it lets governors make the trade-off with those numbers in hand.

The cores are not the whole chip. Around them sit the shared caches, the memory controller, the interconnect and I/O fabric, and the clocks and voltage regulators that feed them, and these draw power as long as anything in the system might need them. The deepest savings therefore come only when everything is idle at once. An Intel or AMD package reaches its deepest package C-states (PC6 to PC10) only when every core is in a deep core C-state and the uncore has nothing to do. The platform state behind Modern Standby, S0ix, goes further: the platform's power-management controller enters it only from the deepest package state, and only if every device is quiescent, with each PCIe link in a low-power state under Active State Power Management (ASPM) and each device having declared, through Latency Tolerance Reporting (LTR), that it can wait for the slow exit. ARM64 SoCs apply the same rule through cluster and system power domains, which can be switched off only when the last CPU or device inside them is idle (§6.4). As a result, one device whose driver never runtime-suspends, or one PCIe link with ASPM disabled, can keep a whole laptop out of its lowest-power state. This is why the tools of §1.4 and §3.5 report how long the platform stayed in each state, not just whether it entered.

Software can waste power as surely as hardware. Every timer interrupt, every work item queued on an idle CPU, and every polling loop pulls a core out of its idle state, pays the exit latency, and restarts the residency clock that deeper states need. A CPU woken a thousand times a second never reaches the states whose target residency is measured in milliseconds. Much of the kernel's timer design is therefore about not waking CPUs. Tickless idle stops the periodic tick on an idle CPU (Chapter 6, §7.3). A deferrable timer never wakes an idle CPU at all, and runs only when the CPU wakes for another reason. Since 6.9 the timer migration hierarchy lets ordinary timers that are not pinned to a CPU expire on a CPU that is awake anyway (Chapter 6, §9.7). Timer slack lets the kernel batch nearby hrtimer expiries, and a process can widen its own slack with prctl(PR_SET_TIMERSLACK) (Chapter 6, §8.8). Power-efficient workqueues, enabled with workqueue.power_efficient or CONFIG_WQ_POWER_EFFICIENT_DEFAULT, let work that would otherwise be bound to the submitting CPU run wherever the scheduler finds a CPU already awake (Chapter 4, §11.11).

Power is also heat. Every joule a chip consumes leaves it as heat, and a phone without a fan or a server packed into a rack cannot dissipate unlimited amounts of it. The thermal framework (§9.2) closes the loop: it watches temperature sensors and, when a trip point is crossed, caps the same frequency and idle controls that performance policy would otherwise use freely.

Three Scales of Power Management

Linux organizes its power-management code around three scales, shown in Figure 21-1. System sleep stops the whole machine: user space is frozen, every device is put into a low-power state, and the CPUs either idle as deeply as they can or are handed to firmware, until a wakeup event brings everything back. This is what closing a laptop lid does, and it lives mostly in kernel/power/. Device power management works on individual devices while the system is running: a USB controller with nothing plugged in, an idle audio codec, or a GPU block whose display is off can be powered down independently. Its core is in drivers/base/power/, and its domain-level extension in drivers/pmdomain/. CPU power management runs continuously, choosing each core's frequency (drivers/cpufreq/) and, whenever a core has nothing to run, its idle state (drivers/cpuidle/).

The three scales share data structures and constrain one another. System suspend invokes the same struct dev_pm_ops table that runtime PM uses, and it must cope with devices that runtime PM has already put to sleep. Suspend-to-idle, the most common system sleep state on modern laptops, is literally implemented by letting every CPU fall into the cpuidle path with the deepest state allowed. And the cross-cutting inputs on the right of the figure—the Energy Model, thermal limits, PM QoS constraints, and power caps—all act by adjusting the policy of the CPU and device layers rather than by switching hardware directly.

Each scale has its own configuration options and its own user-space controls. CONFIG_PM builds the device PM core and runtime PM. CONFIG_SUSPEND and CONFIG_HIBERNATION add the system sleep states, and either of them enables CONFIG_PM_SLEEP; on SMP machines that in turn selects CPU hotplug (CONFIG_PM_SLEEP_SMP), because suspend-to-RAM and hibernation take the non-boot CPUs offline. CONFIG_CPU_FREQ and CONFIG_CPU_IDLE are independent of the rest, so a server kernel can build cpufreq and cpuidle with no sleep state at all. At run time, system sleep is controlled through /sys/power/ (§2.1). Every device has a power/ directory, whose control, runtime_status, and autosuspend_delay_ms attributes belong to runtime PM (§5.2) and whose wakeup attribute decides whether the device may wake the system (§3.4). Each CPU's frequency policy and idle states appear under /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpufreq/ and /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuN/cpuidle/. Devices other than CPUs that can trade performance against power continuously, such as GPUs, memory controllers, and interconnects, are handled by devfreq (§7.3), which brings cpufreq's model of governors choosing among OPPs to the device layer.

One whole-machine transition does not fit the picture of saving power and coming back: switching the machine off or restarting it. Power-off and reboot reuse the device-model walk that system sleep relies on, calling a ->shutdown() method on every device in reverse order, and they end in the same firmware interfaces, ACPI on x86_64 and PSCI on ARM64. The difference is that nothing is saved and there is no resume path. §2.4 covers them alongside the sleep states.

Firmware Partners: ACPI, PSCI, and SCMI

The kernel rarely touches power rails directly. On both architectures the final steps—removing power from a CPU cluster, putting DRAM into self-refresh, changing a voltage regulator—are performed by firmware or by a dedicated power-management controller, and the kernel's job is to decide when and to ask correctly. The two architectures arrived at very different interfaces for that request.

On x86_64 the interface is ACPI. ACPI defines global sleep states S0–S5 (S3 is suspend-to-RAM, S4 hibernation, S5 soft-off), device power states D0–D3 set by AML methods such as _PS0 and _PS3, processor idle states C0–Cn described by _CST, and performance control through either legacy _PSS P-states or CPPC (Collaborative Processor Performance Control), an abstract performance scale the OS writes and the hardware interprets. Modern Intel and AMD processors bypass much of ACPI for CPU control: Intel's Hardware P-states (HWP) and AMD's CPPC implementation are programmed through model-specific registers, and idle states are entered with the MWAIT instruction using hints the intel_idle driver knows per CPU model. Recent laptops increasingly do not implement S3 at all, and offer only “Modern Standby”, which Linux implements as suspend-to-idle with the low-power S0 idle (LPS0) _DSM hooks (drivers/acpi/x86/s2idle.c).

Two further x86 platform components matter even though they are not part of ACPI's core model. The first is the power-management controller (PMC) in the chipset or SoC, which decides whether the platform has actually reached S0ix. The intel_pmc_core driver (drivers/platform/x86/intel/pmc/) and the amd_pmc driver (drivers/platform/x86/amd/pmc/) read its counters, report which IP blocks kept the platform out of S0ix, and on AMD send the final “enter S0ix” message to the SoC firmware. The second is the vendor performance mode that many laptops implement in firmware, often trading fan noise and skin temperature against sustained power. The kernel unifies these as the platform profile (drivers/acpi/platform_profile.c): drivers register the profiles they support, from low-power and quiet through balanced to performance, and user space chooses one through /sys/firmware/acpi/platform_profile. Since 6.14 each handler is also a device under /sys/class/platform-profile/, so that a laptop with more than one firmware interface for this can expose them all.

On ARM64 the counterpart is a set of narrower, purpose-built interfaces. PSCI, the Power State Coordination Interface, is a calling convention for SMC or HVC calls into secure firmware (or a hypervisor) that brings CPUs on and off line, suspends a CPU into an idle state with CPU_SUSPEND, and suspends the whole system with SYSTEM_SUSPEND. SCMI, the System Control and Management Interface, is a message protocol to a separate system control processor that owns clocks, voltages, performance domains, power domains, and sensors; the kernel's SCMI drivers translate cpufreq requests, genpd power changes, and thermal reads into SCMI messages. Where neither is used, the device tree describes OPP tables, idle-states, and power-domain topology directly, and the kernel drives clocks and regulators itself. ARM64 servers that boot with ACPI use ACPI's CPPC and Low Power Idle (_LPI) tables in place of the device tree, but still enter idle and system-suspend states through PSCI.

Both ARM64 interfaces have grown by version. PSCI 0.2 defined the core function set: CPU_ON, CPU_OFF, CPU_SUSPEND, SYSTEM_OFF, and SYSTEM_RESET. PSCI 1.0 added PSCI_FEATURES, SYSTEM_SUSPEND, and the OS-initiated idle mode selected with SET_SUSPEND_MODE (§6.4). PSCI 1.1 added SYSTEM_RESET2 for warm and vendor-specific resets, and PSCI 1.3 added SYSTEM_OFF2, whose hibernate type tells firmware that the machine is being switched off with an image saved. The driver in drivers/firmware/psci/psci.c asks for the version at boot, probes the optional functions with PSCI_FEATURES, and uses only what is present. The conduit, SMC to EL3 firmware or HVC to a hypervisor, comes from the method property in the device tree or from the ACPI FADT. Inside a KVM guest the same calls are HVCs that the host implements (Chapter 28, §4.3), which is how a guest brings vCPUs online and powers itself off. SCMI (drivers/firmware/arm_scmi/) is organized as numbered protocols: base (0x10), power domain (0x11), system power (0x12), performance (0x13), clock (0x14), sensor (0x15), reset (0x16), voltage (0x17), powercap (0x18), and, since 6.11, pin control (0x19). Messages travel over one of several transports: a mailbox with shared memory, an SMC, OP-TEE, or virtio for virtual machines. The system power protocol also runs in the other direction, letting the platform ask Linux to shut down or reboot. scmi_power_control.c turns a graceful request into orderly_poweroff() or orderly_reboot() and a forceful one into kernel_power_off() or kernel_restart() (§2.4). Measuring Power and Residency

Power management fails quietly. A device that never suspends or a timer that fires every millisecond produces no error, only a battery that drains too fast or a server that draws more than it should. Its effects therefore have to be measured, along three lines: how much energy was used, how long each component spent in each state, and what woke it.

On x86_64, energy comes from the RAPL counters described in §9.4. The power perf PMU (arch/x86/events/rapl.c) exposes them as events such as energy-pkg, energy-cores, energy-ram, and energy-psys, counted in joules, so perf stat -a -e power/energy-pkg/ sleep 10 reports the package energy used over ten seconds; the same counters are readable as energy_uj files under /sys/class/powercap/. ARM64 has no architectural energy counter. Where the platform provides one, it usually appears as an SCMI sensor read through hwmon (energyN_input) or through SCMI powercap. A battery fuel gauge reports power_now or energy_now through the power-supply class; otherwise the only option is an external meter.

Residency comes from both software and hardware. Each cpuidle state's sysfs directory counts how often the state was entered (usage), how long was spent in it (time), and how often the choice turned out too deep or too shallow (above, below) (§8.1). The cpufreq stats/ directory, built with CONFIG_CPU_FREQ_STAT, keeps time_in_state and a transition table for each policy. These figures record what the kernel asked for, which may not be what it got: hardware can demote a requested C-state to a shallower one, and package states depend on more than the CPU. The hardware's own residency counters, such as MSR_CORE_C6_RESIDENCY and the MSR_PKG_C*_RESIDENCY family, are exposed by the cstate_core and cstate_pkg perf PMUs (arch/x86/events/intel/cstate.c) and read directly by turbostat (tools/power/x86/turbostat/), which prints busy time, average frequency, core and package C-state residency, and RAPL power side by side. For the platform as a whole, the PMC drivers' debugfs files (pmc_core/slp_s0_residency_usec, amd_pmc/s0ix_stats) and the LPIT attributes low_power_idle_system_residency_us and low_power_idle_cpu_residency_us under /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuidle/ show whether S0ix was actually reached.

Wakeups are traced. The power trace system (include/trace/events/power.h) has cpu_idle, cpu_idle_miss, and cpu_frequency events, and together with the timer, IRQ, and workqueue tracepoints they show exactly which event ended each idle period (Chapter 29, §7; perf, which can record them, is in Chapter 29, §9). powertop builds on the same tracepoints to rank wakeup sources per second, and cpupower (tools/power/cpupower/) reports and sets frequency and idle-state settings. For system sleep, /sys/kernel/debug/wakeup_sources shows which wakeup sources were active and for how long (§3.4), and §3.5 covers the suspend-specific tools.

System Sleep States

A system sleep state is a condition in which the machine does no useful work, user space is stopped, and as much hardware as possible is powered down, but from which the kernel can return to exactly where it was. Linux supports four of them, and the difference between them is how much of the work of stopping the machine is done by the kernel and how much is delegated to firmware.

Which state a machine actually uses depends on its firmware more than on Linux. Most x86 laptops sold in recent years are designed for Microsoft's Modern Standby and therefore for suspend-to-idle, and many no longer implement S3 at all. Desktops and older laptops usually still offer S3, and servers often offer no sleep state but hibernation, or none at all. ARM64 boards offer suspend-to-RAM only when their firmware implements PSCI SYSTEM_SUSPEND, and suspend-to-idle otherwise. The kernel names the states, but on a typical desktop it is systemd that uses them. systemctl suspend, or logind reacting to the lid switch or power button, runs systemd-sleep, which writes the labels configured in sleep.conf to the files described below. Two composite modes are built in user space from the kernel's primitives. Hybrid sleep writes a hibernation image and then suspends to RAM instead of powering off, so that the machine resumes quickly but survives a flat battery (§4.1). Suspend-then-hibernate suspends with a CLOCK_BOOTTIME_ALARM timer armed (Chapter 6, §13.4), and hibernates when that timer wakes the machine. The alarm works because the alarm-timer driver's suspend callback, alarmtimer_suspend() in kernel/time/alarmtimer.c, programs the earliest pending alarm into the RTC as a wakeup event before the system goes down. If that alarm is less than two seconds away, the callback fails the suspend with -EBUSY instead.

/sys/power/state and mem_sleep

User space requests a transition by writing a label to /sys/power/state. Reading the file lists the states the running kernel supports; the possible labels are freeze, standby, mem, and disk. Internally the first three are values of suspend_state_t—PM_SUSPEND_TO_IDLE (1), PM_SUSPEND_STANDBY (2), and PM_SUSPEND_MEM (3), with PM_SUSPEND_ON (0) meaning “running” and PM_SUSPEND_MAX used by decode_state() as the code for disk, which is hibernation and takes a separate path entirely (§4). The store handler in kernel/power/main.c is short enough to show whole: 

/* kernel/power/main.c */
static ssize_t state_store(struct kobject *kobj, struct kobj_attribute *attr,
			   const char *buf, size_t n)
{
	suspend_state_t state;
	int error;

	error = pm_autosleep_lock();
	if (error)
		return error;

	if (pm_autosleep_state() > PM_SUSPEND_ON) {
		error = -EBUSY;
		goto out;
	}

	state = decode_state(buf, n);
	if (state < PM_SUSPEND_MAX) {
		if (state == PM_SUSPEND_MEM)
			state = mem_sleep_current;

		error = pm_suspend(state);
	} else if (state == PM_SUSPEND_MAX) {
		error = hibernate();
	} else {
		error = -EINVAL;
	}

 out:
	pm_autosleep_unlock();
	return error ? error : n;
}

Two details matter. First, a write is refused with -EBUSY while autosleep (§3.4) is active, because the kernel is then managing transitions itself. Second, the label mem does not necessarily mean suspend-to-RAM: it is remapped to mem_sleep_current, which user space can inspect and change through /sys/power/mem_sleep. That file, added in 4.10, lists the variants of “mem” the platform offers using a second set of labels—s2idle, shallow, and deep—with the current choice in brackets. The indirection exists because desktop environments universally write mem, while the best implementation of “sleep” differs from machine to machine; the mem_sleep_default= boot parameter and platform code choose the default. On ARM64 the list depends on PSCI. psci_init_system_suspend() calls suspend_set_ops() only if PSCI_FEATURES reports SYSTEM_SUSPEND, so a board whose firmware lacks it shows only [s2idle]. A KVM guest is in the same position unless the virtual machine monitor has enabled KVM_CAP_ARM_SYSTEM_SUSPEND (5.19), in which case the guest's SYSTEM_SUSPEND call exits to the VMM, which decides how to emulate it. The automatic choice of default can be wrong in either direction. mem_sleep_default=deep or mem_sleep_default=s2idle on the command line overrides it, and drivers/acpi/sleep.c carries a DMI quirk table whose init_default_s3() entries keep deep as the default on laptops whose suspend-to-idle is known to be broken. Writing to /sys/power/mem_sleep accepts only a label that is listed in the file, and like a write to /sys/power/state it is refused while autosleep is active.

Suspend-to-Idle versus Suspend-to-RAM

The states differ in how far the kernel goes before letting go. In suspend-to-idle (s2idle) the kernel freezes user space and suspends devices exactly as for a deeper state, but it never takes CPUs offline and never calls firmware to sleep. Instead, every CPU, including the one running the suspend code, drops into the idle loop, where cpuidle picks the deepest state that has an ->enter_s2idle callback. The last CPU to arrive freezes the tick and suspends timekeeping in tick_freeze(), so that, with no periodic timers and no runnable tasks, the platform can reach its lowest-power idle condition. s2idle_enter() in kernel/power/suspend.c shows how simple this is from the suspending CPU's side: 

/* kernel/power/suspend.c (abridged) */
static void s2idle_enter(void)
{
	raw_spin_lock_irq(&s2idle_lock);
	if (pm_wakeup_pending())
		goto out;

	s2idle_state = S2IDLE_STATE_ENTER;
	raw_spin_unlock_irq(&s2idle_lock);

	/* Push all the CPUs into the idle loop. */
	wake_up_all_idle_cpus();
	/* Make the current CPU wait so it can enter the idle loop too. */
	swait_event_exclusive(s2idle_wait_head,
		    s2idle_state == S2IDLE_STATE_WAKE);

	/*
	 * Kick all CPUs to ensure that they resume their timers and restore
	 * consistent system state.
	 */
	wake_up_all_idle_cpus();

	raw_spin_lock_irq(&s2idle_lock);

 out:
	s2idle_state = S2IDLE_STATE_NONE;
	raw_spin_unlock_irq(&s2idle_lock);
}

Setting s2idle_state to S2IDLE_STATE_ENTER is what idle_should_enter_s2idle() tests in the idle loop (§8.1). A wakeup interrupt, flagged by pm_system_wakeup(), calls s2idle_wake(), which moves the state to S2IDLE_STATE_WAKE and wakes the waiting task. The surrounding s2idle_loop() then asks the platform's ->wake() hook (or pm_wakeup_pending()) whether the event was a real wakeup; if not—a battery-status interrupt from the embedded controller, say—it calls s2idle_enter() again without ever resuming devices. Because no CPU goes offline and nothing jumps through firmware, s2idle resumes in milliseconds and works on every platform, but it saves only as much power as the idle states and device drivers allow. Modern laptop platforms are designed so that this is nearly as good as S3: the LPS0 _DSM calls tell the firmware the display is off and the system is entering low-power idle, letting it gate platform-level power that no device driver controls.

Deciding whether a wakeup is real is the hard part of s2idle on x86, because almost every ACPI event arrives through one shared interrupt, the System Control Interrupt (SCI). Before suspending, acpi_s2idle_prepare() arms the SCI as a wakeup interrupt, enables the GPEs (general-purpose events) of devices allowed to wake the system, and switches the embedded controller's GPE to wakeup mode. When the SCI fires, acpi_s2idle_wake() in drivers/acpi/sleep.c sorts out what happened. A wakeup that did not come through the SCI, for example from a USB controller's own interrupt, counts at once. So does a fixed ACPI event such as the power button, a handler registered by a driver that shares the SCI, or any GPE other than the embedded controller's. If only the EC's GPE fired, acpi_ec_dispatch_gpe() runs the EC's query handlers synchronously. If those handlers produced a wakeup event, such as a lid-open notification, the system resumes. Otherwise the event was housekeeping, such as a battery-level update, and the function clears the wakeup, re-arms the SCI with rearm_wake_irq(), and returns false, so the CPUs go straight back into idle. Platforms whose wakeups arrive as ordinary device interrupts, which includes most ARM64 systems, need no such filtering: pm_wakeup_pending() alone answers the question.

In suspend-to-RAM (the deep variant), the kernel goes further: after the last device callbacks it takes every non-boot CPU offline through CPU hotplug, disables interrupts on the boot CPU, runs the syscore callbacks, and calls the platform's ->enter() hook, which never returns in the normal sense. Firmware removes power from the CPU packages and most of the SoC, leaving DRAM in self-refresh; when a wakeup event arrives, the CPU restarts at a firmware-provided or kernel-provided entry point, restores its context, and returns from ->enter() as though it had been a function call. The standby state (ACPI S1) is the same sequence with a shallower firmware state that preserves CPU context; few modern systems offer it. Figure 21-2 lays the states out by depth.

On ARM64, suspend-to-idle reaches deeper than CPU idle through the PSCI power-domain hierarchy (§6.4). drivers/cpuidle/cpuidle-psci.c installs psci_enter_s2idle_domain_idle_state() as the ->enter_s2idle() callback of the deepest CPU idle state. Where the idle path drops a runtime PM reference on the CPU's PM domain, this callback calls dev_pm_genpd_suspend(), the system-suspend form of the same operation. When the last CPU in a cluster arrives, genpd can select the cluster's, and in turn the whole system's, deepest domain state, and the composite state is passed to firmware in a single CPU_SUSPEND call. On such a platform s2idle can reach the same SoC-wide retention state that SYSTEM_SUSPEND would, without taking any CPU offline. Under PREEMPT_RT, ordinary idle does not use the domain hierarchy, because the extra latency is unwelcome there, but s2idle and suspend-to-RAM still do.

Drivers often need to know which kind of sleep is in progress, because the same ->suspend() callback must be correct whether or not firmware will cut power afterwards. pm_suspend_target_state holds the state being entered, and three flags in pm_suspend_global_flags, read through helpers in include/linux/suspend.h, describe the platform's part. pm_suspend_via_firmware() is true when firmware will finish the transition; ACPI sets it in acpi_suspend_begin() for S3 and deeper. pm_resume_via_firmware() is true when firmware ran on the way back and may have reinitialized devices; acpi_suspend_enter() sets it after an S3 resume. pm_suspend_no_platform() is true for s2idle, where the kernel keeps full control of device power, and suspend_devices_and_enter() sets it. The NVMe driver's nvme_suspend() shows why they matter. For s2idle it leaves the controller running and puts it into its lowest NVMe power state, which resumes in milliseconds. If firmware will be involved, or ASPM is off so that a live PCIe link would keep the platform out of its deepest state, it shuts the controller down completely instead. The PCI core uses pm_suspend_no_platform() in the same way. When a driver has deliberately left its device in D0, as NVMe does, the PCI core leaves the device and the bridge above it untouched during s2idle, instead of forcing them into D3.

The Platform Hooks

The generic suspend code in kernel/power/suspend.c knows nothing about ACPI or PSCI. Platform code registers two operation tables, both declared in include/linux/suspend.h: 

/* include/linux/suspend.h */
struct platform_suspend_ops {
	int (*valid)(suspend_state_t state);
	int (*begin)(suspend_state_t state);
	int (*prepare)(void);
	int (*prepare_late)(void);
	int (*enter)(suspend_state_t state);
	void (*wake)(void);
	void (*finish)(void);
	bool (*suspend_again)(void);
	void (*end)(void);
	void (*recover)(void);
};

struct platform_s2idle_ops {
	int (*begin)(void);
	int (*prepare)(void);
	int (*prepare_late)(void);
	void (*check)(void);
	bool (*wake)(void);
	void (*restore_early)(void);
	void (*restore)(void);
	void (*end)(void);
};

The callbacks bracket the device phases symmetrically. ->begin() runs before any device is touched and tells the platform the target state; ->prepare() runs after the main device suspend phase, ->prepare_late() after the noirq phase; ->enter() does the actual sleep; and ->wake(), ->finish(), and ->end() mirror them on the way back. ->suspend_again() lets a platform that woke only to service a periodic event (a battery-charger check, for example) go straight back to sleep without resuming devices, and ->recover() is called when a device fails to suspend. struct platform_s2idle_ops is the lighter s2idle counterpart; its ->check() and ->wake() drive the spurious-wakeup loop described above.

The ARM64 implementation, registered by the PSCI driver in drivers/firmware/psci/psci.c when firmware advertises PSCI 1.0's SYSTEM_SUSPEND function, is as small as an implementation can be: 

/* drivers/firmware/psci/psci.c */
static int psci_system_suspend(unsigned long unused)
{
	int err;
	phys_addr_t pa_cpu_resume = __pa_symbol(cpu_resume);

	err = invoke_psci_fn(PSCI_FN_NATIVE(1_0, SYSTEM_SUSPEND),
			      pa_cpu_resume, 0, 0);
	return psci_to_linux_errno(err);
}

static int psci_system_suspend_enter(suspend_state_t state)
{
	return cpu_suspend(0, psci_system_suspend);
}

static const struct platform_suspend_ops psci_suspend_ops = {
	.valid          = suspend_valid_only_mem,
	.enter          = psci_system_suspend_enter,
};

suspend_valid_only_mem() accepts only PM_SUSPEND_MEM, so mem_sleep on such a system offers s2idle and deep. The work happens in cpu_suspend() (arch/arm64/kernel/suspend.c), which is shared with the idle path: it masks exceptions with local_daif_save(), saves the CPU's system registers and callee-saved registers on the stack in __cpu_suspend_enter(), and calls the finisher. The finisher issues the SYSTEM_SUSPEND SMC with the physical address of cpu_resume as the entry point. If firmware succeeds, the call never returns; when the system wakes, the boot CPU starts executing at cpu_resume with the MMU off, restores the saved state, and returns from __cpu_suspend_enter() a second time with a zero result, which cpu_suspend() recognizes as a resume and completes through __cpu_suspend_exit(). This “returns twice” pattern, the same as setjmp(), recurs in hibernation (§4.1).

The x86 implementation is ACPI's. acpi_suspend_ops in drivers/acpi/sleep.c maps PM_SUSPEND_STANDBY and PM_SUSPEND_MEM to ACPI S1 and S3 and accepts each only if the firmware provides the corresponding \_S1 or \_S3 object. Its ->begin() records the target state; ->prepare_late(), acpi_pm_prepare(), installs the physical address of a real-mode trampoline as the firmware waking vector with acpi_set_waking_vector(), enables the wakeup devices, evaluates the AML _PTS (“prepare to sleep”) method, and saves the ACPI NVS memory region. Its ->enter(), acpi_suspend_enter(), calls through the acpi_suspend_lowlevel pointer to x86_acpi_suspend_lowlevel(), which saves processor state and ends in do_suspend_lowlevel (arch/x86/kernel/acpi/wakeup_64.S); that routine saves the registers and calls x86_acpi_enter_sleep_state(), which writes the sleep type into the ACPI PM1 control registers. On wakeup the firmware jumps to the trampoline in real mode, which re-enters long mode and resumes at wakeup_long64, restoring the saved context.

That resume path reuses CPU bring-up code. x86_acpi_suspend_lowlevel() (arch/x86/kernel/acpi/sleep.c) records CR0, CR4, and IA32_MISC_ENABLE in the header of the real-mode blob and points initial_code at wakeup_long64. The real-mode wakeup code in arch/x86/realmode/rm/wakeup_asm.S then jumps to trampoline_start, the same trampoline that starts application processors at boot, and the CPU arrives at wakeup_long64 as if it were a secondary CPU coming online. Firmware has rerun part of its power-on initialization by then, including memory-controller training, which is much of why S3 resume takes about a second. It has also lost whatever state it does not own. That is why restore_processor_state() (arch/x86/power/cpu.c) does more than reload segment registers and the GDT, IDT, and TSS. It also reapplies the boot CPU's MTRR and PAT setup, checks the TSC_ADJUST MSR, reloads the microcode update with microcode_bsp_resume(), and only then restores the MSRs that quirk tables mark as lost across S3, because microcode emulates some of them.

On LPS0-capable laptops, acpi_s2idle_ops_lps0 in drivers/acpi/x86/s2idle.c plays the s2idle role. The LPS0 device exposes up to three _DSM interfaces, each identified by a UUID: the original Intel one, Microsoft's, and AMD's. Each numbers its functions differently. On entry, ->prepare_late() calls “screen off” (Microsoft function 3), “Modern Standby entry” (7), and “LPS0 entry” (5) on whichever interfaces the firmware implements. On exit, ->restore_early() calls “LPS0 exit” (6), “turn on display” (9), “Modern Standby exit” (8), and “screen on” (4). Function 9, which Windows 11 22H2 introduced, was added in 7.0 because some laptops' embedded controllers wait for it before turning fans and keyboard backlights back on. Function 1 returns the platform's list of device constraints, the minimum D-state each device must reach for the platform to enter its lowest-power state. Linux checks the list and logs the devices that miss their constraint only when the acpi_x86.check_lps0_constraints parameter is set; it was made optional in 6.19 because evaluating function 1 itself upset some firmware. Platform drivers can join the sequence with acpi_register_lps0_dev(), whose ->prepare(), ->check(), and ->restore() hooks run inside the s2idle loop. amd_pmc uses them to send its “enter S0ix” message and read the hardware sleep time afterwards (§3.5), and the AMD GPIO driver uses them to log which GPIO pins are still active. Booting with acpi_x86.sleep_no_lps0=1 skips the whole LPS0 sequence, which is useful for finding out whether firmware is to blame for a failed resume.

Power-Off and Reboot

At the far end of the range of sleep states is the one that is not a sleep state at all: switching the machine off (ACPI S5, “soft off”) or restarting it. User space asks for both through the reboot() system call in kernel/reboot.c. The caller needs CAP_SYS_BOOT in the user namespace that owns its PID namespace, and as a guard against stray calls it must pass LINUX_REBOOT_MAGIC1 (0xfee1dead) together with one of four second magic numbers. The cmd argument selects the action: LINUX_REBOOT_CMD_RESTART, HALT, POWER_OFF, RESTART2 (restart with a command string that the platform may interpret, for example to boot into a recovery mode), CAD_ON and CAD_OFF (what Ctrl-Alt-Del does), SW_SUSPEND (which calls hibernate(), §4.1), and KEXEC (which boots a preloaded kernel, Appendix A, §8.2). The system call does not sync filesystems: init systems such as systemd stop services, unmount or remount filesystems read-only, and sync before they call it. If no power-off method is registered, POWER_OFF quietly becomes HALT. A caller inside a child PID namespace never reaches the hardware. reboot_pid_ns() sends SIGKILL to the namespace's init and records SIGHUP (for a restart) or SIGINT (for halt or power-off) as the namespace's exit signal, so a container manager can tell which was requested (Chapter 25, §4.1).

The three real transitions, kernel_power_off(), kernel_restart(), and kernel_halt(), share one shape: 

/* kernel/reboot.c */
static void kernel_shutdown_prepare(enum system_states state)
{
	blocking_notifier_call_chain(&reboot_notifier_list,
		(state == SYSTEM_HALT) ? SYS_HALT : SYS_POWER_OFF, NULL);
	system_state = state;
	usermodehelper_disable();
	device_shutdown();
}

void kernel_power_off(void)
{
	kernel_shutdown_prepare(SYSTEM_POWER_OFF);
	do_kernel_power_off_prepare();
	migrate_to_reboot_cpu();
	syscore_shutdown();
	pr_emerg("Power down\n");
	pr_flush(1000, true);
	kmsg_dump(KMSG_DUMP_SHUTDOWN);
	machine_power_off();
}

The reboot notifier chain (register_reboot_notifier(); notifier chains are described in Chapter 3, §7.7) lets subsystems such as watchdogs and MD RAID act while the system is still fully working. Then system_state changes, so code that checks it can tell a shutdown from normal running. The usermode helper is disabled, so the kernel will start no new user processes. Then the device model is walked. device_shutdown() in drivers/base/core.c waits for in-flight probes and blocks new ones, stops cpufreq, and then takes devices off the tail of devices_kset, the same list that system suspend walks (§3.2), so that children and consumers are shut down before their parents and suppliers. For each device it takes a runtime PM reference with pm_runtime_get_noresume() and calls pm_runtime_barrier(), so that runtime PM (§5) cannot touch the device again. It then calls the class's ->shutdown_pre(), and then the bus's ->shutdown() or, if the bus has none, the driver's (Chapter 13, §3.2). Unlike the suspend path, this is a single serial pass with no phases, no asynchronous execution, and no way back. A driver's ->shutdown() has one job: quiesce the hardware by stopping DMA, masking interrupts, and parking any mechanics, so that firmware, or the next kernel after a kexec, finds the device in a sane state.

The last steps run the power-off “prepare” handlers (described below). migrate_to_reboot_cpu() then disables CPU hotplug and pins the task to reboot_cpu (CPU 0 by default). syscore_shutdown() runs the ->shutdown() hooks that core code registered with register_syscore() (since 6.19 each struct syscore carries a data pointer passed to its syscore_ops) with only one CPU left that matters. kmsg_dump() lets pstore save the end of the log (Chapter 29, §3.4), and machine_power_off() passes control to the architecture. On ARM64 that function disables interrupts, stops the other CPUs with smp_send_stop(), and calls do_kernel_power_off(). On x86_64, native_machine_power_off() first runs machine_shutdown(), which quiets the I/O APIC, stops the other CPUs, disables the local APIC and the HPET, and shuts down the IOMMU, unless reboot=force was given. It then calls the same do_kernel_power_off().

do_kernel_power_off() runs the sys-off handler chain introduced in 5.19 to replace the single global pm_power_off function pointer, which several drivers on one board used to overwrite. register_sys_off_handler(mode, priority, callback, data) takes one of four modes. SYS_OFF_MODE_POWER_OFF_PREPARE and SYS_OFF_MODE_RESTART_PREPARE go on blocking notifier chains whose handlers may sleep. SYS_OFF_MODE_POWER_OFF and SYS_OFF_MODE_RESTART go on atomic chains whose handlers must not sleep and normally do not return. Handlers run from the highest priority down, from SYS_OFF_PRIO_FIRMWARE (224) through SYS_OFF_PRIO_HIGH and SYS_OFF_PRIO_DEFAULT (0) to SYS_OFF_PRIO_LOW and SYS_OFF_PRIO_PLATFORM (−256). Any number of handlers may share the default priority, but only one may hold each of the other priorities. A board's PMIC driver typically uses the managed forms devm_register_power_off_handler() and devm_register_restart_handler(). For compatibility, do_kernel_power_off() temporarily wraps a legacy pm_power_off as a default-priority handler, and kernel_can_power_off(), which is what the POWER_OFF-to-HALT fallback tests, reports whether either kind of handler exists.

The firmware handlers sit at the top of this chain. On ACPI systems that provide an \_S5 object, acpi_sleep_init() registers acpi_power_off_prepare(), which evaluates _PTS for S5 and disables the GPEs, and acpi_power_off(), which writes the S5 sleep type to the PM1 control registers. Both are registered at firmware priority. The prepare handler is also registered for restart, because some BIOSes expect to see the S5 preparation that Windows does before a reboot. On ARM64, drivers/firmware/psci/psci.c still sets the legacy pm_power_off to psci_sys_poweroff(), which issues SYSTEM_OFF. When the system is hibernating and firmware implements PSCI 1.3, a handler at SYS_OFF_PRIO_FIRMWARE + 2 issues SYSTEM_OFF2 instead (§4.1).

Restart follows the same pattern through kernel_restart(cmd), which calls the reboot notifiers with SYS_RESTART, device_shutdown(), the restart-prepare handlers, migrate_to_reboot_cpu(), and syscore_shutdown() before machine_restart(cmd). The two architectures diverge in the last step. ARM64 stops the other CPUs, calls the UEFI ResetSystem() runtime service through efi_reboot() if EFI runtime services are available (required for capsule firmware updates), and otherwise runs the restart-handler chain with do_kernel_restart(). There PSCI's psci_sys_reset() (priority 129) issues SYSTEM_RESET, or the PSCI 1.1 SYSTEM_RESET2 warm reset when reboot_mode asks for a warm or soft reboot, and SoC watchdog or PMIC drivers can register competing methods. If every handler returns, the kernel prints “Reboot failed -- System halted” and spins. x86_64 does not use the restart-handler chain. native_machine_emergency_restart() loops through reset methods selected by reboot_type: the ACPI reset register (the default), the keyboard controller's reset line (port 0x64), EFI ResetSystem(), a jump back into the BIOS, the PCI reset register at port 0xCF9, and finally a deliberate triple fault. Each method that fails hands over to the next. The reboot= boot parameter, DMI quirks for known boards, and the files /sys/kernel/reboot/mode, type, cpu, and force (added in 5.11) choose the method, warm or cold mode, and reboot CPU.

On ARM64 and other device-tree platforms, board-level power-off and restart methods live in drivers/power/reset/. Each is a small platform driver that registers a sys-off or restart handler. gpio-poweroff toggles a GPIO line, syscon-reboot writes a value into a system-controller register (at restart priority 192 unless the device tree says otherwise), and PMIC drivers cut their own regulators. The same directory implements reboot modes, which pass the RESTART2 command string to the bootloader. A reboot-mode device-tree node lists properties such as mode-recovery and mode-bootloader, each with a magic number. reboot_mode_notify() in drivers/power/reset/reboot-mode.c sits on the reboot notifier chain, maps the command string (with spaces, commas, and slashes turned into hyphens) to its magic number, and has the backend driver store it somewhere that survives a warm reset. syscon-reboot-mode uses a register, nvmem-reboot-mode an NVMEM cell, and Qualcomm's qcom-pon a PMIC register. This is how adb reboot recovery on an Android device reaches recovery mode. On UEFI machines the restart command is not passed to firmware, and the same job is done by writing UEFI variables, such as systemd's LoaderEntryOneShot, before rebooting.

Not every shutdown starts in user space. emergency_restart() skips the notifiers and device shutdown entirely. It is safe in interrupt context, and panic() and SysRq-B use it (Chapter 29, §3.3). The keyboard's Ctrl-Alt-Del calls ctrl_alt_del(), which either restarts from a workqueue (if CAD_ON was set) or sends SIGINT to init, identified by cad_pid, so that user space can decide. When kernel code needs the machine down but wants user space to shut down cleanly, it calls orderly_poweroff() or orderly_reboot(). These run /sbin/poweroff (configurable as the kernel.poweroff_cmd sysctl) or /sbin/reboot through the usermode helper, and fall back to emergency_sync() and a direct kernel_power_off() or kernel_restart() if the helper cannot start. The most urgent caller is __hw_protection_trigger(), which thermal critical trips (§9.2), regulator failures, and similar hardware-protection events use. It prints “HARDWARE PROTECTION”, acts only on the first request, and schedules a delayed work item that forces kernel_power_off() or kernel_restart(), followed by emergency_restart() as a last resort, in case the orderly path stalls. Whether protection means power-off or reboot is set by the hw_protection= boot parameter or, since 6.15, /sys/kernel/reboot/hw_protection. Hibernation's power_down() (§4.1) also ends here, in kernel_power_off(), kernel_restart(), or kernel_halt() depending on /sys/power/disk.

The Suspend Sequence

Putting a running system to sleep is mostly a matter of ordering. Tasks must stop before the devices they use; children must suspend before their parents and consumers before their suppliers; device interrupts must be quiesced before CPUs are taken away; and every step must be undoable in reverse if anything fails or a wakeup event arrives halfway through. The code in kernel/power/suspend.c and drivers/base/power/main.c is built around a ladder of such steps, each with a matching step on the way back. The ladder runs from notifiers that warn subsystems while user space still runs down to the last core hooks, which run on one CPU with interrupts off. Two mechanisms make the descent safe: the freezer, which parks tasks, and wakeup sources, which let a late event abort the descent or end the sleep.

From pm_suspend() to the Platform

pm_suspend(), the exported entry point called by state_store() and by autosleep, range-checks its argument, logs “suspend entry”, and calls enter_state(), which does the generic setup and teardown around the real work: 

/* kernel/power/suspend.c (abridged) */
static int enter_state(suspend_state_t state)
{
	int error;

	if (state == PM_SUSPEND_TO_IDLE) {
		/* only the freezer, devices and platform pm_test levels apply */
	} else if (!valid_state(state)) {
		return -EINVAL;
	}
	if (!mutex_trylock(&system_transition_mutex))
		return -EBUSY;

	if (state == PM_SUSPEND_TO_IDLE)
		s2idle_begin();

	if (sync_on_suspend_enabled) {
		error = pm_sleep_fs_sync();
		if (error)
			goto Unlock;
	}

	pm_suspend_clear_flags();
	error = suspend_prepare(state);
	if (error)
		goto Unlock;

	if (suspend_test(TEST_FREEZER))
		goto Finish;

	error = suspend_devices_and_enter(state);

 Finish:
	events_check_enabled = false;
	suspend_finish();
 Unlock:
	mutex_unlock(&system_transition_mutex);
	return error;
}

system_transition_mutex serializes every system-wide transition— suspend, hibernation, the user-space snapshot device, and reboot—and mutex_trylock() makes a second concurrent request fail fast with -EBUSY rather than queue behind the first. Filesystems are then synced, because a machine that never wakes should at least not lose dirty data; since 6.19 the sync runs through pm_sleep_fs_sync(), which can be aborted by a wakeup event instead of blocking suspend indefinitely on a slow device. suspend_prepare() calls the PM notifier chain with PM_SUSPEND_PREPARE—the hook through which subsystems prepare while user space is still running; the firmware loader, for example, uses it to cache the firmware images drivers will need to reload on resume, when user space cannot yet supply them—optionally freezes filesystems (a 6.16 option controlled by /sys/power/freeze_filesystems), and freezes tasks (§3.3). suspend_finish() is its exact inverse: thaw tasks, thaw filesystems, and send PM_POST_SUSPEND.

The PM notifier chain is a blocking notifier (Chapter 3, §7.7) in kernel/power/main.c. Code joins it with register_pm_notifier(), or with the pm_notifier(fn, pri) macro for a static block, and receives one of six events: PM_SUSPEND_PREPARE and PM_POST_SUSPEND around suspend; PM_HIBERNATION_PREPARE and PM_POST_HIBERNATION around hibernation; and PM_RESTORE_PREPARE and PM_POST_RESTORE around loading a hibernation image (§4.3). The prepare event is sent through pm_notifier_call_chain_robust(). If one callback fails, the chain stops, every callback that already accepted the prepare event receives the matching post event, and the transition is abandoned. Callbacks run in process context while every task can still run, so they may sleep, allocate memory, and wait for user space. The users show what this early, global hook is for. The random-number driver mixes timestamps into its input pool and reseeds the CRNG after resume, because a resumed machine (or a restored image) must not keep generating the same stream it would have produced before. WireGuard wipes its session keys on PM_SUSPEND_PREPARE and PM_HIBERNATION_PREPARE, so that keys do not survive in a sleeping machine's memory or in a hibernation image. Both skip that work when the kernel is built with CONFIG_PM_AUTOSLEEP or CONFIG_PM_USERSPACE_AUTOSLEEP (§3.4), where suspends are frequent and short. amdgpu notes that a hibernation has begun, because its later device callbacks must save VRAM rather than simply power the GPU down. Before the notifiers run, pm_prepare_console() switches the foreground virtual terminal to a dedicated console (VT 63), so that the graphics state of the user's session is not disturbed by kernel messages. The switch is skipped when every registered display driver has said it can restore the screen itself, as fbdev drivers do through pm_vt_switch_required(). Code that must not run concurrently with a transition takes system_transition_mutex through lock_system_sleep(), which also sets PF_NOFREEZE on the caller so that the freezer does not wait forever for a task that is holding the lock.

suspend_devices_and_enter() does the device work. It records the target state in pm_suspend_target_state (§2.2), calls the platform's ->begin(), and suspends the consoles with console_suspend_all() (named suspend_console() before 6.15) so that printk() output stops reaching hardware that is about to lose power; messages are buffered and printed after resume. It then runs dpm_suspend_start(PMSG_SUSPEND), which runs the ->prepare() and ->suspend() callbacks of every device, and does two pieces of global bookkeeping between them. dpm_prepare() first waits for probes already in progress and then blocks new ones with device_block_probing(). A device that appears in the middle of the transition has its probe deferred until dpm_complete(), instead of binding a driver to a bus that is half suspended. Once every ->prepare() has run, pm_restrict_gfp_mask() clears __GFP_IO and __GFP_FS from the global gfp_allowed_mask (Chapter 8, §1.5). From then on, any allocation that would have to reclaim memory by writing pages out behaves as if it had been made with GFP_NOIO, because the disk it would write to may already be suspended. The restriction is lifted by dpm_resume_end() once the main resume phase has finished. Before 6.16 it was applied before ->prepare(). It was moved after it because GPU drivers use ->prepare() to evict video memory into system RAM, and on a machine under memory pressure that eviction has to be able to push other pages to swap. Since 6.18 the restriction nests, so that hibernation can hold it from the snapshot through the image write. If any device fails to suspend, the platform's ->recover() runs before devices are resumed. It then calls suspend_enter() in a loop that repeats only if the platform's ->suspend_again() asks for it, and afterwards resumes devices with dpm_resume_end(PMSG_RESUME). suspend_enter() is where the descent reaches the bottom, and its structure—a straight run of steps followed by a run of labels that undo them in reverse—is the clearest picture of the whole design: 

/* kernel/power/suspend.c (abridged) */
static int suspend_enter(suspend_state_t state, bool *wakeup)
{
	int error;

	error = platform_suspend_prepare(state);
	if (error)
		goto Platform_finish;

	error = dpm_suspend_late(PMSG_SUSPEND);
	if (error)
		goto Platform_finish;
	error = platform_suspend_prepare_late(state);
	if (error)
		goto Devices_early_resume;

	error = dpm_suspend_noirq(PMSG_SUSPEND);
	if (error)
		goto Platform_early_resume;
	error = platform_suspend_prepare_noirq(state);
	if (error)
		goto Platform_wake;

	if (suspend_test(TEST_PLATFORM))
		goto Platform_wake;

	if (state == PM_SUSPEND_TO_IDLE) {
		s2idle_loop();
		goto Platform_wake;
	}

	error = pm_sleep_disable_secondary_cpus();
	if (error || suspend_test(TEST_CPUS))
		goto Enable_cpus;

	arch_suspend_disable_irqs();
	system_state = SYSTEM_SUSPEND;

	error = syscore_suspend();
	if (!error) {
		*wakeup = pm_wakeup_pending();
		if (!(suspend_test(TEST_CORE) || *wakeup)) {
			error = suspend_ops->enter(state);
		} else if (*wakeup) {
			error = -EBUSY;
		}
		syscore_resume();
	}

	system_state = SYSTEM_RUNNING;
	arch_suspend_enable_irqs();

 Enable_cpus:
	pm_sleep_enable_secondary_cpus();

 Platform_wake:
	platform_resume_noirq(state);
	dpm_resume_noirq(PMSG_RESUME);

 Platform_early_resume:
	platform_resume_early(state);

 Devices_early_resume:
	dpm_resume_early(PMSG_RESUME);

 Platform_finish:
	platform_resume_finish(state);
	return error;
}

The platform_* helpers call the matching platform_suspend_ops or platform_s2idle_ops callback, depending on the state. The order of the steps is the point. Device callbacks run in three passes (late and noirq are described in §3.2) so that drivers can finish work that needs interrupts before interrupts are taken away. Suspend-to-idle stops after the noirq pass and enters s2idle_loop(), never touching CPU hotplug; every other state takes the non-boot CPUs offline with pm_sleep_disable_secondary_cpus(), which pauses cpuidle and runs the ordinary CPU-hotplug teardown of Chapter 6, §4.6 for each CPU except CPU 0. With one CPU left and its interrupts disabled, syscore operations suspend the core devices that have no struct device and must be handled with interrupts off: the timekeeping core, sched_clock, interrupt controllers, and on x86 the machine-check, MTRR, and microcode state. A final pm_wakeup_pending() check catches any wakeup event that arrived during all of that, and only then does suspend_ops->enter() hand the machine to firmware. When it returns, the system is awake again, and execution simply falls through the labels. Figure 21-3 shows the whole ladder.

The syscore rung deserves a closer look, because it is where the two architectures differ most. Its hooks are kept on one list in drivers/base/syscore.c. syscore_suspend() first checks pm_wakeup_pending() once more, then calls the ->suspend() hooks in reverse registration order, warning if any of them re-enables interrupts. If one fails, the hooks that already ran are resumed and the error unwinds the ladder. syscore_resume() walks the list forward. The same list supplies the ->shutdown() hooks used at power-off (§2.4). On x86 the list holds the local APIC, I/O APIC, and legacy i8259 state; the machine-check, MTRR, and microcode hooks; the frequency-invariance counters; and the KVM and Hyper-V guest hooks. Generic entries include timekeeping, which records how long the system slept so that CLOCK_MONOTONIC can stop while CLOCK_BOOTTIME advances (Chapter 6, §3.7), and sched_clock. A small entry in kernel/irq/pm.c re-enables the interrupt lines requested with IRQF_EARLY_RESUME during syscore_resume(), before any device is resumed. On ARM64 the GICv3 ITS uses a syscore hook to save its table configuration, but most per-CPU state is handled by one generic entry in kernel/cpu_pm.c. Its suspend hook calls cpu_pm_enter() and cpu_cluster_pm_enter(), and its resume hook calls the matching exit functions. Those run the same CPU PM notifiers that cpuidle uses before a deep idle state (§8.3): the GICv3 CPU interface, the architected timer, the PMU, FPSIMD and SVE state, SDEI, and KVM's per-CPU hypervisor state. The boot CPU going into SYSTEM_SUSPEND is treated like a CPU going into a very deep idle state, with the cluster notifiers added on top. The secondary CPUs do not need this, because CPU hotplug has already torn their state down.

Device Callbacks and the Four Phases

Every device participates in system sleep through a struct dev_pm_ops, the 23-callback table that also carries the runtime PM callbacks of §5. It was introduced in 2.6.29 to replace the single suspend(dev, state) method that buses and drivers had used since the 2.6 device model, and whose one-size-fits-all semantics had made it impossible to distinguish between a device being suspended for a sleep state and one being quiesced for a hibernation snapshot:

The table is two-dimensional. One axis is the transition, identified by the pm_message_t passed down the ladder: PMSG_SUSPEND and PMSG_RESUME for suspend; PMSG_FREEZE and PMSG_THAW for the hibernation snapshot, where the device must stop DMA and keep its state but need not lose power; PMSG_HIBERNATE for entering the platform's hibernation state after the image is written, and PMSG_RESTORE for resuming in the image kernel after it is loaded. The other axis is the phase, and there are four: a prepare phase that may still allocate memory and register children, the main phase where most drivers do their work, a late phase that runs after runtime PM has been disabled for every device, and a noirq phase that runs after suspend_device_irqs() has disabled every device interrupt handler that was not registered with IRQF_NO_SUSPEND (see Chapter 4, §8.2 for the IRQ flags, and its enable_irq_wake() interface that keeps a wakeup line armed). Resume runs the phases in the opposite order, and ->complete() closes the prepare phase. Table 21-3 shows which callback runs where.

The PM domain wins over everything because it represents the power rail the device actually sits on (§6); the bus type comes last before the driver because buses such as PCI and USB wrap driver callbacks with their own work—saving PCI configuration space, choosing a D-state, arming PME—and call the driver's callback themselves. The driver's own dev_pm_ops, the one most driver writers fill in with DEFINE_SIMPLE_DEV_PM_OPS() or DEFINE_RUNTIME_DEV_PM_OPS() and reference through pm_sleep_ptr() or pm_ptr(), is used directly only when no outer layer claims the phase. Chapter 13 shows where these pointers live in struct bus_type and struct device_driver (Chapter 13, §3).

Ordering comes from lists. Every registered device is on dpm_list in registration order, which guarantees that a parent precedes its children. dpm_prepare() walks it forward, moving each device to dpm_prepared_list; dpm_suspend() walks that list backward, so children suspend before their parents, moving each device to dpm_suspended_list; the late and noirq phases continue onto dpm_late_early_list and dpm_noirq_list. Resume walks the lists forward again. Device links (§5.2) add supplier–consumer edges that the registration order alone may not respect, and device_link_add() reorders dpm_list when necessary.

The core does more around each callback than calling it. In device_prepare() it takes a runtime PM reference with pm_runtime_get_noresume(). From then until ->complete() no device can be runtime-suspended, so a parent cannot power down under a child that the system path is still working on. In device_suspend() it waits for the device's children and consumers, then calls pm_runtime_barrier() to let any runtime PM transition already in flight finish (and to carry out a pending runtime resume), and then checks pm_wakeup_pending(). A wakeup event at this point aborts the whole transition with -EBUSY, so a keypress during a slow suspend undoes the suspend instead of being lost. After a successful callback, a device that is allowed to wake the system (§3.4) is marked as being on the wakeup path, and the mark is propagated to its parent. Bus and domain code uses that mark to keep everything above a wakeup device powered. For example, genpd leaves a power domain flagged GENPD_FLAG_ACTIVE_WAKEUP on while it contains a device on the wakeup path (§6.2). The exception, since 6.19, is a device marked with device_set_out_band_wakeup(), whose wakeup logic sits in an always-on domain, as with some i.MX USB controllers and the TI OMAP 8250 UART. Finally, dpm_suspend_noirq() arms wake interrupts before it calls suspend_device_irqs(). A driver that registered its interrupt with dev_pm_set_wake_irq(), or a separate wake-only line with dev_pm_set_dedicated_wake_irq(), gets enable_irq_wake() on that line automatically if the device may wake the system, so the driver does not have to make the call in its own ->suspend().

Strictly sequential callbacks would make suspend as slow as the sum of every device's latency, so a driver can mark its device with device_enable_async_suspend(), and when /sys/power/pm_async is 1 (the default) such devices run their callbacks in async threads. Correctness then depends on explicit waiting: before suspending a device, dpm_wait_for_subordinate() waits for its children and consumers; before resuming one, dpm_wait_for_superior() waits for its parent and suppliers. The scheduling of async work was reworked in 6.16 and 6.17 so that the core starts an async device's work as soon as its last dependency finishes—leaf devices first on suspend, a parent's children as soon as it resumes (dpm_async_resume_children(), dpm_async_suspend_superior()) —instead of queueing every async device up front and letting most of the threads sleep. Since 6.17 the suspend side treats suppliers like parents and the resume side treats consumers like children, so device links take part in this scheduling as well. Since 6.18 links created with DL_FLAG_SYNC_STATE_ONLY are ignored, because they exist only to delay a provider's sync_state() and say nothing about suspend order. Because a missing link or a wrong parent shows up only as an intermittent async ordering bug, 6.17 also added the pm_async=off boot parameter. It turns asynchronous suspend off before the first transition, when writing 0 to /sys/power/pm_async is not an option.

Two optimizations let the core skip work. If a device's ->prepare() returns a positive number (or it has no PM callbacks at all) and the device is already runtime-suspended, the core sets power.direct_complete and leaves it alone for the whole transition, calling only ->complete() at the end; a device that may wake the system, or whose children may, is never left in this state. The driver flags DPM_FLAG_SMART_SUSPEND and DPM_FLAG_MAY_SKIP_RESUME, set with dev_pm_set_driver_flags(), extend the idea to the late and noirq phases for buses that support them, allowing a runtime-suspended device to stay suspended across a whole sleep cycle without its driver being involved at all.

The Freezer

Before devices are touched, tasks must stop issuing I/O to them. Linux uses the freezer for this: every user-space task, and every kernel thread that has opted in, is driven into a special sleeping state from which it cannot run until the system thaws. The alternative—having every driver cope with requests arriving while it is half-suspended—was judged unmanageable, and the freezer has survived several attempts to remove it. Chapter 3 covers the task-state side (Chapter 3, “Special States”); here the concern is the mechanism.

freeze_processes() in kernel/power/process.c disables the usermode helper, marks the current task with PF_SUSPEND_TASK so it is not frozen itself, enables the freezer_active static key, sets pm_freezing, and calls try_to_freeze_tasks(true), which loops over every thread calling freeze_task() and sleeping with exponential backoff (1 ms growing to 8 ms) until no unfrozen user task remains, a wakeup event is pending, or freeze_timeout_msecs (20 seconds, adjustable through /sys/power/pm_freeze_timeout) expires. It then disables the OOM killer, since a frozen task could never exit to release memory. freeze_kernel_threads() repeats the exercise with pm_nosig_freezing set, which also catches kernel threads that called set_freezable(), and freezes freezable workqueues, those created with WQ_FREEZABLE such as system_freezable_wq, which stop starting new work items and are waited on until the ones in flight finish. In 7.0 the runtime PM workqueue pm_wq stopped being one of them. Freezing it meant that an asynchronous runtime resume requested during a transition would never run, and the block layer could deadlock waiting for one.

freeze_task() works in two ways. If the task is already sleeping in a state that includes TASK_FREEZABLE, or is stopped or traced, __set_task_frozen() saves its state in p->saved_state and replaces it with TASK_FROZEN directly—the task is frozen without ever running. Otherwise the freezer sends a fake signal wakeup (or, for a kernel thread, a plain wakeup), and the task freezes itself the next time it passes a freezing point: the signal-delivery path for user tasks, or an explicit try_to_freeze() call in a kernel thread's loop. Both paths end in __refrigerator(): 

/* kernel/freezer.c (abridged) */
bool __refrigerator(bool check_kthr_stop)
{
	unsigned int state = get_current_state();
	bool was_frozen = false;

	WARN_ON_ONCE(state && !(state & TASK_NORMAL));

	for (;;) {
		bool freeze;

		raw_spin_lock_irq(&current->pi_lock);
		WRITE_ONCE(current->__state, TASK_FROZEN);
		/* unstale saved_state so that __thaw_task() will wake us up */
		current->saved_state = TASK_RUNNING;
		raw_spin_unlock_irq(&current->pi_lock);

		spin_lock_irq(&freezer_lock);
		freeze = freezing(current) && !(check_kthr_stop && kthread_should_stop());
		spin_unlock_irq(&freezer_lock);

		if (!freeze)
			break;

		was_frozen = true;
		schedule();
	}
	__set_current_state(TASK_RUNNING);

	return was_frozen;
}

This is the design introduced by the 6.1 freezer rewrite. Before it, a frozen task was marked by a PF_FROZEN flag and the freezer had to cooperate with every sleeping primitive through freezer_do_not_count() and freezable_schedule() wrappers. Making TASK_FROZEN a real task state that only __thaw_task() wakes (via wake_up_state(p, TASK_FROZEN), or by restoring saved_state for tasks frozen in place) removed those wrappers and let any TASK_INTERRUPTIBLE or TASK_KILLABLE sleep be marked freezable simply by OR-ing in TASK_FREEZABLE. Tasks with PF_NOFREEZE, which includes kernel threads that never call set_freezable(), are skipped by freezing(), as are OOM victims. The freezer_active static key keeps the cost of freezing() checks to a patched-out branch when no freeze is in progress. The same machinery serves the cgroup v1 freezer; the cgroup v2 freezer of Chapter 25 uses a separate, signal-based mechanism.

Freezing fails when some task cannot reach a freezing point within the timeout. The freezer then reports “Freezing user space processes failed after N seconds (M tasks refusing to freeze, wq_busy=0)”, dumps the stack of every task that is still not frozen with sched_show_task(), and thaws everything. If a wakeup event cut the attempt short, the message says “aborted” instead, and the task dump is printed only when pm_debug_messages is on. The usual culprit sits in TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE or TASK_KILLABLE without TASK_FREEZABLE, waiting for something that will not happen while the rest of the system is frozen. For example, a process may be blocked on a FUSE request whose user-space daemon was frozen first. The fake signal does not wake such a sleep, so the task never reaches a freezing point. Simply marking more sleeps freezable is not a safe fix, because a task frozen while holding a lock can block work that suspend still needs. __set_task_frozen() therefore warns under lockdep when a task would be frozen with locks held. NFS and SUNRPC use the lockdep-silent TASK_FREEZABLE_UNSAFE for a few known-safe waits on the server, and the kernel header marks it as not to be used anywhere new.

Freezing tasks stops new system calls, but it does not put filesystems into a defined state. Some filesystems also need to know that the machine slept. Since 6.16, writing 1 to /sys/power/freeze_filesystems makes suspend_prepare() and hibernation call filesystems_freeze() before the tasks are frozen. This walks the superblocks in reverse order, so a filesystem stacked on top of another is frozen first, and calls freeze_super() on each one. That is the same operation as FIFREEZE (Chapter 12, §2.1): it syncs the filesystem, blocks new modifications at the VFS level, and leaves a consistent image on disk. Freezing is best effort, so a filesystem that fails to freeze is skipped rather than failing the suspend. The option is off by default; it is a step towards freezing filesystems explicitly instead of relying on the task freezer to stop filesystem I/O. Filesystems whose type carries FS_POWER_FREEZE are frozen whatever it says. In 7.0 that is only efivarfs (since 6.18), whose thaw callback re-reads every UEFI variable, because firmware or another operating system may have changed them while Linux was asleep.

Wakeup Sources, wakeup_count, and Autosleep

Suspend is a race against the world. A keystroke, a network packet, or a charger being plugged in may arrive at any moment, and if the event is consumed by a driver after user space has decided to suspend but before the system has actually slept, the machine sleeps with an unhandled event and the user sees a frozen laptop. Linux's answer, introduced in 2.6.37 and grown from Android's “wakelocks”, is the wakeup source: an object that a driver holds active for as long as it is processing an event that should prevent suspend.

A driver activates a source with __pm_stay_awake() (or pm_stay_awake(dev) for a device's own source, created by device_init_wakeup()) and releases it with __pm_relax(); for events with no clear end, pm_wakeup_event(dev, msec) activates it with a timeout. All sources feed one atomic variable, combined_event_count, whose upper half counts completed events and whose lower half counts events in progress, so that activation and deactivation are single atomic operations. Every source appears in /sys/class/wakeup/ (since 5.4) and in /sys/kernel/debug/wakeup_sources with its counters, which is where one looks to find out why a phone does not sleep.

Whether a device may wake the system at all is decided in two steps, capability and policy. A driver or bus that knows the hardware can signal a wakeup calls device_set_wakeup_capable(), which adds a power/wakeup attribute to the device's sysfs directory. Enabling wakeup, either by the driver's choice of default through device_init_wakeup(dev, true) or by user space writing enabled to that attribute, registers the device's wakeup source. device_may_wakeup(), the test that bus code and drivers make in their suspend callbacks before arming PME, a GPIO, or a wake interrupt, is true only when both steps have been taken. This is how a distribution keeps a USB mouse from waking a laptop in a bag while still letting the keyboard and lid switch do so. On x86 the older /proc/acpi/wakeup file still exists and toggles the same policy for devices that ACPI lists with a _PRW wake GPE.

The race with user space is closed by /sys/power/wakeup_count. A power manager reads the file, which blocks until no event is in progress and returns the completed-event count; it then processes whatever events it knows about and writes the count back. The write succeeds only if the count has not changed and nothing is in progress, in which case pm_save_wakeup_count() sets events_check_enabled. From then on, pm_wakeup_pending(), which the suspend path calls at every critical point—in the freezer loop, before each device callback, in s2idle_enter(), and after syscore_suspend()—returns true if any new event has been reported: 

/* drivers/base/power/wakeup.c (abridged) */
bool pm_wakeup_pending(void)
{
	unsigned long flags;
	bool ret = false;

	raw_spin_lock_irqsave(&events_lock, flags);
	if (events_check_enabled) {
		unsigned int cnt, inpr;

		split_counters(&cnt, &inpr);
		ret = (cnt != saved_count || inpr > 0);
		events_check_enabled = !ret;
	}
	raw_spin_unlock_irqrestore(&events_lock, flags);

	return ret || atomic_read(&pm_abort_suspend) > 0;
}

The second term covers wakeup interrupts: an interrupt line armed with enable_irq_wake() that fires after suspend_device_irqs() has run calls pm_system_irq_wakeup(), which records the IRQ number (readable afterwards in /sys/power/pm_wakeup_irq) and increments pm_abort_suspend through pm_system_wakeup(), which also wakes an s2idle sleeper.

The two terms behave differently, which matters when no power manager is involved. The event counts are compared only while events_check_enabled is set, and only a successful write to wakeup_count sets it. A plain echo mem > /sys/power/state therefore ignores wakeup sources that are held, and only the abort counter can stop it. Drivers that report events which must always end or abort a sleep use the hard variant, pm_wakeup_hard_event() (or pm_wakeup_ws_event() with hard set). It records the event like any other and also calls pm_system_wakeup(), so the transition is abandoned even without the handshake. The CMOS RTC alarm, the Intel HID and virtual-button power-button drivers, and the Hyper-V synthetic keyboard report their events this way.

Autosleep, added in 3.5 to give Android's opportunistic suspend a mainline home, moves the user-space loop into the kernel. Writing a state to /sys/power/autosleep queues try_to_suspend() on an ordered workqueue; it blocks in pm_get_wakeup_count() until no wakeup source is active, saves the count, calls pm_suspend() (or hibernate()), and requeues itself, pausing half a second if the system woke for no recorded reason. The /sys/power/wake_lock and wake_unlock files (kernel/power/wakelock.c) let user-space processes hold wakeup sources of their own in that mode.

User space can also take part without autosleep. A process that adds a descriptor to epoll with EPOLLWAKEUP gets a wakeup source that is activated when an event becomes ready and released at its next epoll_wait() (Chapter 19, §6.5). An input event that wakes a phone therefore keeps it awake until the program reading the event device has collected it. Both this and the wake_lock files require CAP_BLOCK_SUSPEND, because holding a wakeup source is a way to keep the machine from sleeping. Current Android no longer uses kernel autosleep. Its SystemSuspend service runs the wakeup_count loop in user space and keeps wakelocks in its own process, while drivers still use kernel wakeup sources. CONFIG_PM_USERSPACE_AUTOSLEEP (6.0) lets such a kernel tell the code that cares that suspends are frequent and brief. The random driver then skips its reseed on every resume, and WireGuard keeps its keys (§3.1).

They often happen after the console has been suspended, while only one CPU is running, or during resume before any output device works again, and the usual result is a machine that does not come back. The first tool narrows down where a failure happens. Writing a level to pm_test makes suspend_test() stop at that rung of the ladder in Figure 21-3, wait pm_test_delay seconds (5 by default, settable as suspend.pm_test_delay), and then unwind as if a wakeup had arrived. Since 6.19 the wait checks pm_wakeup_pending() once a second and ends early if a wakeup is pending (§3.4), so a test run can be cut short by a wake interrupt or a hard wakeup event. At the core level, where interrupts are already off, the wait is a busy mdelay() loop. The levels nest. freezer stops right after the tasks are frozen; devices after the main device phase; platform after the late, noirq, and platform prepare steps; processors after the nonboot CPUs have gone offline; and core after syscore_suspend(), one step short of the platform's ->enter(). Suspend-to-idle takes no CPUs offline, so enter_state() refuses the last two levels for it with -EAGAIN. A developer bisects a failure by finding the deepest level that still resumes. The suspend_stats/ directory then records what went wrong in a real attempt: success and failure counts, the failed step (dpm_save_failed_step()), device (dpm_save_failed_dev()), and errno, and, on platforms that report it, how long the hardware actually spent in its low-power state (last_hw_sleep, total_hw_sleep, and max_hw_sleep, added in 6.4 for s2idle residency on AMD and Intel laptops). The debugfs file suspend_stats breaks the failure count down by step and lists the last two failed devices and errnos.

The second set of tools makes the transition talk. Writing 1 to pm_debug_messages, or booting with pm_debug_messages, enables the pm_pr_dbg() messages that the PM core and ACPI emit only while a transition is in progress. pm_print_times, which defaults to the value of initcall_debug, makes the device core log every device callback with the time it took (“calling …” and “… returned 0 after N usecs”), which is usually enough to find the driver that makes a resume slow. Booting with no_console_suspend keeps the console running across suspend, so that messages from the late phases reach a serial port. Booting with pm_async=off (§3.2) runs every device callback in order on one thread. If a failure then goes away, the cause is a missing dependency between two devices rather than a broken driver, and the serial order makes the timing log easier to read. To find out why a system woke up, or would not stay asleep, look at pm_wakeup_irq and the per-source statistics in /sys/kernel/debug/wakeup_sources (§3.4).

For timing, the PM core is instrumented with tracepoints (include/trace/events/power.h). Each rung of the ladder fires suspend_resume at its start and end with an action name such as suspend_enter, freeze_processes, dpm_suspend, or machine_suspend. device_pm_callback_start and device_pm_callback_end bracket every device callback, recording the device, driver, parent, callback name, and result. wakeup_source_activate and wakeup_source_deactivate follow wakeup sources. The kernel tree ships a tool that uses them: sleepgraph, part of pm-graph (tools/power/pm-graph/sleepgraph.py). It enables the tracepoints through ftrace, optionally adds kprobes on further functions (-dev) or a function graph (-f), starts a suspend with an RTC wake alarm (-rtcwake), and turns the trace into an HTML timeline of every phase and every device callback on each CPU. Its companion bootgraph.py does the same for boot.

The last resort is for hangs. With CONFIG_DPM_WATCHDOG, dpm_watchdog_set() arms a timer around each device's suspend and resume callback. If the callback is still running after CONFIG_DPM_WATCHDOG_WARNING_TIMEOUT seconds the kernel prints its stack. After CONFIG_DPM_WATCHDOG_TIMEOUT seconds (120 by default) it panics with “DPM device timeout”, naming the device, and pstore keeps the message for the next boot. The callback is usually stuck waiting for something another CPU is doing. Since 6.19 the dpm_watchdog_all_cpu_backtrace parameter (on the kernel command line as main.dpm_watchdog_all_cpu_backtrace=1, because the code lives in drivers/base/power/main.c) makes the watchdog print a backtrace of every other CPU before it panics. On x86 machines that hang so early in resume that nothing can be printed, CONFIG_PM_TRACE_RTC together with writing 1 to pm_trace makes the device core store a hash of the last device and source line it reached in the CMOS real-time clock. After a hard reset, the boot log reports “hash matches” next to the culprit. The cost is a wrong wall-clock time after every such resume.

Hibernation

Hibernation, also called suspend-to-disk, saves the entire contents of memory to persistent storage and turns the machine off. It draws no power at all while asleep and survives a battery being removed, at the cost of a resume that takes as long as a boot plus the time to read the image back. The idea is old—the swsusp code that Linux 2.6.11 shipped is the ancestor of today's—but the implementation is one of the most delicate in the kernel, because the kernel must take a consistent snapshot of the very memory it is running in, and later a different instance of the kernel must overwrite itself with that snapshot and jump into it.

The code is built only with CONFIG_HIBERNATION, which depends on swap support and on an architecture that provides the image save and restore code (ARCH_HIBERNATION_POSSIBLE: x86, ARM64, RISC-V, PowerPC, LoongArch, and a few others). Laptops are the main users, often in combination with suspend (§2). Virtual machines whose host offers a virtual S4 state are another. A Hyper-V guest can be hibernated by its host, and the Hyper-V balloon driver ignores balloon and memory hot-add requests when hibernation is possible, because an image can only be restored into a machine with the same amount of memory. Some platform features rule hibernation out. On an Intel TDX host the hardware destroys TDX state in S3 and deeper states, so TDX initialization fails unless hibernation has been disabled with nohibernate (Chapter 28, §6.3).

The hibernate() Flow

Writing disk to /sys/power/state calls hibernate() in kernel/power/hibernate.c. It first checks hibernation_available(), which refuses when the command line says nohibernate, when the kernel is locked down (an image could be used to tamper with a running kernel), or when memfd_secret() areas or CXL memory are active, since their contents must never reach a disk. It checks that the configured compressor is available, takes system_transition_mutex through lock_system_sleep(), and claims the hibernation machinery with hibernate_acquire(), which fails if a process has /dev/snapshot open (§4.4). It then runs the PM_HIBERNATION_PREPARE notifiers, syncs filesystems with pm_sleep_fs_sync() (which a wakeup event can abort since 6.19), freezes filesystems if freeze_filesystems is set (§3.3), and freezes user space. lock_device_hotplug() keeps memory and CPUs from coming or going while bitmaps describe them, and create_basic_memory_bitmaps() sets up the forbidden and free page maps of §4.2. Finally it calls hibernation_snapshot(), which preallocates the memory the image will need, freezes kernel threads, and runs the ->prepare() and ->freeze() callbacks of every device. The freeze callbacks are the key difference from suspend: devices must stop DMA and save any state the snapshot needs, but they should not power down, because they will be thawed shortly afterwards to write the image.

The order inside hibernation_snapshot() matters. Memory is preallocated before any device is touched, because making room means reclaiming page cache and swapping out anonymous pages, and that needs working storage. Kernel threads are frozen next, and then dpm_prepare() runs. Some drivers do a lot of work in ->prepare(). A discrete GPU evicts its video memory into system memory there, which on a data-center accelerator can mean many gigabytes of shmem pages that appear after preallocation has finished. Since 6.17, shrink_shmem_memory() calls shrink_all_memory() right after the prepare phase to push those pages out to swap so that the image still fits. Only then are the consoles suspended, the GFP mask restricted (§3.1), and the ->freeze() callbacks run. The restriction stays in force until the image has been written and the machine has powered down or the hibernation has been rolled back.

The snapshot itself is taken by create_image(), whose shape will be familiar from suspend_enter(): 

/* kernel/power/hibernate.c (abridged) */
static int create_image(int platform_mode)
{
	int error;

	error = dpm_suspend_end(PMSG_FREEZE);
	if (error)
		return error;

	error = platform_pre_snapshot(platform_mode);
	if (error || hibernation_test(TEST_PLATFORM))
		goto Platform_finish;

	error = pm_sleep_disable_secondary_cpus();
	if (error || hibernation_test(TEST_CPUS))
		goto Enable_cpus;

	local_irq_disable();
	system_state = SYSTEM_SUSPEND;

	error = syscore_suspend();
	if (error)
		goto Enable_irqs;

	if (hibernation_test(TEST_CORE) || pm_wakeup_pending())
		goto Power_up;

	in_suspend = 1;
	save_processor_state();
	error = swsusp_arch_suspend();
	/* Restore control flow magically appears here */
	restore_processor_state();

	if (!in_suspend) {
		events_check_enabled = false;
		clear_or_poison_free_pages();
	}

	platform_leave(platform_mode);

 Power_up:
	syscore_resume();
 Enable_irqs:
	system_state = SYSTEM_RUNNING;
	local_irq_enable();
 Enable_cpus:
	pm_sleep_enable_secondary_cpus();
 Platform_finish:
	platform_finish(platform_mode);

	dpm_resume_start(in_suspend ?
		(error ? PMSG_RECOVER : PMSG_THAW) : PMSG_RESTORE);

	return error;
}

The comment “Restore control flow magically appears here” is literal. swsusp_arch_suspend() saves the CPU's registers and calls swsusp_save() to copy memory; when it returns, the hibernating kernel continues with in_suspend equal to 1. But the same return also happens, days later, in the restored kernel: the resume code jumps back into the image at the point where swsusp_arch_suspend() saved the registers, so create_image() returns a second time. The two cases are distinguished by in_suspend, which is declared __nosavedata—it lives in a page excluded from the image, so in the restored kernel it holds the value the boot kernel left there, zero. The thaw-versus-restore choice in the final dpm_resume_start() call, and the corresponding one in hibernation_snapshot(), follow from that one variable.

In the restored kernel the lines after swsusp_arch_suspend() also tidy up after the boot kernel. Pages that were free in the image may hold whatever the boot kernel wrote into them, so clear_or_poison_free_pages() walks the free page map. If init_on_free is in force it zeroes them, and if page poisoning is enabled it poisons them, so that those options keep their guarantee (Chapter 27, §9.1). It also clears events_check_enabled, because the wakeup_count handshake that set it (§3.4) belongs to the time before the hibernation. The thaw callbacks, for their part, cannot tell a normal thaw from a failed hibernation. Most devices need not be thawed at all just to write the image, and resuming a large GPU only to power it off again is slow. Since 6.17 a driver can call pm_hibernate_is_recovering(), and since 6.18 pm_hibernation_mode_is_suspend(), and skip the work. amdgpu's ->thaw() returns early unless the hibernation failed, the machine will suspend afterwards, or console suspend has been disabled for debugging.

After the snapshot, hibernate() resumes devices with PMSG_THAW so that the storage stack works again, writes the image with swsusp_write(), and calls power_down(). What that does depends on /sys/power/disk: platform mode runs the devices' ->poweroff() callbacks and enters ACPI S4 through hibernation_platform_enter(), letting firmware arm wakeup devices; shutdown mode simply powers off through kernel_power_off() (§2.4; on ARM64, PSCI firmware that implements v1.3's SYSTEM_OFF2 with its hibernate type is used for this, telling the platform that the power-off is a hibernation); reboot restarts through kernel_restart(); and suspend mode enters suspend-to-RAM with the image already on disk, so the machine resumes quickly if the battery survives and from disk if it does not. Figure 21-4 shows both halves of the round trip.

power_down() has to cope with each of these failing. If the suspend in suspend mode fails, it falls back to platform mode when the platform supports it and to shutdown otherwise; if the suspend succeeds and the machine resumes from RAM, the image on disk is stale, so swsusp_unmark() puts the original swap signature back and the next boot will not try to restore it. hibernation_platform_enter() repeats a short version of the suspend sequence: the platform's ->begin(), the ->poweroff() callbacks through dpm_suspend_start() and dpm_suspend_end() with PMSG_HIBERNATE, the platform's ->prepare(), disabling the nonboot CPUs and the syscore callbacks, and a last pm_wakeup_pending() check before ->enter(). Since 6.8 a wakeup event caught there rolls the whole hibernation back instead of being ignored: the kernel logs “Wakeup event detected during hibernation, rolling back.”, restores the swap signature, and returns, and hibernate() resumes devices and thaws tasks as after a normal resume. In shutdown mode, and if platform mode fails for another reason, kernel_power_off() runs with entering_platform_hibernation set, which drivers can test through system_entering_hibernation(). If even that returns, kernel_halt() is tried, and as a last resort the kernel prints “Power down manually” and spins: a valid image is on disk, and continuing to run would change the filesystems behind its back.

Building the Snapshot

The snapshot code in kernel/power/snapshot.c must copy every page that matters into memory that it does not itself modify while copying. It tracks pages with memory bitmaps: a struct memory_bitmap holds one bit per page frame, organized for each populated zone as a small radix tree (struct mem_zone_bm_rtree) whose leaves are page-sized bitmap blocks, so that a bitmap is allocated only for memory that exists and a lookup by page frame number takes a few pointer hops. Several bitmaps are kept: the forbidden_pages_map marks pages the snapshot code allocated for itself, the free_pages_map marks free pages that need not be saved, orig_bm marks the pages to be saved, copy_bm the pages holding their copies, and, since 6.6, zero_bm the saved pages that turned out to be all zeroes and are recorded rather than copied.

Because the copy must fit in RAM alongside the original, the image can be at most about half of memory. hibernate_preallocate_memory(), called before any device is frozen, aims for the size in /sys/power/image_size (two-fifths of RAM by default): it counts saveable pages, reclaims page cache and swaps out anonymous memory until the image fits, and preallocates the copy pages so that no allocation is needed at the critical moment.

Preallocation is also how the image shrinks. Pages the snapshot code allocates are marked forbidden and are never saved, so every page grabbed for the copy is a page that drops out of the image. The function first computes the largest image that can work: half of the page frames left after the metadata and PAGES_FOR_IO (4 MB kept for the I/O that writes the image), minus twice /sys/power/reserved_size (1 MB by default, for drivers that allocate memory in ->freeze() or ->poweroff()). minimum_image_size() estimates the smallest image reclaim could reach, the saveable pages minus reclaimable slab and the anonymous and file LRU lists. The target lies between the two. Allocating that many pages forces the page allocator to reclaim (Chapter 17, §1.2), and shrink_all_memory() is called directly for whatever is still missing. The surplus is then given back with free_unnecessary_pages(). Writing 0 to image_size asks for the smallest possible image, which takes longer to create but less time to write and leaves less to reload, while a value larger than RAM skips reclaim. If even the minimum cannot be allocated, the kernel reports “Image allocation is N pages short” and hibernation fails with -ENOMEM.

Then, with one CPU running and interrupts off, swsusp_save()—called from the architecture's swsusp_arch_suspend() in arch/x86/power/hibernate_asm_64.S or arch/arm64/kernel/hibernate.c after the registers have been saved—does the copy: 

/* kernel/power/snapshot.c (abridged) */
asmlinkage __visible int swsusp_save(void)
{
	unsigned int nr_pages, nr_highmem;

	drain_local_pages(NULL);
	nr_pages = count_data_pages();
	nr_highmem = count_highmem_pages();

	if (!enough_free_mem(nr_pages, nr_highmem))
		return -ENOMEM;

	if (swsusp_alloc(&copy_bm, nr_pages, nr_highmem))
		return -ENOMEM;

	drain_local_pages(NULL);
	nr_copy_pages = copy_data_pages(&copy_bm, &orig_bm, &zero_bm);

	/*
	 * End of critical section. From now on, we can write to memory,
	 * but we should not touch disk. This specially means we must _not_
	 * touch swap space! Except we must write out our image of course.
	 */
	nr_pages += nr_highmem;
	nr_zero_pages = nr_pages - nr_copy_pages;
	nr_meta_pages = DIV_ROUND_UP(nr_pages * sizeof(long), PAGE_SIZE);

	return 0;
}

copy_data_pages() walks every zone and saves each page that saveable_page() accepts: in use, not forbidden, not free, and not a reserved page outside the kernel's direct mapping or inside a region registered with register_nosave_region() (firmware tables and similar memory the boot kernel will recreate). x86 registers every gap and non-RAM range of the e820 map in this way with e820__register_nosave_regions(), and the kernel's own __nosave data section, which holds the few variables the restore code uses while it overwrites memory, is treated the same way. Two more kinds of page are skipped. Pages marked PageOffline belong to a balloon or memory hot-plug driver (virtio-balloon, virtio-mem, Hyper-V, Xen, or VMware) that has given them back to the hypervisor, so reading them may not even be possible. Guard pages created by debug_pagealloc are unmapped on purpose. The draining of per-CPU page lists before and after allocation ensures that pages sitting in those caches are counted as free. Once this returns, the image in memory is consistent; everything the kernel does afterwards—thawing devices, writing the image—changes memory that is not part of it.

On ARM64, memory tagging adds state that is not in the page data. With MTE enabled, each 16-byte granule of a tagged page carries a 4-bit tag held in separate tag storage, so copying the page contents does not preserve it. swsusp_arch_suspend() therefore calls swsusp_mte_save_tags() before the copy, which saves the tags of every page with page_mte_tagged() set into an xarray indexed by page frame number. After resume, swsusp_mte_restore_tags() writes them back. The function saves the CPU state with __cpu_suspend_enter(), the same routine that cpuidle and suspend use on ARM64 (§2.3), so it also returns twice, and it records the logical CPU it ran on in sleep_cpu for the image header.

Writing the Image and Coming Back

swsusp_write() in kernel/power/swap.c writes the image to the swap device named by resume=. It saves a header with the image metadata, then the page frame numbers of the saved pages (the “meta pages”), then the data, recording the swap slots it used in a chain of swap_map_page structures. Finally it overwrites the swap signature in the first page with HIBERNATE_SIG ("S1SUSPEND") and the location of the image, so that the next boot can recognize the partition as holding an image and a normal swapon will not treat it as swap. By default the data is compressed; since 6.9 the compressor is reached through the crypto API, LZO remains the default (CONFIG_HIBERNATION_DEF_COMP), and LZ4, which decompresses faster, can be chosen with hibernate.compressor=lz4. Compression runs in several kernel threads in parallel with a separate CRC32 thread that checksums the uncompressed data; the number of compression threads, three by default, became tunable in 6.19. hibernate=nocompress disables compression.

Two headers describe the image. The first page of the swap area holds struct swsusp_header: the signature and, in orig_sig, the swap signature it replaced, the sector where the image starts, the CRC32 of the uncompressed data, an optional hardware signature, and a flags word. SF_PLATFORM_MODE records that the image was made in platform mode, so that the restore uses the platform hooks too; SF_NOCOMPRESS_MODE marks an uncompressed image; and SF_COMPRESSION_ALG_LZ4 distinguishes LZ4 from LZO. The boot kernel decompresses with the algorithm named in the flags, whatever its own default. The first image page holds struct swsusp_info: the utsname and version of the kernel, the number of physical pages and CPUs, and the page counts. check_header() refuses an image made on a machine with a different amount of memory. The check of the kernel version and release applies only on architectures without their own header; x86, ARM64, and RISC-V supply one and make their own decision, described below. Which swap device receives the image is chosen by swsusp_swap_check(): the one given by resume= and resume_offset= if it is an active swap area, otherwise the first active one. enough_swap() then makes sure the free swap exceeds the image size by PAGES_FOR_IO, or hibernation fails with “Not enough free swap”. Swap is shared with normal paging (Chapter 17, §8.1), so the image is written to the slots that are free at that moment, which is why the location is recorded as a chain of slot numbers rather than as a contiguous extent.

Resume starts in a freshly booted kernel. software_resume() runs either as a late_initcall_sync, using the device given by resume=, or when an initramfs writes the device number to /sys/power/resume after it has set up the storage stack (the usual arrangement for encrypted or LVM swap; see Appendix A, §8.4). It checks the signature with swsusp_check(), runs the PM_RESTORE_PREPARE notifiers, freezes all tasks, and calls load_image_and_restore(). swsusp_read() loads the image, but it cannot simply put each page at its original address, since the boot kernel may be using those frames. Instead, snapshot_write_next() places each page either directly into its original frame, if that frame is free in the boot kernel, or into a “safe” page that the boot kernel is guaranteed not to need, recording the pairs in restore_pblist.

swsusp_check() opens the resume device exclusively and, as soon as it has recognized the signature, writes back the original swap signature saved in orig_sig. From then on the partition is ordinary swap again. An image is therefore used at most once: if the restore fails or the machine crashes while loading it, the next boot finds no image and boots normally, rather than retrying the same bad image forever. The read side verifies the CRC32 when one was recorded and fails with “Invalid image CRC32!” on a mismatch, and it decompresses in several threads while reading ahead. On ACPI systems the firmware's FACS table holds a hardware signature that is supposed to change when the hardware configuration changes. Linux records it when entering S4, and acpi_hibernation_leave() only warns “Hardware changed while hibernated, success doubtful!” when it differs after resume. With acpi_sleep=s4_hwsig, the default for x86 guests running on a hypervisor, the signature is stored in the swap header with SF_HW_SIG and the boot kernel refuses the image when the signature has changed, as the ACPI specification requires. acpi_sleep=s4_nohwsig turns the check off. In platform mode the ACPI code also saves the ACPI NVS memory regions on the way down and restores them on the way up, unless acpi_sleep=nonvs is given for firmware that misbehaves when this memory is touched.

hibernation_restore() then quiesces the boot kernel's devices with PMSG_QUIESCE and calls resume_target_kernel(), which takes the same one-CPU, interrupts-off path as create_image() and finally calls swsusp_arch_resume(). On x86_64 that function (arch/x86/power/hibernate_64.c) builds temporary page tables, copies the small core_restore_code routine to a safe page with relocate_restore_code(), and jumps to it; the routine copies every page on restore_pblist to its final address and jumps to the image kernel's restore_registers. ARM64 does the same with trans_pgd_create_copy() and a relocated copy of swsusp_arch_suspend_exit(). Either way, the image kernel resumes inside swsusp_arch_suspend(), returns into create_image() with in_suspend clear, and runs its devices' ->restore() callbacks.

Before the jump, resume_target_kernel() runs the boot kernel's dpm_suspend_end(PMSG_QUIESCE), the platform's ->pre_restore(), pauses cpuidle, and calls hibernate_resume_nonboot_cpu_disable(). On x86 the offline CPUs must not wait in MWAIT, because the memory they monitor is about to be overwritten and a write would wake them into code that is no longer there. The x86 version therefore first brings back any SMT siblings that were never fully started with cpuhp_smt_enable(), then takes all secondary CPUs offline in a plain HLT loop. The restored kernel, if it runs with SMT disabled, calls arch_resume_nosmt() to wake those siblings and park them properly. On ARM64 the image must be resumed on the same physical CPU that created it, because __cpu_suspend_enter() saved that CPU's state. The boot kernel reads the MPIDR recorded in the image header, brings that CPU up with bringup_hibernate_cpu() if necessary, and offlines all the others. After the jump the image kernel cannot assume anything about device state: the boot kernel, its firmware, or the boot loader may have programmed a device in any way, so a driver's ->restore() callback has to reinitialize the hardware fully rather than reverse its own ->freeze().

CPU state that does not live in memory has needed the same care, and recent kernels fixed several cases. On x86 with FRED, the boot kernel programs the FRED MSRs with addresses from its own layout, and since 6.15 the image kernel reloads them after the jump. With Indirect Branch Tracking, the relocated restore code is entered through an indirect jump and needs an ENDBR instruction, also fixed in 6.15. On ARM64 with kernel CFI, the indirect call to the relocated copy of swsusp_arch_suspend_exit() failed the type-hash check, because the copy lies outside the kernel image. Since 6.19, swsusp_arch_resume() is marked __nocfi.

The boot kernel and the image kernel need not be loaded at the same address, which matters with KASLR: the architecture header stored in the image records where the image kernel's entry point and page tables are. On x86_64, struct restore_data_record holds the jump address, its physical address, the image kernel's CR3, a magic number, and a checksum of the e820 memory map, which must be unchanged. On ARM64, struct arch_hibernate_hdr holds TTBR1_EL1, the re-entry point, the location of the EL2 stub vectors, and the MPIDR of the CPU that hibernated, and its invariants include the kernel's build version string, so that an image is only restored by the exact kernel build that created it. Hibernation is also related to kexec, which loads one kernel from another without firmware involvement (Appendix A, §8.2): both must hand a CPU from one kernel image to another with a consistent memory map, and the x86 code for both shares helpers for building identity-mapped page tables. User-space hibernation tools such as s2disk use the /dev/snapshot device (kernel/power/user.c) to drive the same snapshot and restore code while doing their own I/O (§4.4).

Two consequences for the system as a whole follow from this design. First, the image contains the in-memory state of every mounted filesystem: cached pages, dirty metadata, and the open journal. If any filesystem that was mounted at hibernation is mounted and modified between hibernation and resume, the restored kernel's view no longer matches the disk, and writing it back corrupts the filesystem. The resume must therefore happen before anything mounts the root filesystem read-write, which is why initramfs implementations write to /sys/power/resume before mounting it, and why booting another operating system that mounts the same partitions in between is dangerous. Second, the kernel neither encrypts nor signs the image. Anything in memory, including keys and passwords, lands on swap in cleartext, and a modified image would run with full kernel privileges. Hibernation to swap on dm-crypt, unlocked by the initramfs before it triggers the resume, deals with the first problem. The second is why the integrity level of the kernel lockdown LSM disables hibernation altogether (Chapter 27, §8.1).

Configuring and Testing Hibernation

The kernel has to be told where the image lives. resume= names the swap device by path, by PARTUUID= or similar, or by device number, and CONFIG_PM_STD_PARTITION can build in a default. When the swap area is a swap file rather than a partition, resume= names the block device that holds the file and resume_offset= gives the position of the file's first page on that device, in page-size units. The offset can be found with filefrag -v on the swap file and works only on filesystems that keep a swap file's blocks in place; the image itself is written through the swap subsystem's own block map. The boot kernel looks the device up in find_resume_device(). Because software_resume() runs as a late_initcall_sync, a disk behind a slow controller may not have been probed yet, so resumedelay= sleeps a number of seconds first and resumewait keeps polling until the device appears. In practice most distributions instead let the initramfs find the device (unlocking dm-crypt or activating LVM on the way) and write its number, and the offset, to /sys/power/resume_offset and /sys/power/resume; writing resume runs software_resume() at once. Recent versions of systemd can also pass the location from the hibernating system to the next boot in an EFI variable, so that no command-line parameter is needed.

Other parameters switch parts of the mechanism off. noresume, or hibernate=noresume, skips the check for an image, so the image is ignored. The partition still carries the hibernation signature, which the kernel's swapon() does not accept, but the util-linux swapon command recognizes it and rewrites it as an ordinary swap signature before activating the area. hibernate=no or nohibernate disables hibernation entirely, and hibernate=nocompress writes uncompressed images. hibernate=protect_image, on kernels with CONFIG_STRICT_KERNEL_RWX, makes each page of the loaded image read-only with set_memory_ro() until the final copy, so that a stray write by the boot kernel during the restore faults instead of silently corrupting the image. hibernate.compressor= and hibernate_compression_threads= select the compressor and thread count (§4.3).

At run time, /sys/power/disk chooses how power_down() ends (§4.1) and lists the choices with the current one in brackets; platform appears only when a platform driver such as ACPI has registered hibernation_ops, and the file reads [disabled] when hibernation is not available. reboot is useful for testing, since the round trip needs no one to press the power button. A faster test is test_resume: the kernel creates and writes the image exactly as for a real hibernation, then, instead of powering off, opens the swap device again with swsusp_check() and runs load_image_and_restore(), so that the image is read back and restored into the running system. This checks image writing, reading, and the restore path without firmware involvement. image_size and reserved_size tune preallocation (§4.2); Table 21-4 lists these files with the others in /sys/power.

The /sys/power/pm_test levels of §3.5 apply to hibernation as well. With freezer, hibernate() stops after freezing tasks; with devices, after the ->freeze() callbacks; and with platform, processors, or core, at the corresponding point in create_image(). In every case no image is created, the kernel waits pm_test_delay seconds, and then unwinds through the thaw path. The delay for hibernation, settable as hibernate.pm_test_delay since 6.16, is a busy wait with mdelay() that, unlike the suspend delay, cannot be cut short by a wakeup event. pm_debug_messages enables the pm_pr_dbg() messages along the hibernation path, which since 6.16 include the steps of image creation and restore.

The /dev/snapshot character device (CONFIG_HIBERNATION_SNAPSHOT_DEV, implemented in kernel/power/user.c) exposes the snapshot code to a user-space program, such as s2disk and resume from the uswsusp package. The program freezes tasks with the SNAPSHOT_FREEZE ioctl(), creates the image with SNAPSHOT_CREATE_IMAGE, and then reads the image from the device and stores it wherever and however it likes, compressed or encrypted. To restore, the resume program writes the image back into the device and issues SNAPSHOT_ATOMIC_RESTORE. The program must run from memory locked with mlockall(), since swap is unusable while it works. The device and the in-kernel path exclude each other through hibernate_acquire(): the device can be opened only by one process and not while hibernate() runs. The option can be turned off to reduce the kernel's attack surface when no such tool is used.

Finally, part of the machinery is reused for a purpose that has nothing to do with saving memory. hibernate_quiet_exec() freezes tasks and devices as if for a snapshot, runs a function with the system quiescent, and thaws everything again. The NVDIMM core uses it to activate new firmware on persistent-memory modules, which must not be accessed while the firmware switches.

Runtime Power Management

System sleep powers everything down at once and only when the whole machine is idle. Most of the energy a phone or laptop saves, however, is saved while it is in use: the camera is off while the user types, the SD card controller sleeps between accesses, the GPU powers down between frames. Runtime PM is the framework that lets each device be suspended and resumed individually, whenever its driver says it is not needed, while the rest of the system keeps running. It was merged in 2.6.32 and lives in drivers/base/power/runtime.c and include/linux/pm_runtime.h.

The framework is policy-free in the same way the device-sleep phases of §3.2 are: the core keeps the books (who is using the device, what state it is in, which other devices it depends on), and the driver and its bus decide what "suspended" means for the hardware, whether that is gating a clock, putting a PCI function into D3hot, or cutting power entirely. PM domains, which power off a whole group of runtime-suspended devices together (§6), and device PM QoS constraints, which can veto a runtime suspend (§8.4), build on it.

The Runtime PM Model

Runtime PM is reference counting applied to power. A driver brackets every period in which it needs the hardware with a pm_runtime_get call, which increments the device's power.usage_count and resumes the device if necessary, and a matching pm_runtime_put call, which decrements the count and, once it reaches zero, lets the core consider suspending the device. The core never asks the driver whether the device is busy; a nonzero usage count is the answer. Ownership of the count is the driver's, so the familiar refcounting bugs—a missing put that keeps a device powered forever, an extra put that suspends it under active I/O—are the characteristic runtime PM bugs.

The state lives in the runtime half of struct dev_pm_info, the power member embedded in every struct device (Chapter 13, §3). Its main fields are listed in Table 21-5. All of them are protected by power.lock, a spinlock, which is why the runtime PM entry points can be called from atomic context when the driver has declared its callbacks safe for it.

RPM_GET_PUT makes the call adjust usage_count (increment before a resume, decrement before an idle or suspend, which then proceeds only if the count reached zero). RPM_ASYNC queues the operation on the pm workqueue instead of running it in the caller's context; RPM_NOWAIT refuses to wait for a transition already in progress; RPM_AUTO honors the autosuspend delay; and RPM_TRANSPARENT, added in 6.18, makes a resume succeed silently when runtime PM is disabled for the device. pm_runtime_get_sync() has a notorious trap: it increments the usage count even when the resume fails, so the caller must put it back on the error path. pm_runtime_resume_and_get() drops the count itself on failure and is the recommended form. The scope-based guards added in 6.18 (guard(pm_runtime_active)(dev) and friends) and the PM_RUNTIME_ACQUIRE() macros added in 6.19 go further, pairing the get with an automatic put when the enclosing scope ends.

The two guards differ in their put: guard(pm_runtime_active) pairs pm_runtime_get_sync() with pm_runtime_put(), while guard(pm_runtime_active_auto) ends with pm_runtime_put_autosuspend(); a third, pm_runtime_noresume, only pins the count without resuming. Because a plain guard cannot report failure, the 6.19 macros build on the conditional-guard machinery of Chapter 5, §5.18: PM_RUNTIME_ACQUIRE(dev, var) attempts the resume (with RPM_TRANSPARENT, so a device without runtime PM counts as active), PM_RUNTIME_ACQUIRE_ERR(&var) yields the error to test, and the reference is dropped automatically only if it was taken. The _AUTOSUSPEND and _IF_ENABLED variants select the autosuspend put and the non-transparent pm_runtime_resume_and_get(), respectively.

Autosuspend is opt-in. A driver calls pm_runtime_use_autosuspend() and pm_runtime_set_autosuspend_delay() in probe, or user space writes power/autosuspend_delay_ms; a negative delay prevents runtime suspend altogether, which the core implements by taking an extra usage reference in update_autosuspend(). Each idle period then ends with pm_runtime_put_autosuspend(), which records the current time in last_busy before dropping the count. Before 6.17 the wrapper did not, and drivers had to call pm_runtime_mark_last_busy() first; the explicit calls that remain in older drivers are now redundant, and are being removed tree-wide. Two conditional helpers serve code that must not power a device up just to look at it, such as an interrupt handler or a debugfs read. pm_runtime_get_if_active() takes a reference only if the device is RPM_ACTIVE, and pm_runtime_get_if_in_use() takes one only if, in addition, someone else already holds a reference or (since 6.17) the device has active children; both return 0 without touching the count otherwise, and -EINVAL when runtime PM is disabled. The return values of the put helpers are of limited use: 1 means the device was already suspended, and a negative code may mean only that no suspend was attempted, for instance because user space has written on to power/control. In 7.0 pm_runtime_put() therefore returns nothing at all, because callers kept mistaking that result for an error.

The driver core helps keep the count honest around binding. Before calling a driver's probe routine, driver_probe_device() in drivers/base/dd.c resumes the device's runtime-PM suppliers (pm_runtime_get_suppliers()) and its parent (pm_runtime_get_sync()) and flushes pending runtime PM work with pm_runtime_barrier(), so probe always runs with everything upstream powered (Chapter 13, §3.3). After probe it queues an idle check with pm_request_idle() and drops the parent and supplier references, so a device the driver left with a zero count is suspended soon after. Unbinding is the mirror image: __device_release_driver() resumes the device with pm_runtime_get_sync() before the driver's remove callback runs, so remove never has to wonder whether the hardware is reachable, and the final device_unbind_cleanup() calls pm_runtime_reinit(), which, if the driver left runtime PM disabled, marks the device suspended, drops the parent reference an irq-safe device held, and clears needs_force_resume. devm_pm_runtime_enable() also undoes autosuspend on unbind, and 6.16 added devm_pm_runtime_set_active_enabled() and devm_pm_runtime_get_noresume() for the other common probe-time pairs.

Inside rpm_suspend() and rpm_resume()

The three exported entry points take power.lock and call the internal workers rpm_idle(), rpm_suspend(), and rpm_resume(), which run with the lock held and drop it only around driver callbacks and waits. An idle notification is the gentlest request: rpm_idle() checks that the device could be suspended, calls the driver's ->runtime_idle() if there is one (which may veto by returning nonzero), and otherwise proceeds to rpm_suspend() with RPM_AUTO added. Idle notifications are accepted only in RPM_ACTIVE and never wait: one that finds another notification running returns -EINPROGRESS, and an asynchronous one merely queues an idle request, so the cost of the many pm_runtime_put() calls a busy driver makes is a few tests under the lock. rpm_suspend() begins by asking whether a suspend is allowed at all: 

/* drivers/base/power/runtime.c */
static int rpm_check_suspend_allowed(struct device *dev)
{
	int retval = 0;

	if (dev->power.runtime_error)
		retval = -EINVAL;
	else if (dev->power.disable_depth > 0)
		retval = -EACCES;
	else if (atomic_read(&dev->power.usage_count))
		retval = -EAGAIN;
	else if (!dev->power.ignore_children && atomic_read(&dev->power.child_count))
		retval = -EBUSY;

	/* Pending resume requests take precedence over suspends. */
	else if ((dev->power.deferred_resume &&
	    dev->power.runtime_status == RPM_SUSPENDING) ||
	    (dev->power.request_pending && dev->power.request == RPM_REQ_RESUME))
		retval = -EAGAIN;
	else if (__dev_pm_qos_resume_latency(dev) == 0)
		retval = -EPERM;
	else if (dev->power.runtime_status == RPM_SUSPENDED)
		retval = 1;

	return retval;
}

The last test but one connects runtime PM to device PM QoS: a per-device resume-latency constraint of zero, settable by user space through power/pm_qos_resume_latency_us or by a kernel user with dev_pm_qos_add_request(), means the device may not afford any wakeup latency, so it must not suspend. With the checks passed, rpm_suspend() handles autosuspend: if RPM_AUTO is set and pm_runtime_autosuspend_expiration() says the delay since last_busy has not elapsed, it arms power.suspend_timer, an hrtimer, for the expiry time (with a slack of a quarter of the delay, so expiries can be batched) and returns. A driver that calls pm_runtime_put_autosuspend() after every I/O therefore keeps pushing the suspend into the future, and the device sleeps only after a quiet interval—the right policy for hardware whose resume is expensive relative to the gaps between requests. An asynchronous request is recorded in power.request and queued as power.work on pm_wq, an unbound workqueue named pm created in kernel/power/main.c; the work function later runs the same worker synchronously.

For a synchronous suspend, the status becomes RPM_SUSPENDING and the callback is looked up by RPM_GET_CALLBACK(). The search order is the same as for system sleep—PM domain, device type, class, bus—except that if the chosen subsystem table has no runtime callback, the core falls back to the driver's own dev_pm_ops. rpm_callback() drops the lock and calls it, under memalloc_noio_save() if the device is on the block I/O path (a storage controller that needs memory to resume must not wait for writeback through itself). On success the device becomes RPM_SUSPENDED, its parent's child_count is decremented, and an asynchronous idle check is queued for the parent, which may now be suspendable in turn; that is how suspends propagate up the device tree. On failure the device returns to RPM_ACTIVE. The error handling, unified for suspend and resume in 6.15, lives in rpm_callback(): -EACCES, which the core reserves to mean "runtime PM is disabled", is converted to -EAGAIN if a driver returns it, and an error other than -EAGAIN or -EBUSY is latched in runtime_error and blocks all further runtime transitions until the driver clears it with pm_runtime_set_active() or pm_runtime_set_suspended(). The two transient codes are how a driver says "not now": a failed autosuspend whose last_busy has moved in the meantime is simply retried at the new expiry time, and a subsequent put tries again.

rpm_resume() mirrors this from the bottom up. If the device has a parent, it takes a usage reference on the parent and resumes it synchronously first, since a device cannot be powered while the bus or bridge above it is off; only then does it set RPM_RESUMING and call ->runtime_resume(). On success the device is RPM_ACTIVE, the parent's child_count is incremented, and the temporary parent reference is dropped (the child count now holds the parent up). A failed resume leaves the device RPM_SUSPENDED, with the error latched by the same rule as for suspend. A successful one also stamps last_busy and queues an asynchronous idle check for the device itself, so a device resumed by a call that holds no reference (pm_runtime_resume() from a wake interrupt, say) drifts back to sleep on its own once nobody takes a reference. A resume of a device that is already active returns 1 at once, and a no_callbacks device skips the parent entirely if the parent is already active, ignores its children, or has runtime PM disabled. If the device was caught mid-suspend, a synchronous resume waits for the suspend to finish, while an asynchronous one sets deferred_resume, which rpm_suspend() honors by immediately resuming the device it just suspended.

The parent–child rule covers the device tree, but power dependencies do not always follow it: a display controller may need a power-management IC on a different bus, or a PCIe device may depend on a clock provider. Device links created with device_link_add() and the DL_FLAG_PM_RUNTIME flag (4.10) express such edges for runtime PM: __rpm_callback() resumes every such supplier, with rpm_get_suppliers(), before calling the consumer's resume callback, and rpm_suspend_suppliers() lets them suspend again after the consumer has. The same links order the device lists for system sleep (§3.2). Callbacks that may run with interrupts disabled are a special case: a driver calls pm_runtime_irq_safe() to promise that its callbacks never sleep, after which the core calls them with the spinlock held and interrupts off, spins rather than sleeps when it finds a transition in progress, and keeps the parent permanently resumed (an irq-safe child takes a reference on its parent at the time of the call), because resuming a parent might sleep. Such devices also skip the supplier handling.

A runtime-suspended device must still be able to ask for service: a UART must notice an incoming character, a touch controller a finger. Many devices signal this through their normal interrupt line, which stays enabled, but on SoCs the block's interrupt often stops working once its clock is gated, and the wakeup arrives instead on a separate always-on line, typically a GPIO or a pin-controller interrupt. A driver registers that line with dev_pm_set_dedicated_wake_irq() (in drivers/base/power/wakeirq.c), and the core arms it around every runtime suspend: rpm_suspend() enables it just before calling ->runtime_suspend() (or just after, with the _reverse variant, for hardware that would otherwise raise it while suspending), and rpm_resume() disables it before calling ->runtime_resume(). The threaded handler, handle_threaded_wake_irq(), simply calls pm_runtime_resume(), and the queued idle check described above lets the device sleep again once the driver has handled whatever woke it. If the same line is armed for system wakeup when it fires, the handler reports a wakeup event instead (§3.4), so one interrupt serves both purposes.

Because every transition passes through these few functions, they are also the place to watch when a device will not sleep or will not stay awake. The tracepoints in include/trace/events/rpm.h—rpm_idle, rpm_suspend, rpm_resume, and rpm_usage at the entry of each worker, rpm_return_int at the exit, and rpm_status on every state change—record the device name, the flags, the usage and child counts, and the return value, which is usually enough to find the missing or extra put (tracepoints themselves are described in Chapter 29, §7).

User space controls runtime PM through the power/ directory of every device in sysfs. Writing on to power/control calls pm_runtime_forbid(), which clears runtime_auto, takes a usage reference, and resumes the device; writing auto calls pm_runtime_allow(), which drops it. Some buses default to on—pci_pm_init() forbids runtime PM for every PCI device, which is why power-tuning tools write auto to hundreds of files at boot—while most platform devices default to auto. The neighboring attributes runtime_status, autosuspend_delay_ms, runtime_active_time, runtime_suspended_time, runtime_usage, and runtime_active_kids expose the fields of Table 21-5; runtime_status reads error while a callback error is latched and unsupported while runtime PM is disabled, which is the first thing to check when a device never suspends.

Runtime PM in Bus Types

The core's callbacks are, by the lookup order above, usually the bus type's, and the bus decides what runtime suspend means for its hardware. PCI shows the pattern clearly. pci_pm_init() sets every new function RPM_ACTIVE and forbids runtime PM, and pci_bus_add_device() enables it only once the device is fully configured. local_pci_probe() then takes a usage reference before calling the driver's probe routine, so an unbound or runtime-PM-unaware PCI device is never suspended; a driver that supports runtime PM drops that reference in probe (usually with pm_runtime_put_noidle()) and takes it back in remove. pci_pm_runtime_suspend() calls the driver's ->runtime_suspend(), which only has to quiesce the device, and then does the PCI part itself: it saves config space and calls pci_finish_runtime_suspend(), which picks the target state with pci_target_state() (the firmware's choice if the platform manages the device, which can be D3cold only while d3cold_allowed is set in sysfs; otherwise the deepest state from which the device can signal PME), enables PME if the device can generate runtime wakeups, and changes the power state. Runtime wakeup is armed whenever the hardware can do it, regardless of the user's power/wakeup setting, because a device that cannot wake itself cannot be allowed to sleep while in use. A driver returning -EBUSY or -EAGAIN is logged only at debug level, since that is the normal way to decline. pci_pm_runtime_resume() restores the power state and config space before calling the driver, and does so even for an unbound device, which may have lost power when the bridge above it went to D3cold. PCIe ports participate too: the port driver allows runtime PM with a 100 ms autosuspend delay when pci_bridge_d3_possible() says the bridge can be put in D3, so a port suspends once all the devices below it have.

USB builds a two-level scheme on the same core. Each struct usb_device is the parent of its struct usb_interface devices, and interface drivers do the counting: usb_autopm_get_interface() and usb_autopm_put_interface() are thin wrappers around pm_runtime_resume_and_get() and pm_runtime_put_sync() on the interface. An interface whose driver does not set supports_autosuspend is left RPM_ACTIVE with runtime PM disabled, and as an active child it holds the whole device up. When all interfaces are idle, the device's autosuspend runs autosuspend_check(), which refuses if an interface driver set needs_remote_wakeup but the device cannot signal remote wakeup, and then suspends the interfaces and asks the hub to suspend the port (USB selective suspend). The policy is conservative: usb_new_device() enables autosuspend with a default delay of two seconds (CONFIG_USB_AUTOSUSPEND_DELAY, overridable with usbcore.autosuspend=) but forbids it for every device, because many devices misbehave when suspended; only hubs, which are known to handle it, allow it themselves, and root hubs only if the host controller driver implements bus suspend. User space opts other devices in by writing auto to their power/control, which is what tools such as powertop and tlp do.

Runtime PM Meets System Sleep

A system suspend can find any device in any runtime state, and the two frameworks must not fight. The PM core keeps them apart in three steps. First, device_prepare() calls pm_runtime_get_noresume() on every device, so that no device can runtime-suspend for the duration of the transition (although devices already suspended stay suspended), and, since 6.15, sets the last_status of devices whose runtime PM has never been enabled to RPM_BLOCKED so that a driver cannot enable it in the middle of the transition. Second, device_suspend_late() calls pm_runtime_disable() on each device, freezing its runtime status; from there on, pm_runtime_status_suspended() is a stable fact that the noirq callbacks and the smart-suspend optimization of §3.2 can rely on. (A direct-complete device has its runtime PM disabled earlier, in device_suspend().) Third, device_resume_early() re-enables runtime PM, and device_complete() drops the reference taken in prepare, letting idle devices runtime-suspend again.

Asynchronous runtime PM work is fenced as well. device_suspend() calls pm_runtime_barrier(), which cancels pending requests, waits for any transition in progress, and resumes the device if a resume request was pending, since someone evidently wanted it awake (§3.2). The checks that let a device stay runtime-suspended through system sleep are deliberately conservative. Direct-complete disables runtime PM and then re-reads the status, so that a resume racing with the check cannot leave a device awake but skipped. Smart suspend (DPM_FLAG_SMART_SUSPEND) is, since 6.15, granted to a device only if its parent and every supplier linked with DL_FLAG_PM_RUNTIME also have it, ignore their children, or never enabled runtime PM; applied unevenly along a dependency chain, the flag could leave a device's runtime status out of step with its real power state. On the way back, device_resume_noirq() writes the outcome into the runtime status: RPM_SUSPENDED if the device's resume is being skipped, or RPM_ACTIVE if a smart-suspend device is being resumed, so that runtime PM picks up from the hardware's real state when it is re-enabled.

Many drivers have nothing different to do for system sleep than for runtime suspend: quiesce the hardware and gate its clocks. For them the core provides pm_runtime_force_suspend() and pm_runtime_force_resume() (3.15), and the DEFINE_RUNTIME_DEV_PM_OPS() macro installs them as the system-sleep callbacks next to the driver's runtime ones. pm_runtime_force_suspend() disables runtime PM and, if the device is not already suspended, calls its ->runtime_suspend() directly. It then decides whether the device must be resumed afterward: if nothing requires the device to be active (no usage reference beyond the one taken in prepare, and no active children), it marks the device RPM_SUSPENDED and leaves it for the next runtime resume to wake; otherwise it sets needs_force_resume, and pm_runtime_force_resume() calls ->runtime_resume() during system resume. A device that was idle before the suspend thus stays off across the whole cycle without the driver writing a line of system-sleep code.

The pair has been reworked several times, and the 7.0 versions carry a few rules worth knowing. pm_runtime_force_suspend() returns at once if the device is already suspended or already has needs_force_resume set, so calling it twice (once from a driver, once from a PM domain) is harmless. It arms a dedicated wake IRQ around the callback exactly as rpm_suspend() does, and if the callback fails it re-enables runtime PM before returning the error, so a failed system suspend leaves runtime PM working. The test for "nothing requires the device" is pm_runtime_need_not_resume(): a usage count of at most 1 (the reference from prepare) and no active children. pm_runtime_force_resume() resumes the device if needs_force_resume is set or, since 6.17, if it is a smart-suspend device whose status the noirq phase set to RPM_ACTIVE; in every case it clears both flags and re-enables runtime PM. Finally, when a bus type wraps the driver's runtime callbacks with its own work, as PCI and the ACPI PM domain do, calling the wrapped version from a system-sleep path would repeat that work, so 6.17 added power.strict_midlayer: a middle layer that sets it with dev_pm_set_strict_midlayer() (PCI does so in pci_pm_prepare()) makes the force helpers call the driver's ->runtime_suspend() and ->runtime_resume() directly, leaving the bus's own system-sleep callbacks to do the bus part.

Power Domains

Suspending one device saves its dynamic power, but the leakage of its transistors continues as long as its voltage rail is up, and on a SoC that rail is usually shared with other devices. Removing the rail—power gating a whole island of silicon—is possible only when every device on it is idle. PM domains are the kernel abstraction for such shared power resources: they sit between the PM core and the drivers, see the runtime state of every device they contain, and switch the island off when the last one suspends.

A PM domain does not replace runtime PM or the system-sleep phases; they drive it. The PM core calls the domain's callbacks in place of the bus type's (§3.2 and §5.2), and the domain decides, from what it sees across all of its devices, whether the shared resource can go. On x86 the domain is usually ACPI's; on device-tree platforms it is genpd, which can also nest domains, carry performance states, and model CPU clusters for cpuidle.

struct dev_pm_domain and the ACPI Domain

At its simplest a PM domain is a struct dev_pm_domain pointed to by dev->pm_domain. Because the PM core looks there first for every callback (§3.2 and §5.2), whoever installs the domain intercepts all of the device's system-sleep and runtime PM callbacks, does its own work around them, and calls the driver's callbacks from inside its own: 

/* include/linux/pm.h */
struct dev_pm_domain {
	struct dev_pm_ops	ops;
	int (*start)(struct device *dev);
	void (*detach)(struct device *dev, bool power_off);
	int (*activate)(struct device *dev);
	void (*sync)(struct device *dev);
	void (*dismiss)(struct device *dev);
	int (*set_performance_state)(struct device *dev, unsigned int state);
};

->activate(), ->sync(), and ->dismiss() are called by the driver core around probe, so that a domain can power up a device before its driver touches it and back down if the probe fails; ->detach() undoes the attachment. Bus types attach the domain automatically: platform, AMBA, I2C, SPI, auxiliary, serdev, rpmsg, SDIO, SoundWire, and DisplayPort AUX-bus devices call dev_pm_domain_attach() in their probe path (the consumer view is in Chapter 13, §8.4). That function tries the two domain implementations in turn: acpi_dev_pm_attach() first, then genpd_dev_pm_attach().

Since 6.17 dev_pm_domain_attach() takes flags instead of a single power_on boolean (drivers/base/power/common.c). It returns at once if dev->pm_domain is already set, so a domain that platform code installed earlier wins. PD_FLAG_ATTACH_POWER_ON asks for the device to be powered for probe: ACPI moves it to D0, and genpd powers up the domain of a single-domain device on attach in any case. PD_FLAG_DETACH_POWER_OFF is recorded in dev->power.detach_power_off for later. In the same release the detach itself moved from the bus types' remove() methods to device_unbind_cleanup() (drivers/base/dd.c), which calls dev_pm_domain_detach() only after devres_release_all(). The old order detached first, so that a devm_pm_runtime_enable() action that runtime-resumed the device during cleanup could reach registers whose clocks the domain no longer managed. If the device tree names a genpd provider that has not registered yet, attach fails with -EPROBE_DEFER, and the consumer's probe is retried later (Chapter 13, §3.3).

On x86 the domain is usually ACPI's. A device with an ACPI companion (Chapter 13, §3) can be put into the ACPI device power states D0 (on) through D3cold (power removed) by evaluating the companion's _PSx and _PRx methods, which encode the platform's knowledge of rails and power resources. acpi_dev_pm_attach() installs acpi_general_pm_domain (drivers/acpi/device_pm.c), whose callbacks wrap the driver's: acpi_subsys_runtime_suspend(), for instance, runs the driver's runtime suspend and then acpi_dev_suspend(), which arms the device's wakeup (the _PRW GPE and _DSW) if it may wake the system and moves it to the lowest D-state that the firmware permits for the target system state (the _SxD and _SxW methods bound the choice). PCI devices take a different route to the same firmware: the PCI bus type manages their D-states through the PCI power-management capability and calls acpi_pci_set_power_state() (drivers/pci/pci-acpi.c) for the parts only firmware can do, such as cutting power for D3cold.

acpi_dev_pm_attach() accepts almost any device with an ACPI companion. It skips ACPI fans, whose driver needs handling the generic domain cannot give, and, when several physical devices share one companion, it attaches only the first, so that the firmware methods do not run twice. It installs a notify handler (acpi_add_pm_notifier()) so that a wakeup signalled through the companion's GPE resumes the device, and with PD_FLAG_ATTACH_POWER_ON it puts the device into D0 and disarms any wakeup the firmware left armed. Its ->detach(), acpi_dev_pm_detach(), removes the notifier and, when asked to power off, hides any PM QoS limits exposed to user space and moves the device to the lowest D-state allowed in S0. A few other users install a dev_pm_domain of their own with dev_pm_domain_set(): drivers/acpi/x86/lpss.c gives the I2C, SPI, and UART controllers of Intel's Low Power Subsystem acpi_lpss_pm_domain, which saves and restores their private registers around ACPI's own callbacks; drivers/gpu/vga/vga_switcheroo.c wraps the discrete GPU of a hybrid-graphics laptop so that its runtime suspend also cuts the GPU's power through the platform handler; and 32-bit OMAP keeps its omap_device_pm_domain. Everything else on device-tree systems uses genpd.

Generic PM Domains

Device-tree platforms have no firmware methods to call; the knowledge of which devices share an island is in the power-domains properties of the device tree, and switching an island is the job of a provider driver for the SoC's power controller. Generic PM domains (genpd), merged in 3.1 and moved from drivers/base/power/ to drivers/pmdomain/core.c in 6.8, are the common implementation of such domains. A provider embeds a struct generic_pm_domain per island, fills in ->power_on() and ->power_off(), optionally a list of domain idle states (struct genpd_power_state, each with power_off_latency_ns, power_on_latency_ns, and residency_ns), and calls pm_genpd_init(), which sets the domain's dev_pm_domain operations to genpd's own (genpd_runtime_suspend(), genpd_prepare(), genpd_suspend_noirq(), and so on). Domains nest: a subdomain's struct gpd_link to its parent makes the parent count it in sd_count while it is on, so that a parent is never powered off under a powered child. Chapter 13 describes the provider and consumer APIs; here we follow the power-off decision.

A provider shapes its domain's behavior with flags in generic_pm_domain->flags, set before pm_genpd_init() (Table 21-7, from include/linux/pm_domain.h). The last argument of pm_genpd_init(), is_off, gives the domain's state at registration, because a provider usually reads it from hardware that the bootloader has already configured; a domain that starts on and has no devices yet is a candidate for power-off later, as described below.

The flags also choose genpd's lock, because they decide where the domain can be switched. genpd_lock_init() gives an ordinary domain a mutex, so that its provider may sleep—to talk to a power-management controller over a mailbox, for example—and gives an IRQ-safe domain a spinlock_t. A CPU domain gets a raw_spinlock_t (6.12), because it is switched from the idle loop, where even a PREEMPT_RT sleeping spinlock may not be taken. All callers go through genpd_lock() and genpd_lock_nested(), which dispatch through genpd->lock_ops; the nested form, with the depth in the hierarchy as its lockdep subclass, lets a child domain's lock be held while its parent's is taken. A device marked with pm_runtime_irq_safe() in a domain that is not IRQ-safe keeps that domain on permanently, because genpd cannot take the mutex in the atomic context in which the device suspends.

Attaching a device creates its per-domain bookkeeping. genpd_add_device() allocates a struct generic_pm_domain_data, reached from the device as dev_gpd_data(dev), holding the device's performance-state vote, its governor timing data, and its hardware-mode and always-on settings (§6.3). It points dev->pm_domain at the domain's dev_pm_domain and adds the device to genpd->dev_list. If the device-tree node of a single-domain device has a required-opps entry, the device starts with that performance state as a default vote. The domain is powered on before the driver probes, and genpd's ->sync() callback, run after probe, queues a power-off check so that the domain can go down again if the new driver leaves its device suspended.

When a device in a domain runtime-suspends, the PM core calls genpd_runtime_suspend(). It asks the domain governor whether the device itself may suspend (->suspend_ok(), which checks the device's PM QoS resume latency against the measured cost of suspending and resuming it), runs the driver's callback, stops the device's clocks if the domain manages them, and then calls genpd_power_off() on the domain: 

/* drivers/pmdomain/core.c (abridged) */
static void genpd_power_off(struct generic_pm_domain *genpd, bool one_dev_on,
			    unsigned int depth)
{
	...
	if (!genpd_status_on(genpd) || genpd->prepared_count > 0 ||
	    genpd_is_always_on(genpd) || genpd_is_rpm_always_on(genpd) ||
	    genpd->stay_on || atomic_read(&genpd->sd_count) > 0)
		return;
	...
	list_for_each_entry(pdd, &genpd->dev_list, list_node) {
		if (!pm_runtime_suspended(pdd->dev) ||
			irq_safe_dev_in_sleep_domain(pdd->dev, genpd))
			not_suspended++;
		...
	}

	if (not_suspended > 1 || (not_suspended == 1 && !one_dev_on))
		return;

	if (genpd->gov && genpd->gov->power_down_ok) {
		if (!genpd->gov->power_down_ok(&genpd->domain))
			return;
	}
	...
	if (_genpd_power_off(genpd, true)) {
		genpd->states[genpd->state_idx].rejected++;
		return;
	}

	genpd->status = GENPD_STATE_OFF;
	genpd_update_accounting(genpd);
	genpd->states[genpd->state_idx].usage++;

	list_for_each_entry(link, &genpd->child_links, child_node) {
		genpd_sd_counter_dec(link->parent);
		genpd_lock_nested(link->parent, depth + 1);
		genpd_power_off(link->parent, false, depth + 1);
		genpd_unlock(link->parent);
	}
}

The first test lists the reasons a domain stays on regardless of its devices: a system transition is in progress (prepared_count), the provider marked it always on, or it was left on by the bootloader and some consumer has not yet probed (stay_on, 6.17). Then every device must be runtime-suspended, except that the device whose suspend triggered the call (one_dev_on) is still in RPM_SUSPENDING. The governor gets a veto and chooses the domain state, the provider's ->power_off() does the work, and the decision propagates to the parent domains, each of which may now be idle. Powering on runs in the opposite direction: genpd_runtime_resume() calls genpd_power_on(), which powers parents on first, recursively, then the domain itself, before it restarts the device and calls the driver's resume callback.

Domains that are already on when their provider registers raise a boot-time problem. The bootloader may have left the display or a storage controller running in such a domain, and if genpd powered it off as soon as it saw no attached devices, the hardware would die under a driver that simply had not probed yet. Since 6.17 genpd solves this with the driver core's sync_state() mechanism, which fires for a supplier once every consumer that the device tree links to it has probed (Chapter 13, §3.3). Each genpd is now a device of its own, registered on the genpd_provider bus, and pm_genpd_init() sets stay_on for an OF domain that starts on, unless the provider passed GENPD_FLAG_NO_STAY_ON. When the provider is registered, of_genpd_add_provider_simple() or of_genpd_add_provider_onecell() installs a sync-state handler. If the provider's device-tree node belongs to a device with a driver, the handler goes into that driver, unless it already has a sync_state() of its own; if no device owns the node, as for a provider registered from early platform code, the genpd device takes over the node and genpd's own provider driver receives the callback. The handler clears stay_on and tries a power-off of each of the provider's domains. A provider with special needs sets GENPD_FLAG_NO_SYNC_STATE and calls of_genpd_sync_state() itself. Independently of all this, genpd_power_off_unused(), a late_initcall_sync, queues a power-off check for every domain, so that a domain that has no stay_on and whose devices never claimed it still goes down after boot; the pd_ignore_unused boot parameter suppresses it for debugging, and fw_devlink.sync_state=timeout makes the driver core give up waiting for consumers that never probe. The first 6.17 version cleared stay_on in genpd_power_off_unused() on non-OF systems and skipped that function on OF ones; regressions on several boards, a boot lockup among them, led to the present arrangement in 6.17-rc7, together with the NO_STAY_ON opt-out.

System sleep takes a separate path. Runtime power-off is blocked while prepared_count is nonzero, and instead genpd_finish_suspend(), called from the domain's ->suspend_noirq(), counts each device in suspended_count; when all of a domain's devices have been counted, genpd_sync_power_off() switches the domain off, defaulting to its deepest state. A device on the wakeup path in a domain flagged GENPD_FLAG_ACTIVE_WAKEUP keeps its domain powered, so that the device can still signal a wakeup. The test is device_awake_path(), which reads the power.wakeup_path mark that the PM core sets on wakeup-enabled devices and their ancestors and that a driver can also set with device_set_awake_path() for a device that must stay powered in sleep. Since 6.19 there is an exception: a device marked with device_set_out_band_wakeup() wakes the system through logic in an always-on domain, such as a GPIO or a wakeup controller, so genpd powers its own domain off as if wakeup were disabled (§3.2). Nor is the deepest state automatic any more when the domain has a governor: before switching a domain off for system sleep, genpd_sync_power_off() asks the governor's ->system_power_down_ok() (6.19), which the CPU governor uses to honor user space's wakeup-latency limit (§6.4). Finally, code that runs after the noirq phase, where genpd's callbacks no longer apply, can switch a device's domain directly with dev_pm_genpd_suspend() and dev_pm_genpd_resume(). The Renesas timer drivers in drivers/clocksource/ use them from their syscore-time suspend, and so does the PSCI cpuidle driver for CPUs.

Multiple Domains and Consumer Controls

A struct device has a single pm_domain pointer, but on large SoCs one block often needs several resources at once. A video codec may need its own gated island plus a shared "multimedia" rail whose voltage scales, and a remote processor may need its core, its memories, and a bus domain. genpd_dev_pm_attach() therefore attaches automatically only when the node has exactly one power-domains entry, and leaves the rest to the driver. dev_pm_domain_attach_by_id() and dev_pm_domain_attach_by_name() (the latter matching power-domain-names) create a virtual device named genpd:index:device on genpd's genpd bus, attach it to the chosen domain, and enable runtime PM for it. Most drivers instead call devm_pm_domain_attach_list(), which does this for every listed domain and, unless told otherwise, links each virtual device to the real one with a DL_FLAG_PM_RUNTIME | DL_FLAG_STATELESS device link. Runtime PM then flows through the links (§5.2): resuming the real device resumes each virtual device, which powers its domain on, and suspending it lets each domain go. The struct dev_pm_domain_attach_data argument names the domains and takes PD_FLAG_NO_DEV_LINK to skip the links for a driver that wants to switch domains separately, PD_FLAG_DEV_LINK_ON to start with the domains powered, and, since 6.13, PD_FLAG_REQUIRED_OPP to bind each virtual device to the matching required-opps entry of the real device's OPP table, so that a rate change sets every domain's performance state at once. A fix backported to 7.0.7 made genpd_dev_pm_detach() disable runtime PM on a virtual device before removing it; without it a detached virtual device could still be runtime-suspended through genpd and crash, or keep a stale performance-state vote.

genpd also gives a consumer driver a few direct controls, declared in include/linux/pm_domain.h:

  • dev_pm_genpd_set_hwmode() and dev_pm_genpd_get_hwmode() (6.11) switch a device's domain between software control and hardware control, in which the hardware itself gates the domain when the block is idle. The provider implements it through ->set_hwmode_dev(); Qualcomm's GDSC power switches (drivers/clk/qcom/gdsc.c) do, and the Venus and Iris video codec drivers switch their cores' GDSCs between the two modes.
  • dev_pm_genpd_rpm_always_on() (6.15) keeps the domain on at run time while the device is runtime-suspended, for a device whose suspended state still needs the domain's power: the Rockchip UFS host sets it for the runtime-PM levels that keep the link powered, and the RK3576 SD/eMMC host because its domain does not come back correctly once it has been off. The flag GENPD_FLAG_RPM_ALWAYS_ON does the same for a whole domain.
  • dev_pm_genpd_is_on() (6.17) reports whether the domain is on; the i.MX remote-processor driver uses it to see whether the bootloader left the coprocessor running.
  • dev_pm_genpd_add_notifier() (5.10) delivers GENPD_NOTIFY_PRE_OFF, GENPD_NOTIFY_OFF, GENPD_NOTIFY_PRE_ON, and GENPD_NOTIFY_ON around the domain's transitions, so that a driver can save state before power is cut or restore it after. Together with dev_pm_genpd_synced_poweroff() (6.3), which asks the provider to wait until the next power-off has really happened, the Adreno GPU driver uses it to guarantee a full power collapse, and hence a clean reset, when recovering from a GPU hang.
  • dev_pm_genpd_set_next_wakeup() (5.12) and dev_pm_genpd_get_next_hrtimer() (6.2) feed the governor's residency calculation and expose its result (§6.4).

Domain Governors and Performance States

A domain's off state is worth entering only if it lasts long enough to repay the energy spent switching, and only if the time to switch back does not break anyone's latency constraint. The governors in drivers/pmdomain/governor.c make that call. simple_qos_governor walks the domain's idle states from deepest to shallowest and picks the first whose power_off_latency_ns + power_on_latency_ns fits within the tightest PM QoS resume latency of the devices and subdomains in it; with GENPD_FLAG_MIN_RESIDENCY (5.12) it also requires that the time until the next wakeup announced by a consumer with dev_pm_genpd_set_next_wakeup() exceeds the state's power_off_latency_ns + residency_ns. pm_domain_always_on_gov is for domains that must stay up at run time: pm_genpd_init() sets GENPD_FLAG_RPM_ALWAYS_ON for a domain that uses it, so the domain is never powered off by runtime PM but can still be switched off during system suspend.

The most interesting governor is pm_domain_cpu_gov (5.2), used for domains that contain CPUs. On ARM64, a cluster of cores and its shared L2 cache or interconnect can be powered off when every core in it is idle, and PSCI 1.0 lets the kernel take that decision in OS-initiated (OSI) mode, in which the last CPU to go idle tells firmware the cluster state as well, rather than leaving the firmware to coordinate it. drivers/cpuidle/cpuidle-psci-domain.c (5.6) builds a genpd per cluster from the device tree's power-domains hierarchy with GENPD_FLAG_CPU_DOMAIN and GENPD_FLAG_IRQ_SAFE, and the PSCI cpuidle driver (§8.3) attaches each CPU to it through a virtual device. When a CPU enters its deepest idle state it calls pm_runtime_put_sync_suspend() on that device; if it is the last CPU in the cluster, genpd's power-off path runs and cpu_power_down_ok() looks at the next hrtimer expiry of every CPU in the domain and the CPU latency QoS limits to choose a cluster state, which psci_pd_power_off() records for the CPU to pass to firmware in its CPU_SUSPEND call. The same device model that gates a video codec thus gates a CPU cluster from the idle loop, with interrupts off.

cpu_power_down_ok() has grown with the hardware it serves. It first runs the ordinary QoS check over the domain's devices and then takes the tightest of three latency limits: the global CPU latency QoS limit (/dev/cpu_dma_latency, §8.4); since 6.16, each CPU's own resume-latency constraint, whose absence had let a cluster enter a state slower to leave than a CPU's limit allowed; and since 6.19, the system-wakeup limit that user space sets through /dev/cpu_wakeup_latency. It finds the earliest next hrtimer among the domain's online CPUs and stores it, so that dev_pm_genpd_get_next_hrtimer() can hand it to Qualcomm's RPMh driver (drivers/soc/qcom/rpmh-rsc.c), which programs the SoC-wide wakeup timer before the last CPU goes down. Then, starting from the state that the QoS check allowed, it picks the deepest state whose residency_ns + power_off_latency_ns fits the predicted idle time and whose entry-plus-exit latency fits the limit. Since 6.19 it finally refuses if cpus_peek_for_pending_ipi() finds an IPI already queued for a CPU in the domain, which would wake that CPU at once and waste the cluster transition. During s2idle, cpu_system_power_down_ok() (6.19) ignores timers and takes the deepest state within the system-wakeup limit. PREEMPT_RT needs special handling, because the runtime PM calls made on every idle entry take locks that sleep on RT. Since 6.3 cpuidle-psci-domain therefore marks its domains GENPD_FLAG_RPM_ALWAYS_ON on RT and the PSCI driver skips the per-entry runtime PM calls, and since 6.12 the raw spinlock of CPU domains lets s2idle and suspend-to-RAM still reach cluster states on RT, through dev_pm_genpd_suspend().

Not every power resource is on or off. Many SoCs scale the voltage of a domain with the frequency of the fastest device in it, and devices vote for a minimum performance state of their domain—an abstract level that the provider maps to a voltage or to a request to a power-management coprocessor. A consumer sets its vote with dev_pm_genpd_set_performance_state() (4.15), or more often implicitly: when the OPP table of a device (§7.3) lists a required-opps entry for its domain, dev_pm_opp_set_rate() sets the domain performance state along with the clock. genpd aggregates the votes by taking the maximum over the domain's devices and subdomains (_genpd_reeval_performance_state()), calls the provider's ->set_performance_state(), and propagates the result to parent domains. A runtime-suspended device's vote is dropped and restored on resume. Qualcomm's drivers/pmdomain/qcom/rpmhpd.c, which turns these levels into requests to the RPMh power controller, is the heaviest user; on SCMI platforms drivers/pmdomain/arm/scmi_pm_domain.c and scmi_perf_domain.c expose the firmware's power and performance domains as genpds.

The performance states themselves are described like a device's operating points. When a provider that implements ->set_performance_state() registers, genpd reads an operating-points-v2 table for each domain from the provider's node into the OPP library, attached to the genpd's own device, and a consumer's required-opps entry points into that table. A provider whose levels come from firmware instead sets GENPD_FLAG_OPP_TABLE_FW (6.7) and fills the table itself; the SCMI performance-domain driver and MediaTek's GPU domain driver do.

All of this state is visible under /sys/kernel/debug/pm_genpd/ when CONFIG_DEBUG_FS is set. pm_genpd_summary prints every domain with its status (with the index of the idle state it is in when off), its subdomains, and its performance state, and under each domain the attached devices with their runtime PM status, their performance-state votes, and whether software or hardware controls them. Each domain also has a directory with current_state, sub_domains, devices, active_time, total_idle_time, perf_state, and idle_states. The last lists, for each idle state, the time spent in it, its usage count, and its rejected count of power-offs the provider refused. Since 6.14 the states carry the names given by idle-state-name in the device tree, and since 6.16 two more columns rate the CPU governor's choices: above counts entries that ended before the state's target residency, so that a shallower state would have been better, and below counts entries long enough for the next deeper state. These are the same diagnostics that cpuidle gives per CPU (§8), lifted to the cluster.

CPU Frequency Scaling

A busy CPU's power is dominated by the CV2f term of §1.1, and every modern processor can trade speed for energy by lowering its clock and, with it, its voltage. The cpufreq subsystem (drivers/cpufreq/) decides how fast each CPU runs. It separates three concerns: a core that holds the policy objects and arbitrates limits, governors that choose a frequency from load, and scaling drivers that know how to program a particular processor. On many current CPUs, the driver hands most of the decision to the hardware itself.

Policies and the cpufreq Core

CPUs that share a clock must change frequency together, so the unit of control is not a CPU but a policy, struct cpufreq_policy in include/linux/cpufreq.h, one per frequency domain. Its related_cpus mask names every CPU in the domain and cpus the online ones; cpuinfo.min_freq and cpuinfo.max_freq are the hardware limits, min and max the current policy limits, and cur the last frequency set, all in kHz. freq_table lists the discrete frequencies for drivers that have them, and governor and governor_data attach the active governor. On x86 each CPU is usually its own policy, because each core can request its own P-state; on ARM64 big.LITTLE systems a policy typically covers a cluster. Each policy appears in sysfs as /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpufreq/policyN/, with scaling_governor, scaling_min_freq, scaling_max_freq, scaling_cur_freq, scaling_driver, and their relatives, and with a cpufreq symlink to it from each CPU's directory.

Only one scaling driver can be registered at a time; a second cpufreq_register_driver() fails with -EEXIST, which is why intel_pstate and acpi-cpufreq, for example, never coexist. Registration checks that the driver is coherent (it must have ->init() and ->verify(), exactly one of the ->setpolicy() and target styles, ->online() only together with ->offline(), and ->adjust_perf() only together with ->fast_switch()), then attaches the core to the CPU bus as a subsystem interface and installs a dynamic CPU hotplug state, "cpufreq:online", so that policies follow CPUs in and out. When a CPU comes online and its domain already has an active policy, the CPU is simply added to cpus. Otherwise cpufreq_policy_online() (split out of cpufreq_online() in 6.16) builds the policy under its write lock: the driver's ->init() fills in cpus, cpuinfo, and freq_table; the core validates and sorts the table, copies cpus into related_cpus, adds the two sysfs frequency-QoS requests described below, reads the current frequency through ->get(), creates the sysfs directory and the statistics table, lets the driver register an Energy Model through ->register_em() (§9.1), and finally starts a governor: the one the policy last used, or else the default chosen at boot with cpufreq.default_governor= (5.9) or at build time with a CONFIG_CPU_FREQ_DEFAULT_GOV_* option. When the last CPU of a domain goes offline the policy becomes inactive rather than disappearing. The governor is shut down and its name saved in last_governor; a driver that provides ->offline() does a light-weight teardown so that ->online() can restore the policy quickly, and other drivers get ->exit() now and a full ->init() later. The struct cpufreq_policy, its sysfs directory, and its QoS requests survive until the driver is unregistered, so limits written by an administrator outlast hotplug. Booting with cpufreq.off=1 stops any scaling driver from registering at all.

Many parties want to limit a policy's range: the administrator through sysfs, the thermal framework when the chip is hot (§9.2), platform firmware through ACPI _PPC notifications, drivers that need a minimum speed. Rather than letting each overwrite min and max, the core aggregates them with frequency QoS (freq_qos_add_request() in kernel/power/qos.c): every party holds a struct freq_qos_request on the policy's constraints, the effective minimum is the largest of the minimum requests, the effective maximum the smallest of the maximum requests, and a change in either notifies the core. The sysfs files are just two more requests, policy->min_freq_req and policy->max_freq_req. cpufreq_set_policy() reads the aggregated values, lets the driver adjust them in ->verify(), stores the result, reports the reduced capacity to the scheduler with cpufreq_update_pressure(), and then either hands the new limits to the driver or tells the governor about them. Figure 21-6 shows how these pieces fit.

Limits also change without anyone writing a request. Platform firmware can lower the ceiling with an ACPI _PPC notification, or report that the highest available performance level has changed. The ACPI processor driver handles the first by updating a frequency-QoS request of its own and both by calling cpufreq_update_limits(). That function invokes the driver's ->update_limits() callback (which receives the policy since 6.16) or, for drivers without one, cpufreq_policy_refresh(), which re-reads the current frequency and re-applies the policy with cpufreq_set_policy(). intel_pstate uses the callback to re-read the HWP capabilities, and handles the HWP interrupt that signals a change of guaranteed performance in its own work item. Other kernel code can follow all of this through the notifier chains registered with cpufreq_register_notifier(): CPUFREQ_TRANSITION_NOTIFIER for frequency changes and CPUFREQ_POLICY_NOTIFIER for policies being created and removed. The trace events power:cpu_frequency and power:cpu_frequency_limits record the same changes for tracing (Chapter 29, §7).

A ->setpolicy() driver receives only the limits and a coarse preference (performance or powersave) and lets the processor choose speeds within them; such a driver has no governor in the usual sense. A ->target_index() driver is told which entry of its frequency table to switch to, after the core has resolved a governor's target frequency to an index; the call happens in process context under the policy's rwsem, may sleep (to wait for a regulator or a firmware message), and is bracketed by cpufreq_freq_transition_begin() and cpufreq_freq_transition_end(), which send the CPUFREQ_PRECHANGE and CPUFREQ_POSTCHANGE notifications that let other code (the x86 TSC calibration on old machines, for instance) react. The older ->target() callback takes a frequency and a relation instead of an index. ->fast_switch() (4.7) is an optional extra for drivers whose hardware can be reprogrammed with a register write: it must not sleep, and it is called directly from the scheduler's context, bypassing the policy lock and the notifiers. ->adjust_perf() (5.11) goes one step further and passes abstract performance levels—a minimum, a target, and the CPU's capacity—to drivers whose hardware interface is not in kHz at all, such as Intel HWP and ACPI CPPC. It receives the policy rather than a CPU number since the 7.0.10 stable update, because looking the policy up with cpufreq_cpu_get() can sleep on PREEMPT_RT and the callback runs with the runqueue lock held.

Drivers with discrete operating points describe them in a struct cpufreq_frequency_table array: each entry has a frequency in kHz, a driver_data word the core ignores (often a register value or an OPP index), and flags. The array ends at CPUFREQ_TABLE_END; entries set to CPUFREQ_ENTRY_INVALID are skipped; and CPUFREQ_BOOST_FREQ marks entries above the nominal maximum (§7.4). The two lists appear in sysfs as scaling_available_frequencies and scaling_boost_frequencies. When the policy comes online cpufreq_table_validate_and_sort() derives cpuinfo.min_freq and cpuinfo.max_freq from the table and records in freq_table_sorted whether it is ascending, descending, or unsorted, so that lookups can stop early. A lookup takes a relation: CPUFREQ_RELATION_L asks for the lowest frequency at or above the target, CPUFREQ_RELATION_H for the highest at or below it, and CPUFREQ_RELATION_C for the closest. Since 5.16 any of them can be combined with CPUFREQ_RELATION_E, which asks for an efficient frequency. When the Energy Model of the policy's CPUs shows that some operating point costs at least as much energy for the same work as a higher one, em_cpufreq_update_efficiencies() marks it CPUFREQ_INEFFICIENT_FREQ, and an _E lookup skips it, falling back to an ordinary lookup only if no efficient frequency fits within the policy limits. schedutil resolves its targets with CPUFREQ_RELATION_LE through cpufreq_driver_resolve_freq(), which also caches the result so the driver need not search the table again; ondemand and conservative use the _E forms too.

The driver also declares, in cpuinfo.transition_latency, how long a frequency change takes in nanoseconds. Governors use it to decide how often a change is worth attempting: cpufreq_policy_transition_delay_us() returns the driver's explicit transition_delay_us if it has one, otherwise the latency plus 50%, otherwise 1 ms. For years, drivers that did not know their latency set it to CPUFREQ_ETERNAL. Once 6.9 stopped capping the derived delay, that value turned ondemand's 10 ms sampling interval into more than six seconds on the platforms that used it, and 6.18 removed the constant: such drivers now use CPUFREQ_DEFAULT_TRANSITION_LATENCY_NS, 1 ms. The governors available in 7.0 are listed in Table 21-10. The two static governors simply pin the frequency to a limit; the userspace governor exposes scaling_setspeed for a daemon to write. A governor is a struct cpufreq_governor with five operations. ->init() and ->exit() allocate and free its per-policy data and sysfs tunables, ->start() and ->stop() install and remove its scheduler hook, and ->limits() reacts to a change of policy->min or policy->max, which the static governors do by jumping straight to the new limit. Writing a name to scaling_governor calls cpufreq_set_policy(); if the governor is unchanged, only ->limits() runs, and otherwise the old governor is stopped and exited and the new one initialized and started, with the old one restored if that fails. Two governor flags matter to drivers. CPUFREQ_GOV_DYNAMIC_SWITCHING, set by ondemand, conservative, and schedutil, marks governors that change frequency on their own, which a driver flagged CPUFREQ_NO_AUTO_DYNAMIC_SWITCHING (Table 21-9) refuses. CPUFREQ_GOV_STRICT_TARGET, set by performance, powersave, and userspace, copies into policy->strict_target the fact that the requested frequency is meant exactly; intel_cpufreq in passive mode then sets the HWP maximum, as well as the minimum, to the target instead of leaving the processor room to go higher.

The tunables of ondemand and conservative live in /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpufreq/ondemand/ (or conservative/), shared by all policies, unless the driver sets CPUFREQ_HAVE_GOVERNOR_PER_POLICY, in which case each policyN/ gets its own copy. sampling_rate, in microseconds, defaults to the larger of two scheduler ticks and the policy's transition delay. ondemand adds up_threshold (80% by default), sampling_down_factor, which multiplies the sampling interval while the CPU runs at full speed so that it does not drop out of it too readily, ignore_nice_load, which counts time spent on niced tasks as idle, io_is_busy, which counts I/O-wait time as busy and defaults to on for Intel x86 processors, and powersave_bias, in tenths of a percent, which lowers every chosen frequency by that fraction. conservative adds down_threshold (20%) and freq_step (5%). None of this applies to schedutil, whose only tunable is rate_limit_us, described below.

schedutil (kernel/sched/cpufreq_schedutil.c) needs no sampling, because the scheduler already knows how busy each CPU is: it maps the CPU's utilization, including its utilization-clamping limits, to a frequency with 25% headroom. Chapter 7 describes that arithmetic and why EAS depends on it (Chapter 7, §10.7); what matters here is how the result is applied. For a policy with a single CPU:

sugov_update_single_common() applies the rate limit: sugov_should_update_freq() refuses to re-evaluate more often than the rate_limit_us tunable, whose default is the policy's transition delay from cpufreq_policy_transition_delay_us() (§7.1). The hook runs with the runqueue lock held and interrupts off, so a driver that can fast-switch is called right there; that is the common case on x86 and on ARM64 SoCs with hardware-controlled DVFS such as qcom-cpufreq-hw.c. Otherwise the change is deferred: sugov_deferred_update() queues an irq_work, which wakes a per-policy kthread named sugov:N to call __cpufreq_driver_target() in process context. That thread runs as a SCHED_DEADLINE task with the special SCHED_FLAG_SUGOV flag, so a frequency change cannot be starved by the very load that made it necessary. Its nominal reservation is 1 ms in every 10 ms, but the flag exempts it from deadline bandwidth accounting. Since 6.14 it is permanently bound to the policy's CPUs only when the hardware requires requests to come from one of them; otherwise it starts with that affinity but may be moved. For drivers with ->adjust_perf(), sugov_update_single_perf() skips the frequency calculation entirely and passes the utilization straight to the driver as performance levels. Policies that span several CPUs use sugov_update_shared(), which takes the highest utilization of the policy's CPUs under a lock.

Several refinements sit around that core. A task that wakes after waiting for I/O has low utilization, because it spent its time blocked, yet running it slowly would delay the next I/O. The fair class therefore passes SCHED_CPUFREQ_IOWAIT with such wakeups, and sugov_iowait_boost() adds a boost that starts at IOWAIT_BOOST_MIN, one eighth of the CPU's capacity, doubles on each further I/O wakeup up to full capacity, halves at each update without one, and is dropped if a whole tick passes without a request, so sporadic I/O costs little energy. In the other direction, sugov_hold_freq() refuses to lower the frequency of a CPU that has not entered idle since the previous update (with NO_HZ, by comparing the idle counter), because a reduction while the CPU is still fully busy is likely premature; the check is skipped when uclamp caps the runqueue or when sched_ext runs every task, since a BPF scheduler sets its own targets. Two events bypass the rate limit. When the policy limits change, sugov_limits() sets limits_changed, and the next update recomputes the frequency at once; if the result equals the current one, the driver is not called unless it has CPUFREQ_NEED_UPDATE_LIMITS, a path whose memory ordering was corrected in 6.15. And when the SCHED_DEADLINE bandwidth on a CPU rises above what the last computation assumed, ignore_dl_rate_limit() forces an immediate update, because a deadline task cannot wait for the next window.

Finally, schedutil is a precondition for energy-aware scheduling, because EAS predicts energy from the frequencies schedutil would choose. Since 6.16 the scheduler asks cpufreq, through cpufreq_ready_for_eas(), whether every CPU of a root domain uses schedutil before it builds the perf domains, and schedutil's ->init() and ->exit() call em_rebuild_sched_domains() so that switching a policy's governor turns EAS on or off (Chapter 7, §10.2).

Scaling Drivers on x86_64 and ARM64

Table 21-11 lists the scaling drivers that matter on the two architectures. On x86 the trend of the last decade is to move the frequency decision into the processor, which can react within microseconds and sees microarchitectural information the kernel cannot. Intel's Hardware P-states (HWP), supported by intel_pstate since 3.19, let the OS write a minimum, a maximum, and an energy-performance preference (EPP) into MSR_HWP_REQUEST and leave the rest to the CPU; the driver's EPP values (performance, balance_performance, balance_power, power) appear in the per-policy energy_performance_preference attribute. intel_pstate registers one of two struct cpufreq_driver instances: intel_pstate, a ->setpolicy() driver used in active mode, where it implements its own “performance” and “powersave” algorithms; or intel_cpufreq, used in passive mode, which implements ->target(), ->fast_switch(), and (with HWP) ->adjust_perf() so that the generic governors, normally schedutil, can drive it. With HWP the default is active mode; without it, passive mode (since 5.8). The mode can be switched at run time by writing to /sys/devices/system/cpu/intel_pstate/status. amd-pstate (5.17) is the analogous driver for AMD's Collaborative Processor Performance Control (CPPC) interface, accessed through MSRs or, on older parts, ACPI shared memory. Its modes are passive (the amd-pstate driver with ->target(), ->fast_switch(), and ->adjust_perf()), active (the amd-pstate-epp ->setpolicy() driver, added in 6.3 and the default since X86_AMD_PSTATE_DEFAULT_MODE defaults to 3), and guided (6.4), in which the OS sets a range and the firmware chooses within it. Machines with neither fall back to acpi-cpufreq, which programs the P-states listed by the ACPI _PSS object.

Both x86 drivers have grown beyond frequency selection. intel_pstate takes its options from the intel_pstate= boot parameter (active, passive, disable, no_hwp, hwp_only, force, per_cpu_perf_limits, support_acpi_ppc, and no_cas) and exposes global controls in /sys/devices/system/cpu/intel_pstate/: status, no_turbo, max_perf_pct and min_perf_pct (limits applied to every policy), turbo_pct and num_pstates (read-only), and, with HWP in active mode, hwp_dynamic_boost, which raises the HWP minimum temporarily for tasks waking from I/O, much like schedutil's iowait boost. On hybrid processors with P-cores and E-cores, it reads each core's HWP highest performance and, since 6.12, reports the ratio to the scheduler as asymmetric CPU capacity, through arch_set_cpu_capacity(), so that the capacity-aware placement of Chapter 7, §10.2 works on x86 as it does on ARM64; since 7.0 this is done only when SMT is impossible, because with SMT siblings the capacities would be misleading, and intel_pstate=no_cas (6.15) turns it off. In 6.16 it went further and registers artificial energy-model perf domains for the core types, enabling EAS on hybrid laptops when the driver runs in passive mode under schedutil. amd-pstate, for its part, implements AMD preferred core (6.9): CPPC reports a per-core highest performance that firmware uses to rank the best cores of a package, and the driver hands the ranking to the scheduler with sched_set_itmt_core_prio() and re-ranks when firmware changes it, exposing it in each policy's amd_pstate_prefcore_ranking and amd_pstate_hw_prefcore. Since 6.17 it leaves ranking to the amd-hfi driver on processors whose hardware feedback interface classifies workloads. The active-mode default EPP is balance_performance since 6.14, and since 6.16 a minimum frequency set in the BIOS setup becomes the default minimum of each policy.

On ARM64 the picture depends on how the platform describes itself (Table 21-1). Servers that boot with ACPI use cppc_cpufreq (4.4), which reads the _CPC performance registers. Platforms with an SCMI-capable system control processor use scmi-cpufreq (4.17), sending performance-level requests to firmware, which owns the clocks and rails. The remaining device-tree platforms use cpufreq-dt, whose entire ->target_index() is a call into the OPP library (drivers/opp/, 2.6.37): 

/* drivers/cpufreq/cpufreq-dt.c */
static int set_target(struct cpufreq_policy *policy, unsigned int index)
{
	struct private_data *priv = policy->driver_data;
	unsigned long freq = policy->freq_table[index].frequency;

	return dev_pm_opp_set_rate(priv->cpu_dev, freq * 1000);
}

An operating performance point is a frequency together with the voltage, interconnect bandwidth, and PM domain performance state (§6.4) needed to run at it, described by an operating-points-v2 table in the device tree. dev_pm_opp_set_rate() finds the OPP for the requested frequency and _set_opp() applies it in a safe order: when scaling up, it raises the required domain performance states, the bandwidth votes, and the regulator voltages first and changes the clock last; when scaling down, it changes the clock first and lowers the rest afterward, so that the CPU never runs faster than its supply allows. The OPP library is not CPU-specific; GPU, memory-controller, and bus drivers use it too. Those devices are scaled by devfreq (drivers/devfreq/, 3.2), a smaller framework parallel to cpufreq with its own governors (simple_ondemand, performance, powersave, userspace, and passive, which follows another device's frequency) that usually take their load from hardware activity counters.

Platform quirks live in the OPP table rather than in the driver. A driver that needs more than one clock, regulators with unusual names, or OPP entries selected by silicon revision describes them once with dev_pm_opp_set_config(), whose struct dev_pm_opp_config carries clk_names, regulator_names, a prop_name suffix for opp-microvolt-<name> properties, a supported_hw mask matched against each OPP's opp-supported-hw, custom config_clks and config_regulators callbacks, and a required_dev for multi-domain parts; the Qualcomm, TI, Allwinner, and ST helper drivers read speed-bin fuses this way and then let cpufreq-dt do the rest. The firmware-driven drivers have their own extensions. cppc_cpufreq can hand control to the platform (6.16) through per-policy auto_select, auto_act_window, and energy_performance_preference_val files, the Arm-server counterpart of HWP, and in 7.0 it updates the frequency-invariance factor directly from the tick when the feedback counters are not behind a PCC mailbox, rather than from a kthread. qcom-cpufreq-hw receives an interrupt when the Limits Management hardware throttles a cluster and reports the capped frequency with arch_update_hw_pressure(), the hardware-pressure signal of §9.2. Two newer drivers fill gaps in the table: virtual-cpufreq (6.13) lets a guest with a qemu,virtual-cpufreq device pass its schedutil requests to the host through MMIO registers so that host DVFS can follow guest load, and the Rust driver drivers/cpufreq/rcpufreq_dt.rs (6.16) reimplements cpufreq-dt in Rust over the cpufreq, opp, and clk abstractions of rust/kernel/, one of the first Rust drivers in a core subsystem (Chapter 26, §8.1).

Frequency Invariance and Boost

The scheduler measures utilization as the fraction of time a CPU is busy, but a CPU that is 50% busy at half speed has done only a quarter of the work it could do at full speed. To keep PELT signals comparable across frequencies, the scheduler scales them by arch_scale_freq_capacity(), a per-CPU factor of current to maximum frequency in units of SCHED_CAPACITY_SCALE (1024), so that utilization measures work rather than time; this is frequency invariance, and schedutil's proportional mapping depends on it (see Chapter 7, §10.2 for CPU capacity). Where the factor comes from depends on who controls the frequency. When the kernel sets it, cpufreq updates the factor on every change through arch_set_freq_scale(), which on ARM64 is topology_set_freq_scale() in drivers/base/arch_topology.c. When the hardware chooses the frequency, only hardware counters can tell what it chose, and both architectures read them from the scheduler tick (both since 5.7). On x86, where the counters are used whichever driver is loaded, arch_scale_freq_tick() in arch/x86/kernel/cpu/aperfmperf.c reads the APERF counter, which counts at the actual clock, and the MPERF counter, which counts at a fixed reference, and derives the factor from the ratio of their increments. On ARM64, the Activity Monitors Unit (AMU) of Armv8.4 provides the same pair of core-cycle and constant-cycle counters, read by amu_scale_freq_tick() in arch/arm64/kernel/topology.c; when counters are available, topology_set_freq_scale() leaves the factor alone. cppc_cpufreq can derive the factor from the CPPC feedback counters on Arm servers without AMUs. The same counters back the cpuinfo_avg_freq attribute (6.15), which reports the average frequency the hardware actually delivered, as opposed to scaling_cur_freq, which reports the last request or a cached estimate.

Two details connect these counters to the rest of the kernel. First, invariance only works if the scheduler and the governor agree on which frequency corresponds to full capacity; if that reference moved, for example when boost is toggled and cpuinfo.max_freq changes, the same utilization would map to a different frequency. Since 6.8, arch_scale_freq_ref() returns the frequency against which each CPU's capacity was computed; on ARM64 it is topology_get_freq_ref(), recorded from the CPPC highest performance or from cpuinfo.max_freq when the first policy is created, while x86 does not define it. schedutil's get_capacity_ref_freq() uses that value, falling back to cpuinfo.max_freq on frequency-invariant systems and to the current frequency plus 25% on systems without invariance. The cpufreq core enables the scheduler's frequency invariance itself, through the cpufreq_freq_invariance static key, when it registers a driver that sets frequencies (not a ->setpolicy() driver), because only then does it know every change. Second, the same APERF/MPERF arithmetic answers “how fast is this CPU running?” for users. arch_freq_get_on_cpu() on x86 computes the average frequency over the last scheduler tick, falling back to cpufreq_quick_get() or the TSC frequency if the sample is more than 20 ms old (an idle CPU with the tick stopped), and its result is the “cpu MHz” line of /proc/cpuinfo; on ARM64 an AMU-based version (6.15) backs cpuinfo_avg_freq. Since 6.15, scaling_cur_freq uses it only when CONFIG_CPUFREQ_ARCH_CUR_FREQ (x86, default y) is set, so that the attribute can return to meaning “the requested frequency” now that cpuinfo_avg_freq exists.

Most processors can exceed their sustainable maximum frequency for short periods when thermal and electrical headroom allows (Intel Turbo Boost, AMD Precision Boost, and the boost OPPs of ARM SoCs). The cpufreq core models this as boost: frequencies above the nominal maximum that may be enabled or disabled globally through /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpufreq/boost or, since 6.6, per policy through policyN/boost, implemented by a driver's ->set_boost(). intel_pstate uses its own intel_pstate/no_turbo file instead. Disabling boost is a common way to make performance repeatable for benchmarking, and to reduce the power and heat of a laptop at a modest cost in burst performance.

The boost logic was reworked between 6.14 and 6.16 to cope with systems where only some policies can boost, such as big.LITTLE SoCs whose big cluster alone has boost OPPs. Each policy now has policy->boost_supported (6.15), which the frequency-table helpers set when the table contains CPUFREQ_BOOST_FREQ entries and which drivers such as acpi-cpufreq, amd-pstate, and cppc_cpufreq set from firmware. Writing the global boost file applies the state to every boost-capable active policy and fails with -EOPNOTSUPP if there are none; writing a per-policy boost file requires global boost to be enabled and the policy to support it. A newly created policy copies the global setting, while a policy that comes back online after CPU hotplug keeps its previous per-policy setting unless global boost has been turned off in the meantime. Table-based drivers use the generic cpufreq_boost_set_sw(), which recomputes cpuinfo.max_freq from the table with or without the boost entries and reapplies the policy's maximum-frequency QoS request so that the new ceiling takes effect, and acpi-cpufreq re-applies the boost state on resume (6.15), because firmware may have reset it.

CPU Idle

A CPU with nothing to run is idle far more often than it is busy, so how it sleeps matters as much for battery life as how fast it runs. Every processor offers a menu of idle states, called C-states on x86 and idle or low-power states on ARM, ranging from a light instruction-level wait that ends within a microsecond to deep states that flush caches, turn off the core's power rail, and take hundreds of microseconds to leave. A deeper state saves more power but only pays off if the CPU stays in it long enough. The cpuidle subsystem (drivers/cpuidle/), added in 2.6.24, makes the choice every time a CPU goes idle. As with cpufreq, a governor predicts and a driver acts.

The Idle Loop and the cpuidle Core

When the scheduler finds no runnable task, it switches to the CPU's idle task (Chapter 3, §11.5), which spends its life in do_idle() in kernel/sched/idle.c. The function calls tick_nohz_idle_enter() and then loops while need_resched() is false. Each pass disables interrupts, lets an offline CPU leave through arch_cpu_idle_dead(), and then either polls, when idle polling is forced or the tick-broadcast device has just expired, or calls cpuidle_idle_call(). When a task becomes runnable, the loop ends, tick_nohz_idle_exit() restarts the tick if it was stopped, and the idle task calls schedule_idle(). Figure 21-7 shows one trip through this loop.

cpuidle_idle_call() is where the generic idle loop meets cpuidle. If no cpuidle driver is registered, or cpuidle is disabled with cpuidle.off=1, it falls back to default_idle_call(), which runs the architecture's arch_cpu_idle(): HLT through default_idle() on x86 and WFI through cpu_do_idle() on ARM64. Otherwise it takes one of three paths, shown in the excerpt below. 

/* kernel/sched/idle.c (abridged) */
static void cpuidle_idle_call(bool stop_tick)
{
	struct cpuidle_device *dev = cpuidle_get_device();

		idle_call_stop_or_retain_tick(stop_tick);
		entered_state = call_cpuidle(drv, dev, next_state);
		cpuidle_reflect(dev, entered_state);
	} else {
		idle_call_stop_or_retain_tick(stop_tick);
		call_cpuidle(drv, dev, 0);
	}

	__current_set_polling();
	...
}

The first path is suspend-to-idle. While s2idle_enter() in kernel/power/suspend.c holds the system in s2idle (§2.2), idle_should_enter_s2idle() is true. Every CPU then skips the governor and asks for the deepest state whose exit latency fits within the system wakeup latency limit, a PM QoS constraint added in 6.19 (§8.4). call_cpuidle_s2idle() uses the state's ->enter_s2idle() callback, which runs with the tick and possibly all of timekeeping suspended, so it must not enable interrupts. The same branch serves idle injection (§9.2), which sets forced_idle_latency_limit_ns to force CPUs into a deep state regardless of the prediction. The second path is the normal one: the governor picks a state and reports through stop_tick whether the scheduler tick should be stopped, and after the CPU wakes, cpuidle_reflect() lets the governor compare the prediction with what happened. The third path, added in 7.0, skips the governor when the driver offers only one state, since there is nothing to choose.

The tick decision is the link to the tickless kernel of Chapter 6, §7.3. Stopping the periodic tick costs time on entry and exit, and is worth doing only if the CPU will sleep longer than a tick period. do_idle() therefore enters NO_HZ mode lazily and leaves the decision to the governor: idle_call_stop_or_retain_tick() calls tick_nohz_idle_stop_tick() when the governor said so, or when the tick is already stopped, and tick_nohz_idle_retain_tick() otherwise. In 7.0 the two paths that do not consult a governor, the no-driver fallback and the single-state path, follow one simple rule instead: do_idle() passes in got_tick, which records whether the tick woke the CPU on the previous pass, and the tick is stopped only then. Earlier kernels always stopped the tick on the no-driver path.

Several boot options bypass this machinery. nohlt, and idle=poll on x86, set cpu_idle_force_poll, so do_idle() spins in cpu_idle_poll() with interrupts enabled and never sleeps. This gives the lowest possible wakeup latency at the cost of a core that is always busy and hot, and kernel code that needs it briefly can call cpu_idle_poll_ctrl(true). On x86, any idle= option also sets boot_option_idle_override, which keeps intel_idle from loading. idle=halt leaves only HLT, and idle=nomwait lets acpi_idle run without its MWAIT entry method. cpuidle.off=1 disables cpuidle altogether, and cpuidle.governor= picks the governor at boot.

The states themselves are described by an array of struct cpuidle_state inside the driver's struct cpuidle_driver, ordered from shallowest to deepest. Each CPU has a struct cpuidle_device holding the per-state usage statistics.

Two numbers drive every decision. The exit latency is the worst-case time from a wakeup event to the CPU running code again. The target residency is the shortest stay for which the state saves energy overall, once the cost of entering and leaving it is counted. A state is worth choosing only if the predicted idle time exceeds its target residency and its exit latency is within every latency limit. A driver usually registers with cpuidle_register(), which calls cpuidle_register_driver() and then creates a struct cpuidle_device for each CPU in the driver's mask. __cpuidle_driver_init() converts each state's microsecond values to the nanosecond fields that the core uses, and sets drv->bctimer when any state stops the timer. The core pauses cpuidle with cpuidle_pause() while it takes secondary CPUs offline for suspend and hibernation. An offline CPU parks through cpuidle_play_dead(), which tries the states' ->enter_dead() callbacks from deepest to shallowest. On x86 native_play_dead() calls it before falling back to HLT, and since 6.15 intel_idle supplies ->enter_dead() so that offline CPUs sleep in the deepest MWAIT C-state.

cpuidle_enter_state() in drivers/cpuidle/cpuidle.c does the bookkeeping around the driver call. For a state with CPUIDLE_FLAG_TIMER_STOP it calls tick_broadcast_enter(), so that another timer will wake this CPU while its local timer is off (Chapter 6, §6.6), and if that fails it falls back to the deepest state that keeps the timer running. It records the state with sched_idle_set_state(), so the scheduler can prefer waking a CPU in a shallow state, and tells RCU that the CPU is idle with ct_cpuidle_enter() unless the driver does that itself. Then it calls ->enter(). On return, it measures the time actually spent and updates the state's counters, including above (the stay was shorter than the target residency, so a shallower state would have been better) and below (a deeper state would have fitted). The counters appear in /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuN/cpuidle/stateK/ next to name, latency, residency, usage, time, and a writable disable switch.

The rest of the per-state directory completes the picture. desc and power repeat the driver's description and power figure. rejected counts entries in which ->enter() returned an error, for example when PSCI firmware refused the request. default_status shows whether the state starts disabled because of CPUIDLE_FLAG_OFF. A s2idle/ subdirectory with its own usage and time counts only entries made during suspend-to-idle, so after an s2idle cycle it shows whether each CPU actually reached its deepest state while the system was asleep. Writing disable requires CAP_SYS_ADMIN and sets a "disabled by user" bit that is separate from the "disabled by driver" bit the driver sets with cpuidle_driver_state_disabled(), so neither undoes the other. One level up, /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuidle/ reports current_driver, available_governors, and current_governor. The power:cpu_idle tracepoint fires on entry to and exit from every state, and power:cpu_idle_miss on every stay counted in above or below, so perf or trace-cmd (Chapter 29, §7) can show each wrong guess the governor made.

State 0 deserves a closer look. poll_idle() in drivers/cpuidle/poll_state.c enables interrupts, sets the idle task's polling flag so that a remote wakeup needs no IPI, and spins on need_resched() with cpu_relax(). A wrong guess must not leave the CPU spinning for a long time, so every 200 iterations it compares the time spent with cpuidle_poll_time(). That limit is the target residency of the first enabled state of at least 10 µs, capped at a sixteenth of a tick, unless the governor has set dev->poll_limit_ns, as the haltpoll governor does. When the limit expires, the device's poll_time_limit flag tells the governor that the poll ended without a wakeup, and the idle loop runs again and chooses a hardware state.

Idle Governors

A governor's ->select() method must answer one question quickly, with interrupts disabled: how long will this CPU stay idle? The time to the next timer event is known, and tick_nohz_get_sleep_length() returns it. But many idle periods are cut short by device interrupts or by IPIs that wake a task, and those cannot be known in advance. The governors differ in how they guess. Table 21-13 lists them. The core picks the registered governor with the highest rating, and both cpuidle.governor= on the command line and /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuidle/current_governor can override it.

The menu governor (drivers/cpuidle/governors/menu.c) shows the general shape. menu_select() first reads the combined latency limit with cpuidle_governor_latency_req(). It then forms a prediction from the recent idle intervals with get_typical_interval(), and if that does not already say "short", it also asks for the next-timer distance and multiplies it by the correction factor of its distance bucket. The correction factors are running averages of how actual idle time compared with the timer distance in that bucket, updated by menu_update() after each wakeup. The governor walks the states from shallowest to deepest, stops at the first one whose exit latency exceeds the limit, and keeps the deepest one whose target residency is within the prediction. Finally, unless the tick is already stopped, it tells the caller to keep the tick running if the chosen state is the polling state or the predicted idle time is shorter than a tick, since stopping the tick would then cost more than it saves.

The menu governor has changed more since 6.12 than in the decade before. Until 6.13 it also counted the tasks waiting for I/O on the CPU: an iowait task selected a separate set of 6 correction factors, and a "performance multiplier" made deep states harder to reach. Iowait says nothing reliable about when the CPU will be woken, so 6.13 removed both, leaving 6 buckets indexed only by distance. In 6.15 get_typical_interval() began discarding outliers at both ends of the 8 samples rather than only the largest. When too few samples remain it still returns "no prediction", which lets a deep state be chosen; an attempt to use the remaining samples instead was reverted in 6.18, because it hurt processors whose maximum turbo frequency depends on how long their idle cores spend in deep states. A shortcut chooses state 0 at once when the latency limit is zero, or when the next timer or the limit rules out state 1. When the prediction is too short even for state 1, the loop may still replace the polling state with a physical state whose exit latency fits the prediction; since 6.19 it does so only for states with a target residency below 15 µs (RESIDENCY_THRESHOLD_NS). Without that limit, a pseries guest, whose only hardware state has a long residency, entered that state for idle periods far too short to pay for it. Since 7.0 the governor always asks for the next timer when the tick is already stopped. With no tick to rescue a short guess, it then uses the timer distance itself whenever the prediction is shorter than a tick.

The teo governor (drivers/cpuidle/governors/teo.c) is a response to the menu governor's weakness with frequent non-timer wakeups. It places each observed idle period into a bin for the state whose residency range contains it, and for each bin keeps two metrics that grow by a fixed step and decay geometrically: hits, where the CPU woke at about the time the next timer predicted, and intercepts, where something else woke it much earlier. Starting from the deepest enabled state, it looks for a shallower one when intercepts in the bins below that state account for more than half of the recent wakeups. Both governors try to keep the tick running for short predictions. A missed prediction is cheap while the tick is running, since it will wake the CPU from an over-deep choice within a tick; with the tick stopped, a CPU in a shallow state could wait a long time for its next timer.

teo has been reworked in nearly every release since 6.11. 6.11 removed two experiments: a separate "recent intercepts" metric, and the CPU-utilization check added in 6.3, which had capped the depth on busy CPUs. Reading the next timer with tick_nohz_get_sleep_length() is itself expensive, so since 6.14 teo skips it when at least half of the recent idle periods were short (the short_idles counter) or the latency limit is under 7.5 µs, and the candidate is already state 0 or a state with a target residency below 15 µs. 6.19 changed how wakeups by the tick itself are counted: when they make up more than two-thirds of all wakeups, teo records each one as a hit on the deepest state, which makes it more likely to stop the tick. 7.0 tightened the classification further. Any other tick wakeup counts as an intercept only if intercepts shorter than a tick (tick_intercepts) make up at least two-thirds of the wakeups, and as a hit otherwise, since the tick may simply have fired before an expected interrupt. Any other wakeup counts as a hit only if it came after the predicted timer event, not merely in the same bin. The search for a shallower state now starts at the state with the largest intercepts metric, and those same short intercepts keep the tick running when they dominate.

intel_idle, acpi_idle, and cpuidle-psci

On x86_64 Intel processors the driver is normally drivers/idle/intel_idle.c, added in 2.6.35. It enters every state with MWAIT, passing the C-state as a hint in EAX, and carries per-model tables of states with their latencies rather than relying on ACPI _CST; its header comment gives the aim as making Linux "more immune to ACPI BIOS bugs". For example, the Sapphire Rapids table spr_cstates lists C1 (exit latency 1 µs, target residency 1 µs), C1E (2 and 4 µs), and C6 (290 and 800 µs). For Intel processors without a table, it builds the list from _CST as long as they support MWAIT, and intel_idle.max_cstate=0 disables the driver. On other x86 processors, including AMD's, the generic ACPI driver acpi_idle (drivers/acpi/processor_idle.c) takes over. It reads _CST and enters each state by the method the table names: HLT, an I/O-port read, or a "functional fixed hardware" entry, which on x86 is MWAIT through acpi_processor_ffh_cstate_enter(). Both drivers put a busy-polling state 0 in front, set up by cpuidle_poll_state_init(), for idle periods too short for any hardware state.

In 7.0 intel_idle carries 27 state tables for more than 50 processor models, the newest being Granite Rapids (6.12), Granite Rapids-D (6.13), and the Clearwater Forest E-core server (6.14), which reuses the Sierra Forest table. Since 6.17 the _CST fallback is no longer limited to family 6 processors. On ten older server models, the Xeons from Sandy Bridge-EP to Ice Lake-SP and several Atom-based server parts (use_acpi in the model data), and on any model when intel_idle.use_acpi=1 is given, the built-in table is still used but _CST decides which states are on by default: a state the firmware does not list gets CPUIDLE_FLAG_OFF, unless it is marked CPUIDLE_FLAG_ALWAYS_ENABLE. no_acpi ignores _CST entirely, no_native (6.15) ignores the built-in table in favor of _CST, and states_off= is a bit mask of state indices to start disabled. 7.0 added intel_idle.table=, which changes the exit latency and target residency of named states, as in intel_idle.table=C1E::10,C6:150:600. The driver checks that the edited values still rise from state to state, that each residency is at least its latency, and that they stay below 5 ms and 100 ms, and ignores the whole string if any check fails. The same release dropped the old preferred_cstates parameter. On models that support it, the file /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuidle/intel_c1_demotion (6.16) turns the hardware's C1 auto-demotion on or off. When this feature is on, the processor turns a request for a deep C-state into C1 if it predicts a short stay.

On ARM64 with device tree, the driver is cpuidle-psci (drivers/cpuidle/cpuidle-psci.c, since 5.4). State 0 is always the architectural WFI. The deeper states come from idle-states nodes in the device tree, whose entry-latency-us, exit-latency-us, and min-residency-us properties are parsed by drivers/cpuidle/dt_idle_states.c, and whose arm,psci-suspend-param gives the 32-bit PSCI power-state parameter. Entering a state calls psci_cpu_suspend_enter() in drivers/firmware/psci/psci.c. For a retention state, where the core keeps its context, this is just the PSCI CPU_SUSPEND call. For a power-down state, it goes through the arm64 cpu_suspend(), which saves the CPU context so that execution can come back through the firmware's warm-boot entry point as if returning from the call. Because a power-down state loses CPU-local state such as the GIC CPU interface and the PMU, the driver first calls cpu_pm_enter() (kernel/cpu_pm.c), which runs the CPU PM notifiers that save it, and cpu_pm_exit() after waking.

ARM systems also power down whole clusters when all their CPUs are idle. When the device tree describes this hierarchy with power-domains, drivers/cpuidle/cpuidle-psci-domain.c creates a genpd for each cluster (§6.4), and cpuidle-psci attaches each CPU to it through a virtual device. Each idle entry then drops a runtime PM reference on that device with pm_runtime_put_sync_suspend(), as in the code below. When the last CPU of a cluster does so, genpd calls the domain's power-off callback, which records the composite cluster state that this CPU will pass to firmware. This is PSCI's OS-initiated mode, which the driver enables with psci_set_osi_mode() when the firmware supports it. During s2idle, runtime PM is disabled, so the driver uses dev_pm_genpd_suspend() instead.

Without OS-initiated mode, PSCI works in its default platform-coordinated mode. The device tree then lists the composite states, such as "core off and cluster off", directly as CPU states, and each CPU simply requests the state it wants. The firmware powers a cluster down only when every CPU in it has asked for a deep enough state. The kernel cannot see whether that happened, but needs no domain topology either, so cpuidle-psci-domain keeps its domains always on. In OS-initiated mode, only the deepest per-CPU state is routed through the domain path above; shallower states still call psci_cpu_suspend_enter() directly. Since 6.14 the cluster domains carry GENPD_FLAG_ACTIVE_WAKEUP, so during s2idle a cluster domain that also powers a device in the wakeup path stays on. On PREEMPT_RT kernels the runtime PM calls in the domain path take spinlocks, which sleep there and so cannot be taken in the idle loop with interrupts off. The cluster states were therefore disabled on RT in 6.3, and 6.12 brought them back for s2idle and suspend-to-RAM only; ordinary idle uses only the per-CPU states. For system suspend the driver registers syscore callbacks that move every CPU's virtual device through dev_pm_genpd_suspend() and dev_pm_genpd_resume(). The psci_domain_idle_enter and psci_domain_idle_exit tracepoints (6.15) record which composite state each CPU requested.

ARM64 servers booted with ACPI describe idle states in the _LPI (Low Power Idle) object instead, so acpi_idle drives them, and acpi_processor_ffh_lpi_enter() in drivers/acpi/arm64/cpuidle.c enters each one through the same psci_cpu_suspend_enter(). x86 KVM guests can use cpuidle-haltpoll (5.4), which pairs a polling state with the haltpoll governor, named in the driver's governor field so that its low rating does not matter. A vCPU first polls, and halts only if the poll window passes with no work, so a wakeup that arrives soon costs no HLT exit to the host and no IPI to bring the vCPU back. The governor adapts the window, stored in dev->poll_limit_ns, after every halt. If the vCPU slept longer than the window but no more than guest_halt_poll_ns (200 µs by default), it doubles the window, starting at 50 µs; if the vCPU slept longer still, it halves the window, and drops it to zero once it falls below 50 µs. The driver loads only when the host sets KVM_HINTS_REALTIME, meaning that each vCPU has a physical CPU to itself, so spinning takes no time from other guests, or when cpuidle_haltpoll.force=1 is given, and never when an idle= option is set. Only x86 selects ARCH_CPUIDLE_HALTPOLL in 7.0, so ARM64 guests rely on host-side halt polling after the WFI trap (Chapter 28, §7.4).

PM QoS and Latency Limits

Deep idle states save power at the cost of wakeup latency, and some workloads cannot afford it: an audio driver with a small buffer, a network card with strict response targets, or a real-time control loop. PM QoS (kernel/power/qos.c) lets such users state a limit and lets the governors respect it. A request is a struct pm_qos_request added to a constraint list, and the effective value is the minimum of all requests, kept in a priority list so that reading it is cheap.

Three limits apply to idle-state selection. The CPU latency QoS limit, read with cpu_latency_qos_limit(), is system-wide. Drivers set it with cpu_latency_qos_add_request() and cpu_latency_qos_update_request(); the e1000e network driver and several SoC drivers for serial, MMC, and USB controllers do so. User space sets it through /dev/cpu_dma_latency: opening the device adds a request, writing a value in microseconds updates it, and the request lasts until the file is closed. The write must be either exactly four bytes, taken as a binary 32-bit integer, or a string, which is parsed as hexadecimal, so echo 10 > /dev/cpu_dma_latency asks for 16 µs, and the request is dropped as soon as the shell closes the file anyway. A tool must keep the file open for as long as it needs the limit. With no requests the limit is PM_QOS_CPU_LATENCY_DEFAULT_VALUE, 2000 seconds, which no idle state comes near. Any change in the effective limit calls wake_up_all_idle_cpus(), so that CPUs already sleeping too deeply wake up and choose again.

The per-CPU resume latency limit is a device PM QoS value on each CPU's device, set through /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuN/power/pm_qos_resume_latency_us. It lets one CPU, such as one running a real-time task, be kept shallow while the others sleep deeply. Its encoding is the opposite of what one might expect: writing 0 removes the constraint, and the string n/a stores an actual zero, which permits no exit latency at all; reading the file returns the same two encodings. The CPU system wakeup latency limit, added in 6.19 with the /dev/cpu_wakeup_latency device (CONFIG_PM_QOS_CPU_SYSTEM_WAKEUP), bounds how long the system may take to wake from s2idle. It uses the same file format as /dev/cpu_dma_latency but has no in-kernel request API; only user space sets it. It is the only limit applied to the s2idle path above, which ignores the other two, and the genpd governor checks it for CPU cluster domains. Since 6.19 it also bounds ordinary idle, so that a state too slow for the system wakeup limit is not picked at run time either.

cpuidle_governor_latency_req() in drivers/cpuidle/governor.c takes the smallest of the three and converts it to nanoseconds, and the governors exclude every state whose exit latency exceeds it. A limit of zero allows only state 0, the polling state, which is how latency-sensitive applications such as low-latency trading or real-time audio keep CPUs out of deep idle without disabling cpuidle. Other PM QoS classes reuse the same code. The frequency QoS requests that cpufreq combines into a policy's limits (§7.1) are freq_qos_add_request() requests, and device PM QoS also carries the resume latency limits that genpd's governor checks before powering a domain off (§6.4).

The Energy Model and Thermal Management

The mechanisms of §7 and §8 choose frequencies and idle states one CPU at a time. Two further subsystems add a wider view. The Energy Model records how much power each performance level of a CPU or device costs, so that the scheduler can place tasks where they cost the least energy. The thermal framework watches temperatures and throttles devices before the silicon overheats, using the same frequency limits and idle mechanisms in the opposite direction. The powercap framework lets user space set power budgets directly.

The Energy Model

The Energy Model (kernel/power/energy_model.c, since 5.0) describes each performance domain, a set of CPUs or a device whose performance is scaled together, usually matching one cpufreq policy. A domain is a struct em_perf_domain holding a table of struct em_perf_state entries in ascending order, one per frequency. 

/* include/linux/energy_model.h (abridged) */
struct em_perf_state {
	unsigned long performance;
	unsigned long frequency;
	unsigned long power;
	unsigned long cost;
	unsigned long flags;
};

struct em_perf_table {
	struct rcu_head rcu;
	struct kref kref;
	struct em_perf_state state[];
};

struct em_perf_domain {
	struct em_perf_table __rcu *em_table;
	struct list_head node;
	int id;
	int nr_perf_states;
	int min_perf_state;
	int max_perf_state;
	unsigned long flags;
	unsigned long cpus[];
};

frequency is in kHz, performance is the CPU capacity at that frequency on the scheduler's 0–1024 scale, and power is the power drawn by one CPU or by the whole device, static and dynamic together. The domain flag EM_PERF_DOMAIN_MICROWATTS says the values are real microwatts; a model built from abstract units leaves it clear, which is acceptable to the scheduler, since it only compares costs, but not to a thermal governor that needs real watts. The core rejects any power value above EM_MAX_POWER (64 W, so that power times utilization cannot overflow 32-bit arithmetic), and a CPU domain may hold at most EM_MAX_NUM_CPUS CPUs (4096 on 64-bit kernels).

The derived fields are filled in at registration. em_init_performance() sets performance to the CPU's maximum capacity scaled by frequency over the highest frequency, and em_compute_costs() then sets cost to 10 * power / performance: the power paid for one unit of capacity, multiplied by ten to keep precision in integer arithmetic. (The kernel-doc comment in the header still gives the older 10 * power * max_frequency / frequency, which leaves out the CPU's capacity.) Costs are computed only for CPU domains, since only the scheduler uses them. The same loop walks the table from the top and flags a state EM_PERF_STATE_INEFFICIENT when its cost is no lower than that of a higher frequency: running faster would finish the same work for no more energy. For CPU domains, em_cpufreq_update_efficiencies() copies these flags into the cpufreq table as CPUFREQ_INEFFICIENT_FREQ, so that cpufreq's CPUFREQ_RELATION_E lookups (§7.1) step over them, and sets EM_PERF_DOMAIN_SKIP_INEFFICIENCIES on the domain.

The scheduler's question is answered by em_cpu_energy(pd, max_util, sum_util, allowed_cpu_cap). It first clamps max_util, the busiest CPU's demand, to allowed_cpu_cap, the capacity still allowed after thermal and user limits. em_pd_get_efficient_state() then picks the lowest state between min_perf_state and max_perf_state whose performance covers that demand, skipping inefficient states when the domain says so, which is the frequency schedutil would choose. The estimate is that state's cost times sum_util, the summed utilization of the domain's CPUs. Idle-state power is ignored on the assumption that it is similar whichever CPU a task goes to. The scheduler side of this calculation, and how energy-aware scheduling uses it to compare candidate CPUs, is covered in Chapter 7, §10.3. Energy-aware scheduling is enabled only when a root domain has asymmetric CPU capacities and no active SMT, an Energy Model for every domain, frequency-invariant load tracking, and schedutil as the cpufreq governor (cpufreq_ready_for_eas() checks the last), and when the kernel.sched_energy_aware sysctl is 1, which is its default.

Drivers register the model, usually from the cpufreq driver's ->register_em() callback, with em_dev_register_perf_domain(). They provide either the power values directly or an active_power() callback that the core calls for each frequency. On ARM64, cpufreq-dt uses cpufreq_register_em_with_opp(), which calls dev_pm_opp_of_register_em() in drivers/opp/of.c. That function estimates power from the device-tree dynamic-power-coefficient as coefficient × V2 × f, the CV2f law of §1.1 with the capacitance folded into one constant. scmi-cpufreq and cppc_cpufreq register the power values that firmware reports. Devices other than CPUs, such as GPUs driven by devfreq, register the same way and are used by the thermal code of §9.2 and §9.3 rather than by the scheduler; CONFIG_ENERGY_MODEL accordingly depends on either CPU_FREQ or PM_DEVFREQ.

A domain registered with a get_cost() callback instead of real power values is flagged EM_PERF_DOMAIN_ARTIFICIAL. Its costs only rank the states, so the thermal code refuses to treat it as a power source and later capacity updates leave it alone. Since 6.16, intel_pstate uses such a model on hybrid processors with HWP and no SMT (hybrid_init_cpu_capacity_scaling() in drivers/cpufreq/intel_pstate.c). It registers a one-CPU domain per CPU with four artificial states standing for 40, 60, 80, and 100 percent of its capacity and no power figures at all. hybrid_get_cost() makes each state's cost a small number that grows with the state, plus a per-core-type bias: nothing for the low-power E-cores that sit outside the L3 cache, one for the other E-cores, and two for the P-cores. The intended effect is that the scheduler fills the low-power cores first, spills onto the E-cores when those are well utilized, and wakes the P-cores last, so that energy-aware scheduling can run on x86 laptops with E-cores and P-cores.

The table is no longer fixed once registered. Since 6.9 it sits behind an RCU-protected pointer, and em_dev_update_perf_domain() can install a new table, for example when chip binning or temperature changes a domain's real power figures; em_dev_update_chip_binning(), used by the Samsung Exynos voltage-binning driver, recomputes the power of every state from the voltages that the OPP library reports for that particular chip. Readers in the scheduler simply dereference the current table under RCU, and an old table is freed once its reference count drops. em_update_performance_limits() (6.13) narrows the min_perf_state..max_perf_state window that em_cpu_energy() searches, for platforms whose firmware caps the usable frequency range. If the architecture's view of CPU capacity changes after the model was registered, which happens when cpufreq policies come up late or when a hybrid processor's capacities are rescaled, em_check_capacity_update() recomputes performance and cost for every domain, retrying once a second until all policies exist; since 6.16 drivers can call em_adjust_cpu_capacity() to do the same for one CPU. Because the set of performance domains is attached to the scheduler's root domains, em_rebuild_sched_domains() rebuilds them whenever EAS may have become possible or impossible: when schedutil is started or stopped on a policy (§7.2), or when intel_pstate registers a domain for a CPU that came online late.

Since 6.19 the Energy Model is also visible to user space over generic netlink (kernel/power/em_netlink.c, built when CONFIG_NET is set), as the dev-energymodel family described in Documentation/netlink/specs/dev-energymodel.yaml. get-perf-domains dumps every domain with its unique id (also new in 6.19), flags, and CPU mask, get-perf-table returns the states of one domain, and the event multicast group carries perf-domain-created, perf-domain-updated, and perf-domain-deleted notifications, so that a user-space power manager can follow runtime table changes. For debugging, each domain also appears as a directory under /sys/kernel/debug/energy_model/ with cpus, flags, and id files and one ps:freq subdirectory per state holding its frequency, power, cost, performance, and inefficiency flag.

Thermal Zones, Trips, and Cooling Devices

The thermal framework (drivers/thermal/) is built from three objects. A thermal zone (struct thermal_zone_device) is a temperature sensor and the part of the system it represents, such as a CPU cluster, a GPU, or the skin of a phone. Each zone has trip points, temperatures at which something should happen, of four types from enum thermal_trip_type: THERMAL_TRIP_ACTIVE (turn on a fan), THERMAL_TRIP_PASSIVE (slow devices down), THERMAL_TRIP_HOT (notify the platform), and THERMAL_TRIP_CRITICAL (shut down). A cooling device (struct thermal_cooling_device) is anything that can reduce heat, with integer states from 0 (no cooling) to max_state, set through ->set_cur_state(). Trips are bound to cooling devices with a range of allowed states, and every zone has a governor that decides what state each bound device should be in. Figure 21-8 shows the loop these objects form.

A sensor driver creates a zone with thermal_zone_device_register_with_trips(), passing an array of struct thermal_trip (temperature and hysteresis in millidegrees Celsius, type, and flags), a struct thermal_zone_device_ops, and two polling intervals: one used normally and a shorter one used while a passive trip is in effect. A zero interval means the sensor raises interrupts and the core never polls. The only mandatory operation is ->get_temp(). Optional ones program hardware thresholds (->set_trips()), report a trend (->get_trend()), accept a new trip temperature (->set_trip_temp()), or override the ->hot() and ->critical() actions. Since 6.6, thermal_tripless_zone_device_register() registers a sensor that only reports temperature. A cooling device is registered with thermal_cooling_device_register() or its device-tree and devm_ variants, and implements ->get_max_state(), ->get_cur_state(), and ->set_cur_state(). Binding is decided by the zone: since 6.12 the core calls the zone's ->should_bind() for every pair of trip and cooling device, and the callback fills a struct cooling_spec with the lowest and highest states the trip may use and a weight for the governors that share power. The pairing becomes a struct thermal_instance. A cooling device bound to several trips or zones has one instance per binding, and __thermal_cdev_update() applies the highest state any of them asks for, so the hottest zone always wins.

Zones come from the platform description. On ARM64, the thermal-zones device-tree node lists each zone's sensor, its polling-delay and polling-delay-passive, its trips, and a cooling-maps section that binds trips to cooling devices; drivers/thermal/thermal_of.c parses it and supplies the ->should_bind() callback that matches the maps. Sensors include SoC drivers in drivers/thermal/ and, on SCMI systems, firmware sensors registered as zones by drivers/hwmon/scmi-hwmon.c. On x86_64, ACPI thermal zones (drivers/acpi/thermal.c) evaluate _TMP for the temperature and _PSV, _HOT, _CRT, and _ACx for passive, hot, critical, and active trips. _TZP gives the polling period, firmware raises Notify 0x80 or 0x81 when the temperature or the trips change, and the devices to throttle come from the _PSL list for passive cooling and _ALx lists for each active trip. The ACPI zone's ->get_trend() applies the specification's passive-cooling formula with the _TC1, _TC2, and _TSP constants. drivers/thermal/intel/x86_pkg_temp_thermal.c exposes each package's digital sensor with two writable passive trips that it programs into MSR_IA32_PACKAGE_THERM_INTERRUPT, so that a user-space daemon such as thermald is told when the package crosses them.

All evaluation funnels into __thermal_zone_device_update() in drivers/thermal/thermal_core.c, called from the sensor's interrupt handler through thermal_zone_device_update() or from the zone's polling work.

Since 6.13, thermal_zone_handle_trips() keeps the trips on two sorted lists, those the temperature has reached and those still above it, so that only the trips actually crossed since the last reading need attention. A trip counts as reached at its temperature and as left only when the temperature falls below the trip minus its hysteresis, which stops a zone sitting at a threshold from flapping. Each crossing goes to thermal_trip_crossed(), which keeps a count of passive trips in effect, sends a netlink notification, and passes the event to the governor's ->trip_crossed(). Hot and critical trips skip the governor and call the zone's ->hot() or ->critical() operation. The default thermal_zone_device_critical() starts an orderly power-off through __hw_protection_trigger() (§2.4), with a forced emergency power-off after CONFIG_THERMAL_EMERGENCY_POWEROFF_DELAY_MS if the orderly one stalls (the option defaults to 0, which disables the forced fallback). Whether that action is a shutdown or a reboot follows the hw_protection= setting of §2.4; since 6.15 a device-tree zone can fix it with a critical-action property of "shutdown" or "reboot", the latter useful on boards that cannot power themselves back on. thermal_zone_set_trips() then programs the sensor, where it supports it, to interrupt at the nearest thresholds below and above the current temperature, and monitor_thermal_zone() rearms polling, using the passive polling delay while any passive trip is in effect. User-space thresholds, added in 6.13 through drivers/thermal/thermal_thresholds.c, only generate netlink events.

User space sees the framework in three places. Every zone and cooling device appears under /sys/class/thermal/ as thermal_zoneN and cooling_deviceN. A zone directory has type, temp, mode (enabled or disabled), policy and available_policies for the governor, trip_point_N_type, _temp, and _hyst for each trip, and a cdevN link, cdevN_trip_point, and cdevN_weight for each binding. Trip temperatures and hysteresis are writable only when the trip carries THERMAL_TRIP_FLAG_RW_TEMP or THERMAL_TRIP_FLAG_RW_HYST (6.9), which thermal_of.c sets on every device-tree trip; with CONFIG_THERMAL_EMULATION, writing emul_temp feeds the zone a fake temperature for testing. A cooling device directory has type, max_state, and a writable cur_state, and with CONFIG_THERMAL_STATISTICS a stats/ directory counting state transitions and time spent in each state. Second, the thermal generic netlink family (drivers/thermal/thermal_netlink.c) is the preferred event channel, superseding the uevents of the user_space governor (§9.3). Its sampling multicast group carries every temperature reading while someone listens, and its event group reports zones created, deleted, enabled, or disabled, trips crossed upward or downward or changed, cooling-device state changes, governor changes, and user threshold crossings. Commands query zone temperatures, trips, governors, and cooling devices, and since 6.13 add, delete, or flush thresholds, which requires CAP_SYS_ADMIN. A threshold carries a direction flag, up or down, and the core folds the thresholds into the interrupt window it programs with ->set_trips(), so a daemon can be woken at any temperature without polling. The same family carries the Intel Hardware Feedback Interface (drivers/thermal/intel/intel_hfi.c, 5.18): when the processor revises the performance and energy-efficiency capability it reports for each CPU, for instance because one core is hot, the driver forwards the new values as CPU_CAPABILITY_CHANGE events, batched at most every 100 ms. Third, with CONFIG_THERMAL_DEBUGFS (6.8), /sys/kernel/debug/thermal/ keeps a transition table per cooling device and, per zone, a record of every mitigation episode: when it started, how long it lasted, its peak temperature, and for each trip crossed the time spent beyond it and the average and minimum temperature meanwhile. Since 6.12, CONFIG_THERMAL_CORE_TESTING also lets a test create synthetic zones from debugfs and drive their temperature.

The CPU cooling devices connect thermal back to §7 and §8. The cpufreq cooling device (drivers/thermal/cpufreq_cooling.c) holds a frequency QoS maximum request on its policy, and cpufreq_set_cur_state() maps a cooling state to a frequency and calls freq_qos_update_request(), so a thermal cap is just one more limit among those of §7.1. When the policy's maximum falls, cpufreq_update_pressure() (6.10) records the capacity lost, and the scheduler's get_actual_cpu_capacity() subtracts the larger of that and the hardware-reported pressure from arch_update_hw_pressure(), so that load balancing sees the throttled CPUs as smaller (Chapter 7, §10.2). Its states are the policy's frequencies counted down from the highest, so state 0 means no cap. For the power allocator of §9.3, it estimates each CPU's power from the Energy Model scaled by the CPU's utilization from sched_cpu_util().

The cpuidle cooling device (drivers/thermal/cpuidle_cooling.c, since 5.6) instead forces idle periods with the idle-injection framework of drivers/powercap/idle_inject.c (4.19). Its states run from 0 to 100 and are the percentage of time to spend idle: each cycle injects a fixed idle period, by default one tick or the duration-us of the CPU's thermal-idle device-tree node, and the running time between injections is computed from the percentage, so that state 50 alternates equal run and idle periods. The framework creates one kthread per CPU through the smpboot infrastructure, so that they follow CPU hotplug. An hrtimer wakes the kthreads of all the CPUs an injection device covers together at the start of each cycle, so that their idle periods line up, and each calls play_idle_precise(), which sets the forced latency limit seen in §8.1 so that the CPU goes as deep as it can within the exit-latency-us allowed. Idle injection lowers temperature without changing the voltage, which helps on platforms whose lowest operating point is still too hot. The x86 intel_powerclamp driver (3.9) uses the same framework, since 6.3, to force package C-states. Its target idle ratio is set through its own cooling device, and module parameters set the idle duration per injection (6 to 25 ms), the CPUs to inject on, and the maximum ratio.

Other cooling devices follow the same three-operation interface. drivers/thermal/devfreq_cooling.c caps devfreq devices such as GPUs through a device PM QoS maximum and, with an Energy Model, acts as a power actor using the device's busy time. On ACPI systems, drivers/acpi/processor_thermal.c registers a Processor cooling device per CPU whose first states lower the maximum frequency by 20 percent each through a frequency QoS request and whose remaining states are the processor's ACPI throttling (T-) states, and drivers/acpi/fan_core.c registers Fan devices whose states are the fan's speed levels from _FPS, or simply off and on. Since 6.13, PCIe bandwidth control (drivers/pci/pcie/bwctrl.c) registers a PCIe_Port_Link_Speed_ cooling device for each port that can change its link speed (drivers/thermal/pcie_cooling.c): state 0 is the port's top speed and each further state drops one PCIe generation, down to 2.5 GT/s, cutting the power of the link's transceivers.

Thermal Governors

The governor turns a temperature into cooling states. A governor is a struct thermal_governor whose main hooks are ->trip_crossed(), called from thermal_trip_crossed() when the temperature crosses a trip in either direction; ->manage(), called at the end of every zone update; and ->update_tz(), called when cooling devices are bound or unbound. ->bind_to_tz() and ->unbind_from_tz() let a governor allocate per-zone state when it is attached to a zone. Table 21-14 lists the five in drivers/thermal/, each in its own gov_*.c file and Kconfig option. The default is chosen at build time with the THERMAL_DEFAULT_GOV_* choice, normally step_wise; a zone can name another in its registration parameters, and user space can switch any zone by writing its policy attribute in sysfs.

The power allocator, also called Intelligent Power Allocation, works in power rather than states. Each zone has a sustainable_power, the heat it can dissipate indefinitely at its control temperature, and the PID controller adjusts the budget around that figure according to how far the temperature is from the target. The cooling devices must be power actors, implementing ->get_requested_power(), ->state2power(), and ->power2state(). The cpufreq cooling device implements them by reading the Energy Model of §9.1 and scaling by each CPU's load, which is why accurate power numbers in the device tree or firmware matter for thermal behaviour as well as for scheduling.

The governor (drivers/thermal/gov_power_allocator.c) takes two trips from the zone. The first passive trip is the switch-on temperature: below it the controller is reset and every actor is allowed its maximum power. The last passive trip is the control temperature the loop aims for. On every evaluation, pid_controller() computes the error, control temperature minus current temperature, in fixed-point arithmetic with 10 fractional bits, and sets the budget to sustainable_power plus three corrections. The proportional term uses k_po when the zone is above the target and the larger k_pu when it is below, so the budget falls cautiously and recovers quickly. The integral term accumulates k_i times the error only while the error is below integral_cutoff, so that a long cool spell cannot wind it up. The derivative term, k_d times the change in error, is divided by the passive polling interval. The result is clamped between zero and the actors' combined maximum. Any constant the platform omits is estimated: sustainable_power as the sum of each actor's power in its most-throttled allowed state, k_po as that power divided by the gap between the switch-on and control temperatures, k_pu as twice k_po, k_i as a tenth of k_pu, and k_d and integral_cutoff as zero. All of them can be tuned at run time through the zone's sustainable_power, k_po, k_pu, k_i, k_d, and integral_cutoff files in sysfs.

divvy_up_power() then shares the budget. Each actor's request is multiplied by its binding weight, and each receives the budget in proportion to its weighted share of the total. An actor granted more than its maximum is capped, and the excess is handed to actors that still have headroom, in proportion to that headroom. When no actor requests any power, every actor is granted its maximum (6.13), rather than being throttled for no reason. Each grant goes through ->power2state() and becomes the instance's target state, and the thermal_power_allocator and thermal_power_allocator_pid tracepoints record every step, which is how the constants are tuned in practice.

Powercap, RAPL, and DTPM

Thermal management reacts to temperature. The powercap framework (drivers/powercap/powercap_sys.c) instead lets user space set power limits directly. It exposes control types, each with a tree of power zones under /sys/class/powercap/. A zone is named after its control type and position, such as intel-rapl:0 for a package and intel-rapl:0:0 for a domain inside it, and has a name, an enabled switch, and either an energy_uj counter or a power_uw reading with their max_ ranges. Each of its constraints, numbered constraint_N_, has a name, a power_limit_uw to hold on average over time_window_us, and optional minimum and maximum bounds. The driver supplies struct powercap_zone_ops and struct powercap_zone_constraint_ops, and the framework only generates the sysfs files and passes reads and writes through. Since 5.10, energy_uj is readable only by root: the Platypus attack showed that sampling a package's energy counter at high rate from an unprivileged process can leak information about the instructions and data being processed on other cores.

On x86_64 the main control type is intel-rapl, from Running Average Power Limit. drivers/powercap/intel_rapl_common.c provides the zone logic (the driver was added in 3.13 and split into a common part and back ends in 5.3). intel_rapl_msr.c reaches the registers through MSRs such as MSR_PKG_POWER_LIMIT and MSR_PKG_ENERGY_STATUS, intel_rapl_tpmi.c through the TPMI interface of newer server parts, and the int340x thermal driver adds an intel-rapl-mmio type. A package zone may contain core, uncore (the integrated GPU), and dram domains, and some client parts add a platform-wide psys zone. Package constraints are named long_term (PL1, the sustained limit, typically the processor's rated TDP), short_term (PL2, a higher limit allowed for a short window of turbo), and on processors that support it peak_power (PL4, an instantaneous current ceiling; recent additions are Panther Lake in 6.17 and Ice Lake in 7.0). The processor itself enforces the limits by adjusting frequency, and the kernel refuses writes to a limit that firmware has locked. On AMD and Hygon processors the MSR back end exposes the energy counters, which tools such as perf and turbostat read to measure package and core energy. The power perf PMU of §1.4 normally comes from arch/x86/events/rapl.c; since 6.10 for TPMI servers, and since 6.19 for Panther Lake and Wildcat Lake through the MSR back end, the powercap RAPL code registers it instead. On Intel laptops, thermald is the usual user-space consumer: it lowers the RAPL limits as the skin or package temperature rises, ahead of the kernel's own passive trips.

On ARM64, the Dynamic Thermal Power Management framework (drivers/powercap/dtpm.c, since 5.12) builds a dtpm control type from a platform-described hierarchy with dtpm_create_hierarchy(); the only in-tree description today is the Rockchip RK3399 table in drivers/soc/rockchip/dtpm.c, with a package node above the CPUs and the GPU. Its CPU and devfreq leaves (dtpm_cpu.c, dtpm_devfreq.c) translate a power limit into a frequency cap using the Energy Model, applied through a frequency QoS request for CPUs and a device PM QoS request for devfreq devices, and parent nodes divide their budget among children. SCMI platforms instead expose firmware-enforced limits through the arm-scmi control type of drivers/powercap/arm_scmi_powercap.c (6.2), which maps each SCMI powercap domain to a zone and forwards limits to the firmware, which applies them by its own means. In both cases the kernel's role is the same as in the rest of this chapter: to present one model of the hardware and let policy be set from above while the mechanism sits as close to the hardware as possible.

Summary

This chapter described how Linux 7.0 saves energy at every scale, from the whole machine down to one CPU's next idle period. Power management is a kernel problem because only the kernel knows at once what the hardware can do, what every device and CPU is doing, and what latency each workload can tolerate. The dynamic CV2f power of a busy circuit is cut by lowering voltage and frequency, leakage only by removing power, and every saving must be paid for in wakeup latency. The firmware interfaces differ between the two architectures, ACPI on x86_64 and PSCI, SCMI, and the device tree on ARM64, but the kernel layers above them are shared, and RAPL energy counters, turbostat, and residency counters such as S0ix time show whether they work.

System sleep is driven from /sys/power/state, with /sys/power/mem_sleep choosing what “mem” means. pm_suspend() freezes user space and freezable kernel threads, optionally freezes filesystems, and takes every device through the dev_pm_ops phases from prepare to suspend_noirq, in an order fixed by the device lists and device links and with asynchronous devices in parallel. Suspend-to-idle then idles all CPUs, with the ACPI LPS0 hooks on modern standby laptops; the deep state takes the non-boot CPUs offline, runs the syscore callbacks, and hands the last CPU to the platform, ACPI S3 on x86 or PSCI SYSTEM_SUSPEND on ARM64. Wakeup sources and /sys/power/wakeup_count close the race between deciding to sleep and an event arriving. Hibernation reuses the same phases to quiesce the system, builds a snapshot of every page in use with memory bitmaps, writes it compressed to swap, and restores it from a freshly booted kernel. Power-off and reboot share the device walk but not the way back: device_shutdown() calls each device's ->shutdown(), the sys-off handler chain picks the firmware or PMIC method that cuts power, and hw_protection shutdowns use the same path when hardware is in danger. When a suspend fails, pm_test, the suspend tracepoints with sleepgraph, pm_debug_messages, and the DPM watchdog show where it went wrong.

While the system runs, runtime PM suspends individual devices when their usage count falls to zero and their children are suspended, after an optional autosuspend delay. Device links extend the reference counting to suppliers, wake IRQs let a suspended device signal activity, and pm_runtime_force_suspend() lets a driver reuse its runtime callbacks for system sleep. PM domains act on groups: on x86 the ACPI PM domain moves a device between D-states, and on device-tree platforms generic PM domains power off a whole domain once every device in it is suspended and its governor agrees, nest into hierarchies, carry performance states, and model the CPU clusters that cpuidle powers down.

For CPUs, cpufreq groups CPUs into policies, lets a governor, normally schedutil driven by the scheduler's utilization, choose a frequency within limits combined by frequency QoS, and uses a scaling driver to apply it: intel_pstate with HWP and its energy performance preference, amd-pstate and cppc_cpufreq through CPPC, or scmi-cpufreq and cpufreq-dt with OPP tables. Frequency invariance keeps the scheduler's utilization comparable across frequencies, and boost frequencies sit above the nominal maximum. cpuidle runs from the idle loop: a governor, menu or teo, predicts the idle time and picks the deepest state whose target residency fits and whose exit latency satisfies PM QoS, and a driver such as intel_idle or cpuidle-psci enters it. PM QoS bounds both from /dev/cpu_dma_latency, per-device resume latency limits, and since 6.19 a system wakeup latency limit.

The Energy Model gives each performance state a power and a cost, which energy-aware scheduling uses to place tasks on asymmetric CPUs when schedutil runs and which the power allocator uses to share a power budget. Thermal zones compare sensor readings with trip points, and governors such as step_wise and power_allocator respond by raising cooling device states, which cap frequencies through frequency QoS or inject idle time; netlink events and thresholds keep user space daemons informed, and a critical trip shuts the machine down. Powercap lets user space set power budgets directly: RAPL limits enforced by the processor on x86_64, and DTPM or SCMI powercap on ARM64. Table 21-15 summarizes the mechanisms.
